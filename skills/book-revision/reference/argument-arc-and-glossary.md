## Part 1: The Argument Arc

The book should advance a single chain of propositions. Every chapter should visibly advance one or more of them. Anything that does not should be shortened, moved to an endnote, or removed.

### The chain of claims

1. **Students routinely make varied bids to act as knowers** — through partial explanations, unconventional representations, questions, reframings, self-directed inquiry, and even resistance. These bids are often organized and intelligent even when they are incorrect.

2. **Schools are organized to recognize legible completion more readily than live thinking.** Pacing, accountability, assessment, and walkthrough systems reward quickly visible, countable evidence of completion. This is not a failure of individual teachers but a structural feature of how institutions are designed.

3. **This produces systematic misrecognition, not isolated teacher error.** The taxonomy names recurring configurations of how students stand as knowers: effortful, engaged, epistemic, democratic, and authorial — plus the shadow forms of pseudo-agency and dysfunctional agency that appear when standing is chronically unseated.

4. **Misrecognition damages students' standing as knowers.** When thinking is repeatedly treated as noise, misconception, or noncompliance, students learn to abandon their own perceptions and start guessing what the teacher wants. This is where pseudo-agency is manufactured — not in one class period but over years.

5. **The equity mechanism: standing as a knower is unevenly distributed.** Inequity lies partly in the uneven distribution of apprenticeships into standing as a knower — background experience, adult dialogue, intellectual mentorship, institutional fluency, trust, and time. This is not a deficit in students or families but an unequal distribution of the relationships through which the capacity is built. Race, class, disability, language, and neighborhood are not competing "engines" but interlocking conditions through which this mechanism operates.

6. **Answerable teaching is the classroom-level response.** It joins three things: dialogism (the ethic of receiving student thinking), Variation Theory (the perceptual design that creates a shared object worth thinking about), and intellectual apprenticeship (the sustained practice in which the two are joined). Direct instruction and dialogism are not opposites; concepts and vocabulary are the material students think with.

7. **The capacity to act as a knower develops in phases and through bridges.** Students move through lurking, messing around, and geeking out. Some need adjacent agency — consequential participation in non-academic roles — before academic risk is available. A room organized for visibility and answerability can cultivate more forms of agency for more students.

8. **AI matters only insofar as it can offload production labor and return human presence to the teacher.** Used as output-scoring, it intensifies the problem. The pedagogy underneath the tool is the whole difference. AI can create the conditions for dialogic teaching without itself being dialogic teaching, because dialogic teaching requires not knowing, for a moment, what the right answer to a student's contribution is.

9. **Classroom craft hits institutional ceilings.** Without genuine student participation in governance and legitimate pathways for student-authored learning, even excellent teaching works against a structural current. Marcus, Zara, and Eli show what those ceilings look like.

10. **Agency does not develop when one ethic wins.** It develops when the tension between centripetal forces (structure, shared norms, curriculum) and centrifugal forces (student voice, interpretive difference, authorship) is held — and held for the students who were never apprenticed into standing inside it.

11. **Teachers need not only to notice but to have moves.** Specific, teachable responses to each form of agency a student expresses. The moves index is the beginning of a practice that runs at two speeds: description for the unprecedented, and a case bank for the recurrent.

12. **What all of this adds up to: an apprenticeship in intellectual authorship.** The old structure for building the capacity to treat one's own thinking as real and consequential, staged deliberately for students who were never apprenticed into it anywhere else.

## Part 2: Concepts That Must Stay Airtight

Not every term in the book is equally central. The terms below are the load-bearing concepts. Each definition includes a "not this" boundary to prevent slippage.

### The meta-concept

**Standing as a knower** — the relational position a student holds when their own thinking about a shared object is treated as real, answerable, and consequential: something that can be taken up, tested, revised, and allowed to affect what happens next. This is the book's true subject. It is relational (it exists between student, object, teacher, and community), developmental (it grows through apprenticeship), and institutional (it depends on what the system is built to recognize).

- *Not this:* Standing as a knower is not a personality trait, not a fixed identity, not a label for a kind of student. It is a position that can be gained, lost, simulated, or denied — sometimes within a single classroom moment. The instruction from Chapter 2 ("Read the names as moments, not labels") applies here as the book's foundational methodological safeguard.

### Core concepts

**Agency** — the student's developing capacity to act meaningfully and effectively in relation to an object of knowing, between learner, task, teacher, and community. Agency is one dimension of standing as a knower; it is the capacity side, not the whole phenomenon.

- *Not this:* Agency is not motivation (which describes drive), not autonomy (which describes freedom from constraint), not participation (which describes entering discourse), and not choice (which is one affordance). Agency requires that what the student does can redirect inquiry, reframe a problem, or alter the shared trajectory of sense-making.

**Origination** — the student's contribution of something that comes from their own thinking, not from the teacher, the text, the worksheet, or the algorithm. Origination is not correctness; it is the act of producing a thought that is one's own.

- *Not this:* Origination is not mere choice (choosing a topic from a list is selection, not origination). Origination is not self-expression in the absence of a disciplinary object. It is a contribution that enters a shared intellectual space.

**Answerability** — the student's responsibility to test, revise, and stand behind their thinking in relation to the discipline, the evidence, and the community. Also: the teacher's reciprocal responsibility to take up the student's thinking rather than merely sort it into correct/incorrect. (Bakhtin's *otvetstvennost*: both meanings at once.)

- *Not this:* Answerability is not compliance (complying with a directive requires no origination). Answerability is not accountability in the institutional sense (test scores, pacing, coverage). It is the ethical relation in which both student and teacher are responsible to the thinking.

**Recognition** — the act of treating a student's contribution as a truthful beginning — data about how the phenomenon is showing up for this learner — rather than as noise, misconception, or noncompliance. Recognition is the mechanism by which standing as a knower is confirmed or denied.

- *Not this:* Recognition is not approval (a contribution can be recognized and then shown to be wrong). Recognition is not acceptance (it does not mean treating all claims as equally valid). Recognition is not praise (praising an idea and abandoning it is not recognition — the idea must be taken up). Recognition means the contribution enters the inquiry; what happens next is affected by it.

**Legibility** — what an institution can see, count, and report. Schools privilege legible outputs (grades, test scores, completed work, visible participation) and are structurally poor at recognizing forms of thinking that don't produce legible outputs.

- *Not this:* Legibility is not a conspiracy against students. It is the structural logic of institutions operating under accountability pressure. The problem is not that legibility is evil but that it is incomplete — it mistakes evidence of task completion for evidence of thought.

**Sense-making** — the learner's effort to orient within a phenomenon: "What is going on here?" Perceptual and relational, working with what can be seen, noticed, and half-recognized. Often messy, incomplete, and surprisingly intelligent. Essential for engaged agency.

- *Not this:* Sense-making is not guessing (it has internal logic even when it is wrong). Sense-making is not the same as meaning-making (it can occur without the student authoring anything of their own).

**Meaning-making** — the learner's effort to metabolize an idea into their own thinking: testing it, reframing it, taking a position toward it, while remaining answerable to disciplinary norms. This is where epistemic agency begins to show itself.

- *Not this:* Meaning-making is not personal opinion (it remains answerable to the discipline). Meaning-making is not repeating what the teacher said. It is the student's own interpretive work on the object, which can change what the object means for the room.

**Shared object of attention** — the common problem, text, phenomenon, or task that teacher and student can both respond to. Dialogism requires a shared object; you cannot have real dialogue about something no one can yet see.

- *Not this:* The shared object is not the curriculum (the curriculum is what the institution assigns; the shared object is what becomes visible enough to think about). The shared object is not the worksheet (a worksheet is a task; the shared object is the phenomenon the task points at).

**Dialogism** — an ethic of attention to the learner's current reality as a knower. Not dialogue (talk between people) but the condition under which understanding becomes possible: meaning arises between voices through response, contestation, and re-voicing. A student's contribution is a bid that enters a shared intellectual space where it can be examined, tested, and revised. The student is accountable to the discipline; the discipline, in a dialogic classroom, is accountable to the student's way of seeing.

- *Not this:* Dialogism is not discussion (discussion can be monologic if the teacher determines every question and acceptable conclusion). Dialogism is not "all opinions are equally true" (all voices are answerable, not all claims are valid). Dialogism is not open-ended talk (Bakhtin taught grammar directly; direct instruction in the service of voice is not a betrayal of dialogism but its precondition).

**Variation Theory** — the perceptual design principle that a concept has critical aspects (features a learner must discern to see the concept at all), and that you cannot discern an aspect that never varies. The lesson's job is to create variation in exactly the aspects you want students to grasp, making the shared object of attention visible enough to think about.

- *Not this:* Variation Theory is not making lessons interesting (it is about making critical aspects visible, not about engagement). Variation Theory is not a delivery mechanism (without dialogism, it improves the clarity of transmission but leaves the student's epistemic agency nowhere to go).

**Critical aspect** — a feature of a concept that a learner must discern in order to see the concept at all. You cannot discern an aspect that never varies. Once a critical aspect is on the table, teacher and student are responding to the same thing.

**Intellectual apprenticeship** — the sustained practice in which dialogism and Variation Theory are joined, where direct instruction and the student's origination stop being rivals. The teacher creates the perceptual conditions under which the student can begin to see, stays close enough to respond to what the student actually sees, and gradually hands over the regulating as the student becomes able to carry it.

- *Not this:* Intellectual apprenticeship is not tutoring (it is about standing as a knower, not about skill remediation). It is not laissez-faire (it requires structure, curated objects, and expert guidance). It is the old structure — mentorship around consequential objects of attention — applied to the capacity to think.

**Intellectual authorship** — the underlying capacity that all forms of agency express: the capacity to treat one's own thinking about a subject as real and consequential, to author meaning within it. Authorial agency (Marcus's kind) is the rare outermost form — authoring not just meaning within a subject but the path itself. Intellectual authorship is the more ordinary, teachable capacity beneath the whole taxonomy.

- *Not this:* Intellectual authorship is not the same as authorial agency (authorial agency is a configuration; intellectual authorship is the capacity). Intellectual authorship is not self-expression (it is answerable to the discipline). It is the telos of the apprenticeship — what the whole book is building toward.

**Pseudo-agency** — visible task performance without genuine contact with the object of learning. Legible performance without origination or answerability. Not low effort (the student may be working hard) but performance that substitutes for thinking.

- *Not this:* Pseudo-agency is not laziness (it often requires significant effort). It is not a student trait (it is produced by institutional conditions — pacing, accountability, premature public performance, lack of protected orientation time). It is the form agency takes when standing as a knower has been chronically unseated.

**Adjacent agency** — consequential participation in non-academic roles (responsibilities, governance, community maintenance) that gives students evidence their participation counts before academic risk is available. The developmental bridge between belonging and intellectual agency.

- *Not this:* Adjacent agency is not an eighth agency type (it operates at a different analytical level — it is a developmental bridge, not a configuration of the student-object relation). It is not democratic agency (democratic agency is shaping the conditions of shared classroom life; adjacent agency is the experience of agency through roles that may not yet be academic). It is not choice (choice is one affordance; adjacent agency is the lived experience of consequential participation).

**Authorial agency** — the capacity and drive to set one's own learning path entirely — to define what is worth knowing, why it matters, and how to pursue it — outside the frame school provides. The rare outermost form. Mostly cannot be cultivated from inside a classroom; it can be recognized, protected, or inadvertently crushed.

- *Not this:* Authorial agency is not engaged agency with more independence (it is different in kind, not degree). It is not self-directed learning within teacher-defined ends (that is still engaged or epistemic agency). It is the student who has already built their own curriculum.

### Contrast terms (borrowed from literature, used to mark departures)

These terms appear in the book but should be marked as belonging to other frameworks. They are useful for positioning the argument against existing literature, but they should not be treated as internal categories.

**Motivation** — describes drive. The book's argument is that the language of motivation misframes the problem: it asks "How do we motivate?" when it should ask "What is the student already doing, and how is it being read?"

**Autonomy support** — SDT's term for supporting a student's felt freedom from constraint. Valuable but insufficient: autonomy support presupposes a student willing to be seen choosing. Where trust is broken, autonomy has nothing to land on.

**Agentic engagement** — Reeve's and Patall's term for students shaping the conditions of their learning (activity, pace, format). Real and valuable, but not the same as authoring meaning. A student can negotiate every term of how an assignment is done and still not have one thought of her own about its content.

**Participation** — entering discourse. What separates participation from agency is what happens after the student speaks: does the teacher take up the question? Does the group revise its explanation? Is the direction of inquiry altered?

**Compliance** — meeting externally defined expectations. Can produce effortful agency but cannot, by itself, produce engaged, epistemic, democratic, or authorial agency.

**Choice** — selecting from available options. One affordance in a room, not the same as agency. Agency is so much more than choice.

### Practice mechanisms (important but not master concepts)

These are the practical tools the book describes. They should be treated as applications of the core concepts, not as additional theoretical categories.

**Control of variation** — a design principle for materials: the material reports state rather than issuing verdicts. A student can produce something wrong but interesting, and it persists long enough to be discussed. Contrasted with Montessori's control of error (the material has already decided what the information means).

**Two-tier grading** — a turn structure: the first tier asks whether the student thought (scored generously); feedback opens a question; the second tier is the revision, rescored. The student's second attempt answers the teacher's first response.

**Feedback pipeline** — the system (AI-drafted feedback in a specified dialogic register, read and corrected by the teacher) that makes dialogue-located correction affordable at scale. The enabling condition for answerable teaching with thirty students, not a pedagogical theory.

**Dialogic register** — the voice in which feedback is given: reading the student as a knower, quoting her exact words, naming where her reasoning was pointing at something true, never using deficit language. The pedagogy of the core chapters turned into rules for written response.

## Part 3: The Missing Meta-Level Concept — How to Stop the Proliferation

### The diagnosis

The proliferation is not caused by too many ideas. It is caused by **unmarked levels of analysis**. The book uses "agency" as its master term, but agency is being asked to do too much work. It names:

- a philosophical capacity (capabilities + decision-making + origination + answerability)
- a developmental trajectory (effortful → engaged → epistemic → democratic → authorial)
- a classroom observable (what the teacher sees)
- an institutional reception (what gets recognized)
- a distortion (pseudo-agency, dysfunctional agency)
- a social/material bridge (adjacent agency)
- an underlying capacity (intellectual authorship)

These are not all the same kind of thing. When they are all called "agency" or "forms of agency," the reader cannot tell which level of analysis is in play, and every new term appears to be competing with every other term for the same conceptual space.

### The missing concept: "Standing as a knower"

The book is not finally about "agency" as a student trait. It is about **how students gain, lose, simulate, or are denied standing as knowers** — in a field made up of student, object, teacher, peers, and institution.

This concept is already in the manuscript's language. The Preface says "the crisis commonly called student disengagement is often a crisis of recognition." Chapter 5 says students' contributions are "truthful beginnings." Chapter 8 says "what the system can't see, it can't support." The Introduction says students "make different kinds of bids to act as knowers." The phrase "act as knowers" is already doing the work; it just hasn't been named as the book's master concept.

**Definition:**

> **Standing as a knower** is the relational position a student holds when their own thinking about a shared object is treated as real, answerable, and consequential — something that can be taken up, tested, revised, and allowed to affect what happens next.

**What this concept does:**

It gives the book a single phenomenon to describe, and every other term becomes an aspect of that phenomenon rather than a competitor for the same conceptual space:

- **Agency** is the student's developing capacity to act from and answer for their own thinking — the capacity side of standing.
- **Recognition / uptake** is how others grant or deny standing.
- **Legibility / chronotope** is how institutions make some forms of standing visible and others invisible.
- **Dialogism / Variation Theory / apprenticeship** is the pedagogical architecture for developing standing.
- **Pseudo-agency / dysfunctional agency / the double bind** are breakdowns or distortions of standing.
- **Adjacent agency** is proto-standing — consequentiality in a non-academic domain that becomes the bridge to academic standing.
- **Intellectual authorship** is the mature form of standing — the capacity that the apprenticeship builds toward.

### The organizing principle

Every term in the book should be assignable to one of six analytical levels. When a term appears in the text, the reader should be able to tell which level it operates at:

| Level | What it describes | Terms that belong here |
|---|---|---|
| **Student-object relation** | What the student does with the object of knowing | Origination, sense-making, meaning-making, effortful/engaged/epistemic/democratic/authorial agency |
| **Teacher response** | How the teacher receives and responds to student thinking | Recognition, uptake, answerability, dialogism, the moves |
| **Object design** | How the object of knowing is made visible | Shared object, critical aspect, Variation Theory, control of variation |
| **Developmental process** | How standing as a knower is built over time | Lurking, messing around, geeking out, adjacent agency, intellectual apprenticeship |
| **Institutional condition** | What the system is built to see and count | Legibility, chronotope, governance, credit structures, pacing |
| **Breakdown / distortion** | What happens when standing is unseated | Pseudo-agency, dysfunctional agency, the double bind, performative participation |

This map does not replace the taxonomy. It situates the taxonomy. The seven agency configurations live in the first row (student-object relation), but they are *configurations of standing within the whole field*, not properties of the student alone. "Engaged agency" is not just something the student does — it is a configuration where the student invests effort from their own position within the teacher's frame, AND the teacher is positioned to receive it. When the teacher doesn't receive it, the same student behavior could be read as "off-task" or "incorrect." The agency form is a description of the relational field, not just the student.

### What to demote

Several terms should be explicitly marked as belonging to a different status, not to the same conceptual plane as the core terms:

- **Agentic engagement** and **autonomy support** are literature-contrast terms. Use them to mark departures from existing frameworks, not as internal categories. When they appear, the reader should know you are engaging with someone else's vocabulary, not adding to your own.

- **Adjacent agency** should be marked as a developmental bridge, not added as an eighth agency type. It operates at the developmental-process level, not the student-object level. It is how a student first experiences consequentiality before that consequentiality attaches to academic content.

- **Intellectual authorship** should be the telos — the underlying capacity the whole book builds toward — not another mode parallel to authorial agency. Authorial agency is a configuration (rare, mostly uncultivatable from inside a classroom); intellectual authorship is the capacity that all configurations express.

- **Feedback pipeline**, **two-tier grading**, and **control of variation** are practice mechanisms. They are applications of the core concepts to specific classroom problems, not master concepts. Treat them as worked examples of the architecture, not as additional theory.

- **Chronotope** should appear when the institutional level is in play, but it should not migrate into the student-object level. It describes the time-place of the setting, not the student's relation to the object.

### The sentence that compresses the book

> You are describing the development, recognition, and institutional support of students' standing as knowers. "Agency" is one dimension of that standing — the student's capacity side — not the whole phenomenon. The agency forms are recurring configurations of standing within a relational field. The distortions are what standing becomes when it is chronically unseated. The pedagogical architecture is how standing is built. The institutional critique is about what the system is built to recognize. Everything in the book is an aspect of this one phenomenon.

If every chapter can be read as advancing the understanding of standing as a knower — how it is gained, lost, simulated, denied, recognized, cultivated, or crushed — then the proliferation is not a problem. It is a taxonomy of aspects. The reader knows which aspect is in play, and the terms stop competing with each other for the same conceptual space.

### A practical test

Before finalizing any chapter, ask of every key term in it:

1. Which analytical level does this term operate at?
2. Is it describing the student, the teacher, the object, the developmental process, the institution, or a breakdown?
3. If I replaced "agency" with "standing as a knower" in this passage, would the meaning change? If yes, the passage is using "agency" to mean something more specific — and that more specific meaning should be named.

This test will reveal where the book is using "agency" as a placeholder for the whole phenomenon (where "standing as a knower" would be more precise) and where it is using "agency" to name a specific configuration (where the named form — engaged, epistemic, etc. — is doing the real work).

## Round one: Preface through Chapter 5

Yes—there is a valuable, publishable book here. Its strongest contribution is not simply a familiar claim that students need voice, choice, or engagement; it is the sharper claim that many apparent engagement problems are failures of recognition: teachers and institutions misread students' existing bids to know, particularly when those bids do not produce immediately legible, curriculum-aligned work. The proposed taxonomy gives that claim practical form.

That said, this is not yet ready to submit as a finished scholarly-trade or professional book. It has a compelling conceptual center, memorable cases, and an unusually forceful teacher-facing voice, but it needs a more disciplined theory architecture, a clearer evidentiary contract, and substantial revision to avoid overclaiming, polemical drift, and avoidable vulnerabilities with reviewers and editors.

### What is genuinely original

The manuscript's best idea is answerable teaching: a reciprocal ethical relation in which students are answerable for ideas they originate, while teachers are answerable to take up those ideas rather than merely sort them into correct/incorrect, on-task/off-task, or compliant/noncompliant. That is a substantive reframing of pedagogy, not a slogan.

Three elements make it potentially distinctive:

| Element | What the book contributes | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Recognition thesis | "Disengagement" is often misrecognized thinking, rather than a lack of motivation | Moves the diagnostic question from "How do we motivate?" to "What is the student already doing, and how is it being read?" |
| Agency taxonomy | Distinguishes effortful, engaged, epistemic, democratic, and authorial agency, plus dysfunctional and pseudo-agency as analytically different shadow forms | Gives teachers language for forms of participation that conventional frameworks collapse together |
| Institutional account | Connects classroom perception to institutional "legibility," pacing, accountability, evaluation, and school-model assumptions | Avoids blaming individual teachers while retaining a usable account of classroom action |

The distinction between engaged agency and epistemic agency is especially promising. Angie's partial but self-generated procedure remains inside the assigned mathematical question; Nancy's coin arrangement changes what "evenly" and, therefore, the mathematical task might mean. That is a lucid and teachable distinction. The book's claim is that pedagogy needs not only techniques for eliciting and correcting student reasoning but also a way to recognize when a learner has altered the frame of inquiry itself.

That is valuable because much educational writing either: treats agency as a general positive trait; treats voice, choice, and autonomy as roughly interchangeable; focuses on student participation within already-set instructional goals; or critiques schooling structurally without specifying what a teacher should notice and do in a live classroom moment.

Your book is trying to bridge those gaps. That bridge is publishable.

### The strongest material

**The cases carry the argument.** Angie, Nancy, Priya, Malik, Marcus, Heidi, and Peter are not interchangeable anecdotes. Each is positioned to reveal a different phenomenon: Angie demonstrates an engaged learner whose partial reasoning is shut down because it is incorrect rather than developed as an object of inquiry. Nancy demonstrates a possible epistemic reframe: an aesthetic-spatial reorganization of a division problem that the teacher reads as departure from mathematics. Priya gives the manuscript a vivid account of pseudo-agency: visible task performance without meaningful contact with the object of learning. Malik makes the discussion of lurking, exploratory manipulation, and later uptake especially concrete. Marcus anchors authorial agency in a form that schools frequently fail to credential or recognize. Peter makes the institutional problem tangible: a student-originated, sustained project is reduced to marginal credit because the school lacks a structure for recognizing it.

The Nancy vignette is probably the book's flagship example. It demonstrates the manuscript's central claim at the level of a single classroom interaction without requiring readers to accept the entire theoretical system first. It also has intellectual afterlife: readers can disagree about whether the flower is a mathematically productive representation while still seeing that the teacher's interpretation should have been more curious, more dialogic, and less prematurely corrective.

**The book has a strong practical promise.** The manuscript understands a crucial market and professional problem: teachers cannot use a theory that adds unmanageable interpretive labor. The repeated insistence that "noticing" is not enough—that teachers need moves after they notice—is strategically strong.

The phrase "teachers must become answerable to the forms of thinking students actually bring" is a powerful organizing line. So is the claim that this should not amount to heroic, charisma-dependent teaching. These commitments position the book against both simplistic motivational advice and idealized constructivist rhetoric.

**The school-model comparison is useful.** The traditional/progressive/democratic comparison has real explanatory potential because it does not merely rank models. It asks what each makes visible, what each hides, and how each can fail students. That is more sophisticated than conventional "traditional versus progressive" polemic.

The strongest version of that chapter will show that: traditional arrangements can provide access, coherence, and disciplinary structure while suppressing origination; progressive arrangements may honor inquiry and experience while still failing to respond to radical reframing or uneven readiness; democratic arrangements can expand authorship and shared governance while risking insufficient instructional scaffolding, hidden inequities, or uneven access to expert mediation.

If maintained with symmetry and empirical care, this section could appeal across ideological camps rather than only to readers already committed to progressive or democratic education.

### The central editorial risks

**The taxonomy needs tighter conceptual boundaries.** The seven-part map is the manuscript's core contribution, so reviewers will test it relentlessly. At present, some categories appear to work at different analytical levels: Engaged, epistemic, democratic, and authorial agency are presented as generative forms of agency. Effortful agency is called constrained, sometimes useful, and morally ambiguous. Pseudo-agency is described as an appearance or decay of agency. Dysfunctional agency is explicitly called a non-example, although it also contains forms of origination, decision-making, and capability.

This creates a taxonomic problem: are these all forms of agency, orientations to an object, developmental states, institutional receptions, or labels for observable classroom patterns? At different points, the manuscript says each of these.

You can solve this without giving up the framework. I would explicitly establish a hierarchy such as: Dimensions of agency: origination, answerability, capability, decision-making. Objects of action: assigned disciplinary object, framing of the object, shared conditions of learning, self-authored course of study. Observable configurations: effortful, engaged, epistemic, democratic, authorial. Distortions or breakdowns: pseudo-agency and forms of withdrawal/resistance that are not themselves educational agency but may preserve or redirect agency under alienating conditions.

That would make the map more defensible. It would also help readers understand whether "dysfunctional agency" should remain within the taxonomy or be discussed as an important diagnostic contrast rather than a genre of agency.

The manuscript is right to caution readers not to label children. But that ethical caution needs to be structurally built into the taxonomy rather than appended. Consider calling the units "agency configurations," "classroom bids," or "moments of agency" throughout. The current instruction—"Read the names as moments, not labels"—is excellent and should become a recurring methodological safeguard.

**"Epistemic agency" may face a naming challenge.** Your distinction is intellectually meaningful, but the term epistemic agency already has substantial use in learning sciences and science education. You acknowledge that your usage differs, especially in locating epistemic agency at the point where a learner changes the framework of discernment rather than merely participating authoritatively in disciplinary inquiry.

That distinction may be publishable, but it will be challenged. A reviewer may say: "This is not epistemic agency; it is epistemic reframing, heterodiscoursia, authorial positioning, or a dialogic reconstitution of the task."

You have three viable editorial options: Retain the term, but devote a rigorous early section to comparison with existing epistemic-agency literature and state exactly why your redefinition is necessary. Retain the term with a modifier, such as "reframing epistemic agency" or "epistemic-reframing agency." Rename the category and preserve "epistemic agency" for the broader literature, perhaps using "reframing agency," "frame-transforming agency," or "interpretive agency."

The best choice depends on your desired audience. For an academic press, conceptual precision and engagement with existing scholarship matter more than the elegance of a familiar term. For a practitioner book, the simpler label may be worth retaining, but you should still acknowledge that it is a deliberate departure from common usage.

**Some claims are too broad for the evidence presented.** The manuscript's voice is one of its assets, but a number of lines make claims that are more categorical than the material can yet carry. Examples include claims that modern schooling "teaches for completion," that teachers are systematically stopping students "precisely when they are thinking," that pseudo-agency is especially prevalent in high-poverty schools, and that the system "literally doesn't recognize" certain kinds of knowing.

The issue is not that these claims are implausible. The issue is that a book pitched as theoretically serious needs to distinguish: interpretive claim: what you see in cases; normative claim: what education ought to recognize; empirical claim: what generally occurs across schools; causal claim: what institutional conditions produce; rhetorical diagnosis: language intended to make a professional experience vivid.

For example, instead of saying that a practice "creates" disengagement, you might write that it "can contribute to disengagement by repeatedly treating students' self-originated sense-making as irrelevant, mistaken, or disruptive." This remains forceful while making a claim your evidence can support.

**The equity argument is high-stakes.** The Preface says that the engine is economic rather than race or class, while race enters through the proposed mechanism. That formulation will be one of the first places editors and peer reviewers pause.

The problem is not simply political. The phrase risks setting up false alternatives: racialized inequality is not "inherent in race," but it is structural, historical, material, and institutional; class does not protect all learners, but class power, cultural capital, residential segregation, racial stratification, and school-resource distribution do shape differential access to recognition, mentorship, curricular enrichment, disciplinary safety, and time; "economic" can become too narrow if it excludes racialized institutional arrangements and differential exposure to surveillance, exclusion, and deficit interpretation.

The manuscript will be stronger if it says something like: the proposed mechanism is not an intrinsic trait of racial or class groups; rather, unequal access to material security, institutional trust, cultural recognition, discretionary time, and intellectual mentorship shapes which forms of agency are legible, cultivated, or punished. Then show how race, class, disability, language, gender, immigration status, and neighborhood are not competing "engines" but often interlocking conditions through which this mechanism operates.

This chapter needs exceptionally careful scholarship and probably external developmental editing or sensitivity review before submission. It is central enough that it cannot remain a promise deferred to Chapter 4.

**The book needs a clearer evidentiary contract.** The Preface admirably states that this is a case study of one teacher's classrooms and research sites in dialogue with theory, rather than a meta-analysis. That honesty is good.

But an editor will still ask: Which cases come from published research, dissertation data, field notes, teaching memory, family observation, or reconstructed composite scenes? What are the dates and settings of the cases? What consent, IRB, institutional, and pseudonymization protections apply? Which claims arise from systematic analysis versus practitioner inference? When a scene is used as an exemplar, how do readers know it is not selected only because it perfectly fits the model?

You do not need to turn the book into a methods monograph. But a short methodological appendix—or a "How to Read the Cases" section early in the book—would substantially increase credibility. It could name the evidence types, explain reconstruction practices, clarify that cases illuminate possibilities rather than establish prevalence, and explain how theory was developed iteratively across settings.

### Voice, audience, and positioning

The book currently straddles three genres: a research-informed theory of agency; a teacher-facing professional book; a polemical critique of contemporary schooling and policy. That hybridity can work, but the market positioning needs to be deliberate.

For a university or academic press, the book needs: a more explicit scholarly intervention; a refined literature review at the points of conceptual departure; robust methods and ethical framing; careful qualification of empirical generalizations; a more measured treatment of rival theories and school models.

For a practitioner/professional publisher, the book needs: a repeatable teacher decision tool; lesson-level examples across disciplines and grades; scripts for responding to each kind of agency; guidance for common constraints: 30 students, pacing requirements, multilingual learners, disability accommodations, grading, state assessments, and administrators; usable tables, protocols, planning templates, and "what to say next" moves.

At present, the manuscript has the theory and urgency of a serious academic-professional crossover book, but not yet enough of the practitioner apparatus to prove its promise of doability. The book repeatedly says that it will help teachers know what to do next; Chapters 1–5 have established why that is needed, but a proposal should demonstrate that the later chapters genuinely deliver it.

The direct, colloquial lines—"flip the sucker," "effort porn," "zombification," "epistemic gaslighting," and the sexualized Montessori-training joke—will attract some readers and repel others. Their use is not automatically disqualifying, but they need strategic review. "School-as-theater" and "pseudo-agency" are memorable and analytically useful. "Zombification" may work as a vivid rhetorical device but can become reductive if overused. "Effort porn" is likely too polarizing for many school leaders, teacher educators, and academic-press reviewers; "the aestheticization of strain" conveys the critique without narrowing the readership. "Epistemic gaslighting" has force but invokes a term associated with psychological abuse and manipulation. Use it only with careful qualification—or replace it with language such as "erosion of epistemic confidence" or "systematic invalidation of students' sense-making." The joke about "one man" making "five women" happy risks shifting the tone away from the intellectual point and may undermine professional trust.

Your best voice is the one that is candid, perceptive, unsentimental about institutional constraints, and generous toward teachers and students. That voice is already present. It does not need the most combustible phrasing to remain memorable.

### What would make it publishable

I would advise pursuing publication, but through a revision process organized around the book's central contribution rather than chapter-by-chapter polishing.

1. **Write a one-page argument architecture.** Before revising prose, specify the chain of claims: Students routinely make varied bids to act as knowers. Institutions and pedagogies privilege legible, answer-aligned forms of participation. This produces recurrent misrecognitions, not simply isolated teacher errors. Misrecognition can weaken students' relationship to themselves as knowers and contribute to disengagement, compliance, or displaced agency. A taxonomy can improve teacher perception. Distinguishing forms of agency permits differentiated pedagogical response. Schools must also alter structures—time, assessment, credit, evaluation, and authority—if teachers are to sustain answerable practice. Every chapter should visibly advance one or more of these propositions. Anything that does not should be shortened, moved to an endnote, or removed.

2. **Stabilize the vocabulary.** Create a glossary and apply it ruthlessly. In particular, differentiate: motivation; engagement; autonomy; agency; origination; answerability; sense-making; meaning-making; authorship; epistemic agency or reframing; recognition; legibility; dialogue; chronotope. The manuscript currently does much of this work, but occasionally offers multiple definitions of agency with changing emphasis. For example, agency is introduced as "capabilities + decision-making + origination + answerability," then narrowed in the classroom to origination and answerability on an object of knowing. That can be coherent, but it must be framed as a deliberate shift from a broad philosophical account to a situated analytic definition.

3. **Make the taxonomy testable in practice.** For each agency configuration, include a standard sequence:

| Teacher question | Needed content |
|---|---|
| What might this look like? | Observable student language, behavior, artifacts, and interaction patterns |
| What is commonly misread? | The usual interpretive error |
| What is at stake? | What is lost if the bid is redirected, ignored, or punished |
| What can the teacher do next? | Specific discursive moves, not merely aspirations |
| What should the teacher avoid? | Common overcorrections |
| What are the limits? | When a reframe should be tested, bounded, redirected, or deferred |
| What institutional support is needed? | Time, assessment flexibility, credit, grading, policy, or collegial norms |

That would convert the taxonomy from a compelling interpretive map into a professional tool.

For Nancy, for example, the teacher moves might be: "Show me how the center coin works in your arrangement." "What makes this equal in your model?" "Does 'equal' have more than one meaning here?" "How is your arrangement related to 33 remainder 1?" "What would change if we had four people, or five?" "What question is your representation answering that the usual equation does not?"

This preserves disciplinary rigor. Answerable teaching cannot mean that any student reframe is automatically accepted as mathematically sufficient. It means that the reframe is received as an intelligible contribution, investigated, tested, and placed in relation to disciplinary knowledge.

4. **Strengthen the disciplinary account.** The manuscript's critique will be more persuasive if it repeatedly says: student origination is not the alternative to disciplinary knowledge; it is a route into more meaningful disciplinary accountability. Without that clarification, skeptical readers may worry that the book substitutes personal expression for expertise or encourages teachers to validate every interpretation. But your strongest passages already point elsewhere: Angie's procedure should be tested against more cases; Nancy's model should be surfaced and investigated; students' contributions should become answerable to peers, objects, evidence, and disciplinary standards. That is the intellectual heart of the book: teaching should neither suppress student meaning nor romanticize it. It should bring students' originating moves into an inquiry where they can be elaborated, contested, revised, and made more powerful.

5. **Revise the equity chapter early.** Do not leave Chapter 4 until late-stage drafting. It will determine whether readers see the book as a serious structural account or as a universalizing critique grounded too narrowly in individual experience. The chapter should: distinguish institutional mechanisms from individual traits; avoid claims about high-poverty schools unless supported by research and carefully contextualized; address racialization, material inequality, surveillance, exclusion, cultural capital, linguistic hierarchy, disability, and access to intellectual mentorship; show how agency is differentially interpreted depending on who the student is and where they attend school; avoid treating economic analysis as a replacement for race-conscious analysis.

6. **Remove every visible draft artifact.** The current compilation explicitly identifies itself as unfinished and contains bracketed gaps, placeholder notes, unresolved citations, duplicated material, and claims that await verification. None of that is a conceptual defect, but it means the manuscript is in developmental form rather than submission form. Before querying publishers, complete: citation verification, especially quotations and contested theoretical claims; permissions review for any lengthy quotations, screenshots, or reproduced material; a consistent endnote/reference system; case-source and consent documentation; cross-reference correction after the final chapter order is stable; removal of all [GAP], [PLACEHOLDER], [DECIDE], and internal AI-provenance material from any external-facing version.

The provenance note is appropriate for internal editorial transparency, but it should not be part of a manuscript circulated to acquisitions editors unless specifically requested. The externally relevant issue is authorial responsibility, source integrity, and rights—not the workflow history of particular drafting passes.

### Bottom line

The book's ideas are valuable enough to publish. Its clearest original contribution is a theory of answerable teaching built around recognition, origination, and differentiated forms of student agency. The argument has genuine intellectual ambition, and the classroom cases make it emotionally and pedagogically credible.

The manuscript will be strongest if it becomes less a broad denunciation of schooling and more a rigorously developed answer to one precise question: How can teachers distinguish a student's compliance, partial sense-making, reframing, collective claim, or self-authored purpose—and respond in ways that deepen both disciplinary learning and the student's standing as a knower?

That is a question educators need help answering, especially amid AI-mediated completion, increasingly visible disengagement, and institutional pressure to mistake evidence of task completion for evidence of thought. The book has a real chance of making a lasting contribution if the taxonomy is clarified, the empirical and equity claims are strengthened, and the promised teacher moves become as concrete as the diagnosis.

### Revised bottom line

The later chapters significantly improve the manuscript's publishability. Two of the major concerns from my earlier review — the absence of a concrete practitioner apparatus and the lack of a structural/institutional account — are now directly addressed. Chapter 5 supplies the pedagogical architecture that makes answerable teaching a teachable practice rather than an ethic. The AI chapters give the book its most timely public-facing hook. Chapters 8–9 move from classroom to institution without losing the thread. And Chapter [11] delivers the teacher decision tool I said was missing.

The book is stronger than it looked from Chapters 1–4 alone. Its strongest publishable thesis is now visible in full:

Schools mistake visible completion for learning because their accountability systems privilege legible outputs. This misreading bears unequally on students whose prior access to guided participation, background experience, and institutional fluency is restricted. Answerable teaching — joining dialogism, perceptual design (Variation Theory), and intellectual apprenticeship — offers a classroom-level response, and AI can offload the production work that made this response impossibly expensive for ordinary teachers. But classroom craft hits institutional ceilings: without genuine student governance and legitimate pathways for student-authored learning, even excellent teaching works against a structural current.

That is a serious, timely, and original argument. The manuscript now has theory, diagnosis, pedagogical architecture, practical tools, an AI contribution, structural critique, and a philosophical capstone. It is closer to a complete book than it has ever been.

### What the later chapters add

**Chapter 5 makes answerable teaching teachable.** This is the chapter that converts the book's ethic into a practice. Three ideas do the work: Dialogism as reception, not just talk. The distinction between structured talk (think-pair-share, Socratic seminar) and dialogism as an ethic of attention to the learner's current reality as a knower is sharply drawn. The Ferris Bueller opening is perfect — recognizable, funny, and exactly the phenomenon being named. Sense-making vs. meaning-making. This distinction is one of the book's most valuable analytical moves. Sense-making is orienting within a phenomenon ("What is going on here?"); meaning-making is metabolizing the idea into one's own thinking, testing and reframing it. The distinction explains effortful agency (stalls at sense-making), engaged agency (invests in sense-making within the frame), and epistemic agency (crosses into meaning-making by reframing the frame). That is a clean developmental progression that the taxonomy needed. Variation Theory as the perceptual bridge. The argument that dialogism needs a shared object of attention — and that Variation Theory creates it — is the missing piece. A dialogic classroom without perceptual structure becomes unmoored; Variation Theory without dialogism becomes a better delivery mechanism. The bridge is the object of attention. This is intellectually precise and practically actionable.

The Bakhtin grammar-teaching passage is a strong rhetorical move: it shows that direct instruction and dialogism are not opposites, that teaching concepts carefully is the precondition for dialogue, not a retreat from it. This directly addresses a concern reviewers will have.

**The AI chapters give the book its widest possible audience.** Chapter [A] and [B] are the manuscript's most immediately relevant contribution to current educational discourse. The argument is narrower and more interesting than "AI will transform teaching" or "AI can't replace teachers": AI does the production work of teaching (lesson architecture, slides, feedback drafts, IEP-accessible assessments, simulations), which frees the teacher's presence for the work that requires a person. The feedback pipeline — AI-drafted feedback in a specified dialogic register, read and corrected by the teacher before it goes out — is the mechanism that makes dialogue-located correction affordable at scale. Two-tier grading creates a turn structure: the first tier asks whether the student thought; the feedback opens a question; the second tier is the revision. This is the closest the book comes to operationalizing the dialogic register with thirty students. The boundary argument is precise: AI can create the conditions for dialogic teaching without itself being dialogic teaching, because dialogic teaching requires not knowing, for a moment, what the right answer to a student's contribution is.

The "control of variation vs. control of error" distinction — drawn from Montessori but extended — is genuinely original. A material with a control of error has already decided what the information means. A material with a control of variation reports state and lets the student produce something wrong but interesting. This is a design principle educators can use immediately.

**Chapter [7] fills the developmental gap.** "Adjacent agency" is the book's most useful new concept. It names the phenomenon the taxonomy was missing: students who need to experience themselves as agents through real participation — roles, responsibilities, consequences — before that agency reliably attaches to academic learning. Noelle's classroom is a vivid case study, and the town hall, Gustov's lab-cleaning, and the sandwich-making lesson give the concept concrete form.

The Patall complication is strategically important: autonomy support benefits students who already have an agentic orientation, but fails for students who don't — and those students are disproportionately the ones most exposed to stereotype threat. Adjacent agency is the intervention into that loop. It gives students something real to be responsible for first, so the experience of agency becomes available before it is demanded in academic form. This connects the equity argument to the pedagogical architecture in a way the earlier chapters could not.

**Chapters 8–9 move from classroom to institution without losing the thread.** Chapter 8 does several things well: it defines authorial agency as different in kind (not degree) from the other forms, uses Marcus as the limit case, introduces the Marcus/Paul pair to guard against reading institutional reception as student difference, and critiques both SDT and the democratic school tradition for treating authorial agency as a universal endpoint. The Karen/Dana/Cindy cases from the democratic school show that freedom without structure produces disorientation, not agency. This is a fair and sophisticated critique.

Chapter 9 is where the book's structural argument fully arrives. Zara's voice ("We don't got no voice here") is the strongest piece of student testimony in the manuscript. The bathroom policy analysis, the two theories of order, Lewin's research, and Eli's greenhouse give the institutional critique concrete form. The Big Picture Learning and Mastery Transcript Consortium references show that alternatives exist — this is not merely a critique but a demonstration that the structural absences are solvable.

**Chapter 10 is the philosophical capstone.** The Bakhtinian framing of centripetal and centrifugal forces gives the book its theoretical culmination. The argument that agency emerges in the sustained tension between structure and freedom — not in the victory of either — is the right philosophical grounding for the taxonomy. The double bind analysis (drawn from Bateson) is an important caution: schools can stage the appearance of tension while quietly eliminating any valid way to respond. The Socratic School example is a vivid case of managed monologism disguised as dialogue.

**Chapter [11] delivers the practitioner tool.** This is the chapter my earlier review said was missing. The annotated moves index — thirteen entries, each with the move, what the institution saw, what was there, and what catches it — is exactly the kind of teacher decision tool the book promised. It is not yet prose; it reads like field notes or an index. But the structure is right, and the content is genuinely useful. The guard in entry 13 ("never let a material, a question set, or a repertoire become the complete list") applies unfinalizability to practice rather than to persons — a beautiful move.

### How prior criticisms change

**1. Taxonomy: improved but now risks proliferation.** The later chapters add several new terms — adjacent agency, intellectual authorship, agentic engagement, autonomy support, meaning-making — that need to be situated in a stable hierarchy alongside the original seven-part map. The risk is no longer that the taxonomy is underspecified; it is that it has grown faster than its architecture can hold.

I recommend explicitly establishing a layered structure: Dimensions of agency: origination, answerability, capability, decision-making. Objects of action: assigned disciplinary object, framing of the object, shared conditions of learning, self-authored course of study. Generative configurations: effortful, engaged, epistemic, democratic, authorial. Distortions/breakdowns: pseudo-agency, dysfunctional agency. Developmental bridges: adjacent agency (social/material agency before academic agency attaches). Underlying capacity: intellectual authorship (the capacity all forms express).

Adjacent agency and intellectual authorship should be explicitly marked as operating at different analytical levels from the seven configurations. A glossary is now essential, not optional.

**2. "Epistemic agency" naming: still an open question.** Chapter 5 makes the category more intelligible — the sense-making/meaning-making distinction clarifies what epistemic agency is and where it begins. But the later chapters also show that your usage may really mean "epistemic reframing" or "frame-transforming agency." The Nancy counter-scene in Chapter 5 is excellent: John could have said "I wonder how it fits as a math model" — that is the move that receives an epistemic bid without requiring the teacher to already understand it. But the term itself still competes with established learning-sciences usage. The three options I offered earlier (retain with rigorous comparison, retain with modifier, or rename) all remain viable. Chapter 5's conceptual precision makes the case for your usage stronger, but the literature engagement still needs to happen.

**3. Claims too broad for evidence: still blocking.** The later chapters add many new empirical and theoretical claims that need verification and bounding: The AI pipeline's "roughly forty percent" of teacher hours and "twenty to sixty percent increase in presence" — honestly offered as estimates, but still need framing as practitioner inference, not measurement. Patall's findings (Kennedy et al. 2022 and Zambrano et al. 2022) — need exact attribution; the stray "(Patall, 2024)" citation flag is noted in the text itself. Lewin, Lippitt & White (1939) — classic research, but the specifics of the authoritarian-group collapse need to be verified against the original. Big Picture Learning and Mastery Transcript Consortium — need URL verification and current status. The executive function / SES claims (Raver, Blair, & Willoughby, 2013; Bibok, Carpendale, & Müller, 2009) — need careful framing to avoid implying that poverty produces cognitive deficit. The Socratic School / Bhaer / Tom material — needs verification against the dissertation. The "no-excuses models never hand the instrument over" — still categorical; should be bounded.

The principle remains: distinguish interpretive, normative, empirical, causal, and rhetorical claims. The later chapters are more careful than the earlier ones in some respects (Chapter 5's Lezir passage is appropriately modest), but the AI and whole-school chapters introduce new claim types that need the same discipline.

**4. Equity argument: substantially improved.** Chapter 4 already improved this, and Chapters 7–9 extend it. The Patall complication, the executive-function/developmental-floor argument, and Zara's testimony give the equity argument structural depth it did not have before. The formulation in Chapter [B] — "Pedagogy mitigates structural problems; it does not cure them" — is exactly the right level of claim.

The remaining vulnerability is the executive-function argument. The claim that chronic stress associated with poverty "measurably shapes" how executive function develops is defensible if carefully framed, but it risks being read as a deficit account of poor children's cognitive capacity. The framing in the text is good — "She was given fewer of the relationships through which the capacity is built" — but it needs to be airtight. The no-excuses reading of the same research ("the institution holds the goal, narrates the steps, and never hands the regulating over") is one of the book's strongest analytical moves and should be foregrounded.

**5. Evidentiary contract: more urgent, not less.** The later chapters introduce more case types than the earlier ones: personal classroom cases (Lezir, Gustov, Zara, Eli), dissertation fieldwork (The Circle School, The Socratic School, Karen/Dana/Cindy), colleague's classrooms (Noelle), family/homeschool examples (Ben, Peter), student quotes (Zara's dialogue), and AI-generated feedback pipeline examples. A "How to Read the Cases" section or methodological appendix is now essential, not optional. It should name the evidence types, explain reconstruction practices, clarify consent and IRB arrangements for dissertation material, and state that cases illuminate possibilities rather than establish prevalence.

**6. Audience and genre: improved but more hybrid.** The manuscript is now even more of a hybrid: a research-informed theory of agency, a teacher-facing professional book, an AI argument, and a school-reform argument. ### New vulnerabilities introduced by the later chapters

**The AI chapters could date quickly.** The specific pipeline descriptions — the shared deck, the feedback drafting, the two-tier grading — are tied to current tool affordances. If the tools change, the mechanics become obsolete. Recommend framing the principles (production labor vs. presence; control of variation vs. control of error; the feedback pipeline as enabling condition) as durable, and the specific tool uses as current examples. A short note acknowledging that practices depend on current tool capabilities would help.

**"AI cannot do dialogic teaching" may be challenged.** The claim is rhetorically strong and currently defensible, but as models evolve, a reviewer may challenge it. The safer formulation — which the text already approaches — is: "Current AI systems, especially when used as output-scoring systems, do not provide the human answerability this pedagogy requires." The argument that the limitation is structural (a tool built to produce and assess correct outputs cannot run the second chain) is more durable than a claim about any particular model's capabilities.

**Chapter order and numbering are unstable.** The placeholder numbering ([A], [B], [7], [11]) and the editorial notes about placement reveal that the architecture is not yet settled. A publisher will need one coherent arc. The strongest sequence appears to be: Preface → Introduction; Ch 1: Disconnect (cases); Ch 2: Taxonomy; Ch 3: School models; Ch 4: Manufacture of pseudo-agency (equity/structural mechanism); Ch 5: Dialogic teaching (pedagogical architecture); Ch [A/B]: AI (production labor freed → presence restored); Ch [7]: Room-level ecology (adjacent agency, democratic agency in a classroom); Ch 8: Authorial agency (limit case); Ch 9: Whole-school change; Ch 10: Holding the tension (philosophical capstone); Ch [11]: The Moves (practitioner index); Capstone (apprenticeship in intellectual authorship).

The AI chapters belong after Chapter 5 (they depend on Variation Theory and dialogism) and before the room-level chapters (they explain how the pedagogical architecture becomes affordable). This also gives the book a natural midpoint pivot: from theory and diagnosis to practice and structure.

**Chapter [11] needs expansion or deliberate formatting.** The annotated moves index is the right idea but currently reads as notes, not prose. Two viable paths: Expand into full sections: For each move, write a brief scene, the institutional misread, what was actually there, the teacher move, and what to avoid. This would make it a genuine practitioner chapter. Format deliberately as a field guide: Use the current structure but design it as a reference tool — numbered entries, consistent sub-headings, cross-references. This is unusual for an academic press but would work for a professional/crossover book. Either way, the current draft-note format cannot be the final form.

**The whole-school chapters risk overextending scope.** Chapters 8–9 move from classroom to institution, which is necessary for the argument. But the bathroom-policy analysis, the Lewin research, the two theories of order, the governance mechanisms, and the Big Picture Learning references could expand the book beyond what its core claim requires. The strongest version keeps these chapters tightly connected to the central question: what can classrooms do, where do they hit institutional ceilings, and what must schools recognize and credit? The bathroom analysis is vivid but could be shortened; the governance mechanisms could be compressed into a single clear section rather than developed across multiple sub-sections.

### Bottom line

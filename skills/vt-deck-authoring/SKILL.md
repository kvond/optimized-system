# vt-deck-authoring

*Public-facing. This skill ships with two companion documents: the rationale, and
the case file. Read the case file first. It will not take long and the rest of
this makes a different kind of sense afterward.*

---

## 0. What this skill will and will not do

**It will** carry the structure: the order of the questions, what has to be held
still while something else varies, how exposed the work is, the format, and the
checks.

**It will not** decide what is worth teaching in your content, whether a student's
question should be followed, or when to abandon the plan. Those are yours. The
structure exists so there is room left for them.

[If you find yourself wanting the skill to make one of those calls, that is worth
noticing. It usually means the content decision underneath has not been made yet.]

---

## 1. Before any slide exists

Answer these three, in writing, before building.

**1. What is the critical aspect?** The one feature of this phenomenon a student
must be able to discern in order to understand it. Not the topic. Not the
standard. The thing that, if she cannot see it varying, leaves her unable to
reason about the rest.

**2. What varies, and what is held invariant?** A student discerns an aspect by
meeting cases that differ in that aspect and in nothing else. If your examples
differ in three ways, she cannot tell which difference matters, and she will
usually pick the wrong one.

**3. What does she already have to have discerned?** Cycles depend on each other.
Write down the dependency.

---

## 2. The 5 core questions

During origination, apply the shared [clear-wording standard](../vt-skill/reference/17-lesson-renewal.md#1710-clear-student-facing-wording-on-every-slide)
to every slide type, not only these questions. State necessary conditions in short
sentences and give one clear ask at a time. Match the course, topic and student
level while preserving the scientific reasoning demand and neutral wording before
discernment.

In this order, once for each critical aspect.

1. **Critical Aspect question.** Opens the aspect.
2. **Contrast Set.** Case A against Case B. Everything the same except one thing.
3. **Build a Rule.** The student completes a sentence stating a general rule.
4. **Pattern Break.** The case that strains the rule she just made.
5. **3-Tier Question.** Getting Started, Working On It, Mastery. With a word
   bank, offered as "use any, modify any, or use none."

The vocabulary is **5 core questions** and **slide types**. Use no other word for
them — not in a deck, not in a manual, not in sales copy, not in this file.

**The rule on question 1, and it is where most cycles fail in the room.** An
aspect *is* a difference. A question that names a thing without naming a
difference cannot be answered, and the class goes quiet. "How are polar bears
adapted?" produces silence. "Which polar bear is more adapted, one with long fur
or short fur?" produces an answer, and *then* "why did you choose?" opens it.

Put the variation inside the question. Do not name the aspect on slide 1 and wait
for the Contrast Set on slide 2 to supply the difference.

Show that difference as well: each VT comparison slide needs pictures that let
students inspect the cases. Add them when absent, retain suitable existing
pictures, and check the contrast at final size without revealing the answer.
For sourcing, attribution and layout, use [the image standard](../vt-skill/reference/06-images.md)
and [the shared initial-build/renewal rule](../vt-skill/reference/17-lesson-renewal.md#177-visual-reasoning-response-mode-and-titles).

**The check, and it is mechanical.** Does the Critical Aspect question text itself
contain a comparison, a choice between named alternatives, or a stated change
condition? If it does not, it is structurally the polar bear question, whatever
its subject, and the room will go quiet before the contrast arrives.

Narrow first, then widen. This inverts the common failure, where a teacher asks
open, meets silence, and collapses into gap-fill — at which point the chance to
explore is gone.

## 3. The four conditional slide types

Use only where the content calls for them. Forcing one on every cycle is the
commonest way this design gets flattened.

6. **Continuity question.** A question with no answer to look up.
7. **Stock-and-flow model.** Only where the aspect turns on a quantity that fills
   and drains.
8. **Compensatory pair.** Two aspects vary inversely while the outcome stays the
   same, so neither alone predicts it.
9. **Conflict case.** One case in which opposing pulls cannot be resolved from
   either aspect alone.

The numbering is not decoration. The 5 core questions are moves 1 through 5 and
the four conditional structures are moves 6 through 9. Moves 7, 8 and 9 are the
coordination structures, and section 5 is where you choose between them.

A cycle that carries none of the four is not thereby defective. A cycle that
carries one you cannot give a reason for is.

## 3b. The nine moves, and where they come from

Nine moves, each with a cognitive purpose and a source. The lineage runs
**Marton and Tsui → Moore-Anderson → von Duyke**.

| # | Move | Cognitive purpose | Source | What is added |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Critical Aspect question | Opens the aspect as a thing that can vary | Marton (aspect); Moore-Anderson (form) | The difference has to be inside the question — see §2 |
| 2 | Contrast Set | Discernment: one dimension differs, everything else held | Marton (contrast) | A fixed slot in a fixed order, once per aspect |
| 3 | Build a Rule | Generalization: the student states the pattern in her own words | Marton (generalization) | Sentence completion, so the rule is hers and is *written*, which makes it available to strain |
| 4 | Pattern Break | Separation: the case the rule cannot hold | Marton (separation) | Positioned immediately after her own rule, so the strain is on something she authored |
| 5 | 3-Tier Question | Graduated demand without tracking | von Duyke | Getting Started / Working On It / Mastery, word bank offered as "use any, modify any, or use none" |
| 6 | Continuity question | A question with no answer to look up | von Duyke | Bakhtinian: a question that cannot be received as authoritative |
| 7 | Stock-and-flow model | Coordinates rate against amount | Moore-Anderson | Conditional, with a decision rule for when not to use it |
| 8 | Compensatory pair | Fusion: two aspects vary inversely, outcome invariant | von Duyke | Extends fusion to content that does not accumulate |
| 9 | Conflict case | Fusion: opposing pulls in one case, unresolvable from one aspect | von Duyke | Aimed at teleology, biology's central obstacle |
| — | What-if | Tests whether coordination happened | Moore-Anderson (form); von Duyke (position) | Evidence rather than occasion. Individual and written — see §5b |

**Marton** supplies the four patterns of variation — contrast, generalization,
separation, fusion — and the intended, enacted and lived objects of learning.
*Learning and Awareness* (Marton and Booth, 1997); *Classroom Discourse and the
Space of Learning* (Marton and Tsui, 2004); *Necessary Conditions of Learning*
(2015).

**Moore-Anderson** supplies two things: that the question must contain the
variation, and stock-and-flow as a question form for rate against amount.
*Teaching Meaning: What Works When Telling Isn't Enough*; *Difference Maker*.

**von Duyke** supplies the sequence, the repetition unit, the coordination
structures beyond stock-and-flow, the coordination layer, and the distribution of
judgment.

[Citation caution, and it matters if you write any of this up. Moore-Anderson is
an independent practitioner-author, not peer-reviewed literature. Cite him for the
design lineage — that is honest and it is correct — and ground the underlying
claims in Marton and Tsui, so a reviewer has something she can check.]

[Open, and carried here as open: whether the what-if is a tenth move or a required
companion to whichever coordination structure a cycle carries. It is treated as a
companion throughout this file, because it tests coordination rather than creating
it. That has not been settled.]

## 4. Simultaneity, and the two questions that follow from it

A teacher says a student does not **conceptualize** it. That is a verdict with no
next move attached, and the default responses — more review, more practice —
address neither of the two things that could actually be wrong.

Marton's construct is **simultaneity**: holding more than one thing in awareness
at once. It divides two ways, and your cycle works on one of them or on both.

**Synchronic simultaneity.** Two or more critical aspects present in the same
case, in the same moment, so the student has to coordinate them to resolve it.
This is fusion in the strict sense, and section 5 is how you build for it.

**Diachronic simultaneity.** Things met at different times brought into awareness
together, so relations between them become discernible for the first time.
Nothing new is introduced; a sequence becomes a structure. The Concept Bank does
this, and it is the harder of the two to engineer. Most curricula do not attempt
it.

**The diagnostic that follows, and it is the most useful thing in this file:**

| Ask | If it failed | What the student needs next |
|---|---|---|
| Did she discern the aspects? | Separation | Another contrast |
| Did she coordinate them when the case required both? | Fusion | A case that will not resolve on one aspect |

Same observed performance. Opposite instruction. A student who has not separated
the aspects needs another contrast and will be wasted on a fusion case; a student
who separated them cleanly and did not coordinate them will sit through another
contrast learning nothing she does not already have.

Both terms are teacher-facing, with a one-line gloss. **Neither appears in
anything a student reads.** No coding vocabulary to students, ever.

## 5. The fusion decision

Separating aspects one at a time is not the end of the work. A student can
discern each aspect cleanly and still fail to coordinate them, which shows up on
a transfer task and gets misread as low effort.

Ask: **can two of my critical aspects operate at the same time in a real case?**

If yes, pick the structure that fits. These are moves 7, 8 and 9.

- **Stock-and-flow (move 7)** — the aspects are a quantity and the rates that
  change it.
- **Compensatory pair (move 8)** — two aspects vary inversely while the outcome
  stays the same, so neither alone predicts it. *Sea turtle and elephant: a
  hundred eggs with almost no survival, one calf with high survival, both
  populations steady.*
- **Conflict case (move 9)** — one aspect points one way, another points the
  other, and the case cannot be resolved from either alone. *Legless snakes.*

If no, say so and record why. Some content has no coordination to do, and an
invented device wastes a slide and teaches a false relationship.

**The requirement, whichever you choose:** the case must be unanswerable from one
aspect. If a student can reach a defensible answer using only one, the slide has
not done fusion work.

### 5a. Three structures, three kinds of object

They are not the same kind of thing, and the difference decides what you can put
into a cycle and what you have to find already sitting in it.

**Stock-and-flow is a representation.** The student reads or builds a model, and
the coordination is rate against amount — a stubborn confusion in its own right,
independent of your content. It can be **added** to any cycle whose content
accumulates.

**A compensatory pair is a case set.** Two real cases, the same outcome, inverted
values on two aspects. Nothing is modeled; the coordination comes from the
comparison. It has to be **found** in your content.

**A conflict case is a single case** with opposing pulls. It also has to be
**found**.

[This is why nobody, and no skill, can hand you a fusion device for arbitrary
content. A representation ports. A case set does not, and a manufactured one
teaches a relationship that is not there. If the content carries no compensatory
relationship and no genuine conflict case, the honest output is "none, and here is
why" written into the teacher note.]

## 5b. The what-if — evidence, not occasion

A counterfactual asks the student to hold the case, change one aspect, and
generate the consequence. She cannot do that without already holding the relation
between the aspects. So it does not create coordination. It reveals whether
coordination happened.

Moves 7, 8 and 9 are **occasions** for coordination. The what-if is **evidence**
of it, and the evidence is visible in what she writes rather than inferable from a
score. Treat it as a required companion to whichever coordination structure the
cycle carries. A fusion slide with no what-if is an occasion you did not read.

Three constraints, and the cycle fails at this without them:

- **Individual and written.** Moves 8 and 9 may run as group work — high
  complexity at low exposure is a good pairing. But a group product says nothing
  about who coordinated, so a group what-if is an empty diagnosis.
- **Specify what changes and what is held.** "What if there were no decomposers?"
  leaves the student not knowing how far to go or at what scale. The constrained
  forms are: how would this differ *in this other context*, or *if I changed this
  one thing*. An unconstrained counterfactual is the polar bear question again,
  wearing a different hat.
- **In biology, hold the mechanism fixed and vary the condition.** Open
  counterfactuals invite teleological reasoning straight back in. "What if snakes
  had kept their legs?" hands a student reaching for purpose an easy answer.
  Ask instead what would have happened if the ancestral population had lived in
  open ground rather than in burrows. Same coordination demand, no invitation to
  purpose.

## 6. The Concept Bank

Terms met across separate weeks, brought onto one page so relations between them
become visible. Nothing new is introduced. What changes is that a sequence becomes
a structure. This is diachronic simultaneity as a built artifact.

**The requirement: the page must ask the student to state how two of the terms
connect.** Co-presence is the precondition, not the achievement. A page that only
lists the terms has achieved co-presence and left the relating to chance. Same
absence of testing pressure either way — the difference is entirely in the demand.

[This is also the reason review sheets and cumulative tests do not do this work.
They attempt diachronic simultaneity and reach only co-presence, because filling a
blank exercises retrieval, and retrieval leaves a sequence a sequence.]

## 7. The teacher note

One slide. Not speaker notes. Deletable in one action if you decide against it.

Seven declarations:

1. The critical aspect, in plain words.
2. What is held invariant, and the statement that the examples differ in one
   dimension on purpose.
3. **What breaks if an example is substituted.**
4. What this cycle assumes, and what depends on it.
5. Which slide types appear, and which conditional ones were left out, and why.
6. **Which simultaneity this cycle works on** — synchronic, diachronic, or both.
7. **The visibility rung** — for example, "written and private, building toward
   unattributed read-aloud."

Declaration 7 names a rung. The slide right after this one explains what the five
rungs are — see §7c, "The slide that carries this". Ship the pair, or declaration 7
reads as jargon to the one teacher who most needs it.

[Why this is a slide and not a manual entry: a manual is read after compliance is
already the frame, if it is read at all. A teacher adapting your deck in good
faith, not knowing that three examples differ in exactly one dimension by design,
will substitute a better example and destroy the contrast. The note protects the
design against helpful improvement. In Marton's terms it protects the intended
object of learning as the enacted one changes hands.]

[Why the visibility line in particular is worth writing down, and it is not
paperwork. A room at rung 1 or 2 shows quiet students writing. That reads as low
engagement against every walkthrough rubric in use, and it is the correct
instruction for that room at that time. A cycle that declares its rung converts an
absence into a stated design decision. An observer can disagree with a decision.
She cannot disagree with an absence, because an absence looks like a failure.]

## 7b. Enactment — how the question runs in the room

Design is half of it. This is the other half, and it is the part a slide cannot
carry on its own.

1. Establish the difference.
2. Ask a multiple-choice question about it. Narrow the possibilities on purpose.
3. Pair talk.
4. Sequential hands-up vote, so you can see who votes for what.
5. Choose the next move from the vote pattern:
   - Split → two students who voted differently justify, then re-vote.
   - Low confidence → more pair talk, vote again on the original question.
   - Confident and wrong → several students justify, to surface what they saw.
6. Widen: why did you choose?

**On the vote, and this matters if your students are not there yet.** A hands-up
vote asks for two things at once: a commitment, and public exposure. In a room
where a wrong first answer is not yet safe, the exposure cost dominates and the
rational move is to look at nobody. Voting is a capability you build toward, not
a technique that works on demand. It is rung 5 of the ladder below, and it is
last.

## 7c. Visibility — the second scale

Visibility runs on its own scale, from lowest to highest: written and private, to
a partner, read aloud by the teacher without a name, owned aloud, voted.

Complexity runs on a separate scale: one aspect, a rule, a strained rule, two
aspects coordinated, a counterfactual.

**Do not lock them together.** A high-complexity question is usually easier in a
low-visibility form, because the student needs thinking time more than she needs
an audience. A vote is low complexity at high visibility, which is why it lands
badly early. If visibility rises monotonically across the arc, your
highest-exposure moves land on your hardest content, which is the worst pairing
available. Raise visibility across the year; reset it low inside each cycle.

### The ladder, gated by evidence

The rungs open on what you can observe, not on the week of the year. The mechanism
throughout is that the student is gathering evidence about what happens to wrong
answers in your room, and she cannot get that evidence from being told.

**Rung 1 — written, seen only by you.** No precondition beyond not having punished
wrongness. The structure is the two-tier response: first answer scored for
completion, revision is where thinking counts.
*Ready for rung 2 when* most students write a first answer at all, and some first
answers are wrong in interesting ways rather than blank or copied. A blank is a
refusal to commit even privately, which means rung 1 is not yet secure.

**Rung 2 — written, shown to one assigned partner.** Assigned, not chosen. Chosen
partners reproduce the existing social order, and the students most at risk choose
nobody.
*Ready for rung 3 when* pairs talk about the answer rather than about something
else, and at least some students change or extend an answer after talking.

**Rung 3 — you read a wrong but productive answer aloud, unattributed, and use
it.** This is the rung that does the work, and the one no PD teaches. Take a
genuinely useful wrong answer from the stack — not a straw man, students detect
the difference immediately. Read it without a name. Then *use* it: build on it,
ask what it assumes, put it against a case. Do not correct it and move on. Never
signal whose it is, including by looking at them. Four or five instances change
what a wrong answer means in that room.
*Ready for rung 4 when* students volunteer answers you did not solicit, or a
student says a version of "that was mine" without distress.

**Rung 4 — a student owns an answer aloud, by invitation.** Privately arranged
where you can: "your answer about X is the one I want to start with — will you say
it?" It is available because she watched rung 3 and knows what will be done with
what she says.
*Ready for rung 5 when* more than a few students speak, disagreement happens
between students rather than through you, and a wrong answer aloud does not end
that student's participation for the period.

**Rung 5 — public simultaneous commitment.** The vote, with pair talk, sequential
hands, justification, re-vote. Section 7b. Last, not first: it asks for commitment
and exposure at once, and it works only where everything above has made the
exposure cheap.

**Descent.** Any of these resets. A new class, a new semester, a hard week, a
student who was humiliated somewhere else that day. Drop a rung without announcing
it.

[The gates are written as observations rather than as measures on purpose. You can
see every one of them from the front of the room in one period, which is the only
kind of criterion that gets used. The cost, stated so nobody has to discover it:
they are not research instruments and should not be presented as such.]

[Rung 3 resembles revoicing, and it resembles what Lampert does with student work.
The difference is that those treat it as a discourse move available whenever it is
useful, and this treats it as a scaffold with a position in a sequence and a
precondition for what comes after. Say that plainly if anyone asks, or someone who
knows revoicing will find you.]

### Visibility belongs to the move, not to the lesson

Each move tolerates a different amount of exposure, and the sequence already ramps
it — so visibility resets low at the start of every cycle while the ceiling rises
across the year. Operate at or below the ceiling, depending on where the room is.

| Move | Most exposure it will bear | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Critical Aspect question | Read aloud unattributed | A choice between named alternatives, both defensible |
| 2. Contrast Set | Read aloud unattributed, or a vote | The cheapest place to be wrong, and the most productive |
| 3. Build a Rule | A partner | She authored it; it is hers in a way a choice is not |
| 4. Pattern Break | Written and private | Her own rule fails here. Do not do that publicly |
| 5. 3-Tier Question | Written and private | The tier she picks reveals her self-assessment |
| 6. Continuity question | A partner, or aloud | No right answer exists, so wrongness is not at stake |
| 7. Stock-and-flow | A partner, or a group | Building a model is collaborative work |
| 8. Compensatory pair | A group | High complexity at low exposure |
| 9. Conflict case | A group | Same |
| What-if | Written and individual | It is your evidence |

Move 4 is the lowest for a reason: the student's own rule breaks there. If you
cold call, this table is what tells you where it is safe. Cold call at move 2 asks
a student to pick between two options with a reason for either. Cold call at move
4 asks her to watch her own rule break in front of thirty people. The move, not
the technique, is what makes it appropriate.

[Rejected, and recorded so it does not come back: a per-student readiness list. It
is real information, and it is also a written record that this student cannot
speak in front of the class. It follows her, it becomes a lens, and Cohen's status
finding is precisely that expectations become self-confirming. A list is an
expectation written down. Track by **move** instead — "the move 2 read-aloud is
working; nobody yet owns an answer at move 4" — which tells you what to do next
without classifying anyone. Individual knowledge stays where it already is: in
your head, and in a private invitation.]

### Group work

**Group work is a visibility reducer, not a rung.** A student speaks to three
people instead of thirty and the product carries the group's name. That is most of
why a class likes it, and it is not a bad reason.

The lever: group work carries higher complexity than the room's whole-class rung
allows. Moves 8 and 9 can run in groups early. You do not need rung 4 to ask a
hard question — you need rung 4 to ask it in front of everyone.

The trap: a group product says nothing about who coordinated. It can be complete
and correct with one student having done the relating and three having watched. So
group work is a good **occasion** for coordination and useless as **evidence** of
it, which is exactly why the what-if is individual and written.

Two things to watch, and to put in the teacher note if they apply:

- Cohen's status finding operates *inside* groups, not only across the class. The
  student who does not speak in class often does not speak in the group either,
  and the product conceals it. Rotating roles helps only if the role has real
  content.
- A class can enjoy group work indefinitely without moving up, because a group is
  a place to be quiet in. If group work is your main mode, rung 3 still has to
  happen separately, on individual written answers, or rung 4 never opens.

[A class that has taken to group work is telling you rung 2 is secure. They are
getting something from a partner's thinking, which is the precondition for rung 3.]

The written first answer with a rewrite is the low-visibility form of commitment.
It gets you the student's real thinking in rooms where a vote would produce
silence.

### The slide that carries this (added 2026-08-29)

The ladder above goes into the deck as **one slide, immediately after the teacher
note.** The note declares the rung this cycle is built for; that slide says what
the rungs are. Build it in that order and the declaration is read first and the
slide answers it. Build it the other way round — the ladder at slide 2, in front
of the note — and it is a glossary standing in front of a term nobody has met.

It is teacher-facing and **never projected.** Put the string `do not project` in
its kicker. That is not decoration: it is the string the linter and the grader
both test *before* they classify anything as a diagnostic slide, so it is what
keeps a teacher slide out of the count of slides students are scored on.

**What goes on it.** All five rungs, each with what you actually do and the
observable thing that opens the next one. Give rung 3 the most room — it is the
rung that does the work. Then descent, and the fact that group work is a
visibility reducer rather than a rung. Then the per-move ceilings, which is what
tells you where cold call is safe.

**One trap, and it will bite you.** The move names are also the marker strings
the teacher prompts match on, anywhere in the deck. Write "Pattern Break" plainly
on this slide and your deck now reports two Pattern Breaks where it has one.
Write the names so a reader sees no difference and a matcher sees no marker:
`Pattern-Break`, `Build-a-Rule`, `What-if`, and `Critical Aspect question` with no
colon after "aspect". Count the marker strings before and after you add the slide;
every count must be unchanged.

**It fits on one slide, but only just.** Arial 11pt in a single full-width column.
Measure the copy against the box before you ship it — at 12pt this text runs off
the bottom, and the check that finds that is a measurement, not a glance.

`scripts/deck_apply_changes.py` holds the copy and inserts the slide. Take it from
there rather than retyping it; the wording is the same on every deck in the arc.
Its absence is an advisory in `deck_lint.py`, never a hard failure.

## 8. Format

4:3. Arial. Defined palette. White background. Clone slide types from a reference
build rather than styling by hand — a cloned slide keeps its sizes, colors, and
positions, so a deck built this way cannot drift.

## 9. Before you teach it

- Every slide type has the student-facing wording review in §17.10; the saved
  wording is concise and appropriate without losing conditions or giving answers.
- Every example in a contrast set differs in exactly one dimension.
- The Critical Aspect question carries a difference in its own text — a
  comparison, named alternatives, or a stated change condition.
- Every VT comparison has useful case pictures showing the intended difference;
  missing pictures have been added and visually checked (§2).
- The Pattern Break actually strains the rule the Build a Rule slide produces.
- The fusion case cannot be answered from one aspect.
- The what-if is individual and written, and it names what changes and what is
  held.
- The Concept Bank asks the student to state how two terms connect. A page that
  only lists terms does not pass this.
- The teacher note names what breaks on substitution.
- The teacher note declares which simultaneity the cycle works on.
- The teacher note declares the visibility rung.
- Links resolve.

## 10. What this is for

Two aspects at once is the demand on a student. Content, this student, this
class, and the design at once is the demand on you, in about two seconds, several
times a period. The structure here is meant to be settled in advance so your
attention is free for the part no skill can specify.

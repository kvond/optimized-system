I want to begin with Lezir's question because of what it was not. It was not the thing I had been told, for my entire career, to build my teaching on. The field has a name for the thing it wants from students, inquiry, and it has spent decades telling teachers that good teaching begins there: find the child's question, follow the child's interest, let curiosity lead. I believe in inquiry. But I have come to think the advice, as it is usually given, is close to useless in a room like mine, and sometimes worse than useless, because it leaves out the one thing such a room most needs named. Inquiry has prerequisites.

Lezir did not walk into class on Tuesday wondering about Toxicofera and lost legs. He did not arrive with that question, and no amount of waiting would have drawn it out of him, because the question did not yet exist. It came into being over the course of being taught snake anatomy in a way that let him see what he was looking at and treated him as someone who could think about it. The inquiry was not the starting point of the lesson; it was its product. That reversal, inquiry as the consequence of teaching rather than its precondition, is the most important thing I have learned as a teacher, and it is what this chapter is finally about.

It matters most in schools like mine. The students who arrive already brimming with questions about the curriculum are, overwhelmingly, the students who were apprenticed into questioning somewhere else: at a kitchen table, in a museum, beside an adult who treated their noticing as worth something. To tell a teacher of students who were never given that apprenticeship to "start from the child's inquiry" is to tell her to start from something that is not in the room, and then to let her conclude, when it fails to appear, that the lack is in the children. It is not in the children. Inquiry has prerequisites, and the prerequisites can be built. The rest of this chapter is about how they are built.

## What disciplines pose

Before I describe how that building happens, I need to say something about why creating the conditions for it is so hard in the first place: not because students lack intelligence or curiosity, but because the disciplines themselves pose specific problems that conventional teaching makes worse rather than better.

Biology poses two such problems, and Christian Moore-Anderson, whose work on biology teaching introduced me to Variation Theory in practice, names them with unusual clarity.[1]

The first is that biology is not linear. It spans three fundamentally different domains, the organism, ecology, and evolution, each with its own logic, its own timescale, its own causal structure. A concept that makes sense at the level of the organism behaves completely differently at the level of an ecosystem or across evolutionary time. Students who do not yet see these as distinct domains will flatten everything into one undifferentiated mass of biological facts, and no amount of dialogue will help them think more precisely if they cannot yet locate which domain a question is even asking about. The student who answers a question about the function of an enzyme by describing what happens to a population is not confused about enzymes. They are confused about which domain the question lives in. That confusion is invisible to most assessments and central to most failures of understanding.

The second is that biology is causal and dynamic, but is almost always taught mechanistically. Students can memorize a labeled diagram of a feedback loop and still miss how a system regulates itself: how the parts interact, how the organism sustains itself over time in a way that a machine simply cannot. When a car needs gas, it does not go find some. An organism does. That difference, between a mechanism and a self-regulating system, is one of the most important things biology teaches. It is also one of the hardest things to convey through a diagram, because the diagram shows the parts but not the dynamic. Students see the representation and mistake it for the thing.

Both problems come down to the same underlying issue: students see the label but not the phenomenon, the symbol but not the relationship it encodes. They encounter the encryption problem the rest of this book has been describing, except that in biology the compression is not a jingle. It is a diagram, a definition, a labeled photograph. The form of the encryption changes from discipline to discipline. The problem is the same. And in science the missing substrate is not background knowledge in the generic sense; it is perceptual attunement, learning to see what varies, what matters, what is worth noticing. A child who has messed around with plants, watched things grow, noticed that some need more sun than others, has a foothold for photosynthesis. A child who hasn't has no spontaneous concept to scaffold.

This is why the perceptual work matters as much as it does. The students in front of me are perfectly capable of producing sentences that sound like they understand the material. What they are not yet able to do, what no amount of producing sentences will give them on its own, is to perceive the conceptual feature the sentences are pointing at. This chapter is about creating conditions under which perception of that feature becomes possible, and then about what happens between people once it is.

## Two registers around one held object

What happens in my classroom, when it works, operates in two registers at once, and the work depends on both. If the last chapter named how teachers *receive* student thinking, this one names how teachers make something *thinkable* in the first place.

The first register is a shared object held between us. The slide points at it, a contrast that varies one feature against a background that does not vary, but the slide is not the object. The slide is the vehicle. The object is the conceptual feature the slide is pointing at: the thing students need to come to perceive in order for the rest of the lesson to make sense. There is something specific being held in the room, and the fact that we are all looking at the same thing is what makes the rest of what happens possible.

The second register is what happens between people around that object. This is Lezir asking what he asked, and my response to him while the rest of the class continues to work the slide. It is improvisational. It treats the student as a knower whose thinking matters in its own right, not as a person who needs to be steered toward a destination they have not yet reached. It happens in real time, in the seconds before the moment closes, in the texture of an ongoing class.

Neither register is sufficient on its own. The shared object without the dialogic register is a lecture organized around a clever contrast: students may perceive what the lesson is pointing at, but the room is still flat, the thinking that emerges is still private, and the student whose mind is moving in a direction the curriculum did not predict has nowhere for that movement to go. The dialogic register without the shared object is what most attempts at dialogic teaching feel like when they fail: a room full of student voices working around a concept none of them can yet see. The two registers are not parallel tracks. They are the two halves of the same structure, and the structure is older than either of them.

## The older scene

The structure is older than the classroom. It is the scene the Germantown library work was actually showing. A four-year-old on an adult's lap, a book between them, the adult's finger pointing at something on the page, the child looking at what the finger points at, the adult naming it, the child asking a question, the adult answering, the child connecting it to something from outside the room. The child whose perception sharpens over the course of that exchange is not the child who has been exposed to the most books. It is the child whose adult got close enough to share an object of attention and stayed in dialogue around it long enough for the perception to develop.

In that scene the shared object is not the book. The book is the vehicle. The shared object is whatever the adult and child are looking at together at any given moment: a picture, a word, the way a character's face has changed across two illustrations. The adult chooses the book and points; the adult is not waiting for the child to set the curriculum of the read-aloud. But the adult is also not pouring information into a vessel. The child's noticing enters the read-aloud and changes it. The book stays the book. The reading is theirs.

This is what I am trying to do in a classroom. I cannot put thirty students on my lap, and I cannot make the page small enough for all thirty to see what my finger is pointing at. But I can do something structurally equivalent. I can choose the material. I can design conditions under which a specific conceptual feature becomes perceivable to all of them at once: a slide that holds a contrast still long enough for them to see it, a sim that lets them put a hand on a variable, an artifact that puts the critical aspect under their fingers. And then, inside that shared attention, I can stay close enough to specific students to receive what they are noticing, and to let what they notice enter the room as something the room now knows.

## What the shared object actually is

I want to be careful here, because a lot of the confusion in writing on dialogic teaching collapses at this point.

The shared object is not the slide. It is not the sim, the lab bench, or the artifact. Those are vehicles. The shared object is the conceptual feature the vehicle is pointing at: in Variation Theory's terms, the critical aspect of the lesson, the one feature whose discernment is what the lesson is for. It is what we are jointly attending to when we are jointly attending. It is what makes the moment a shared one rather than thirty private ones.

This matters because a lesson can have one shared object running across many different vehicles. The critical aspect that the dark reactions of photosynthesis depend on products from the light reactions can be made perceivable through a slide that contrasts a green plant with an Indian pipe, through a sim that lets students turn the light on and off and watch the downstream chemistry stall, through an activity that has them sketch the dependency in their own arrows, through a what-if question spoken aloud in the last five minutes. The critical aspect is the same in all four. The vehicles differ. The room is sharing attention to the aspect, not to the vehicle.

Variation Theory's contribution, in its strongest form, is a grammar for designing conditions under which a specific conceptual feature becomes perceivable: through contrast that varies only the critical aspect, through pattern breaks that interrupt the over-generalization the contrast will produce, through fusion that puts the parts back into the whole. The grammar is powerful and I use it. But the grammar belongs to the vehicle, not to the object. The object is the critical aspect itself. Designing the vehicle well is what makes the object perceivable. The object is what the room is for.

This grammar is not new, and I did not first get it from Variation Theory. I got it years earlier from Montessori, and recognizing the two as the same thing was part of what made Variation Theory feel like a homecoming rather than a discovery. A Montessori material is built to isolate a single quality. The pink tower varies one thing, size, while holding color, shape, and material constant, so that size is the only difference the child's hand and eye can find. The color tablets vary hue against an otherwise identical form. The sound cylinders vary sound and nothing else. This is exactly the logic Variation Theory names as variation against invariance: move the one critical aspect, hold everything else still, and the aspect becomes perceptible because it is the only thing changing. Montessori solved the problem of discernment through objects, physical things a child could pick up, on a mat, one at a time.

What I am trying to do is carry that same solution into content that cannot be held in the hand, and into a room of thirty students rather than one child on a rug. The contrast on the slide, the variable in the sim, the artifact pair on the counter: these are my attempt to do at scale, and for abstract ideas, what the pink tower does in the hand for size. I dwell on the lineage because it names the first thing any apprenticeship in thinking requires. Before a student can take a stance toward a concept, question it, or make it their own, the concept has to become perceptible: an object they can actually attend to rather than a word they are asked to repeat. The perceptible object comes first. Everything else is built on it, and it is the piece I return to at the end of this book.

One confusion is worth heading off, because Moore-Anderson is sharp about it: variation theory is not the activity called examples-and-nonexamples. A nonexample shows what an aspect isn't; variation varies the aspect itself, and only the second triggers the meaning-making of the aspect (*Teaching Meaning*). The two devices do different work, and this book uses both knowingly. Dysfunctional agency stands in my taxonomy as a non-example, marking a boundary; the classroom designs in this chapter vary critical aspects, building discernment. Boundaries and discernment are different gifts.

This is why a lesson can fail with a beautifully designed slide and succeed with a clumsy sketch on the board. The slide is not the object. If the critical aspect is not made perceivable, the slide has not done its work, no matter how elegant. If a clumsy sketch makes the critical aspect perceivable, the sketch has done what was needed. The judgment is not about producing the best vehicle. It is about producing whatever vehicle makes the critical aspect of this lesson perceivable to these students in this room on this day.

And it recasts a scene from Chapter 1. If John had been thinking in terms of critical aspects, he would have known that when he asked about the remainder, he had perceived a difference: two forms of math had come up. Nancy's arrangement did not answer the word problem, but it provided an interesting variation on one of its aspects, and her classmates' fairness question varied the remainder in another way, comparing the problem with and without a human context. Moore-Anderson puts the principle plainly: meaning cannot be transmitted, but there are ways of triggering meaning-making; we make meaning when we perceive a difference that makes a difference, so we must prompt students with differences (Moore-Anderson, *Teaching Meaning*). And, interestingly, students can raise those differences too. Nancy did.

## Choosing the critical aspects

If the critical aspect is the thing the whole lesson turns on, then the most consequential decision I make is which aspects those are, and there are never many of them. A lesson, in my experience, can carry two or three critical aspects that actually shape understanding. Not eight facts. Two or three discernments, each of which, if a student fails to make it, leaves everything downstream incoherent. The art is in finding them, and the way I find them is to ask where students reliably go wrong, because the critical aspects of a topic are usually the exact places where the discipline's compression hides a misconception.

Natural selection is my clearest example. Students arrive believing, almost universally, that it is a story about individuals improving: that a giraffe stretched for high leaves and its offspring inherited the longer neck, that animals adapt or learn within their lifetimes by trying, that the environment hands out the trait that is needed. None of that is selection. So the critical aspects of the unit are not the vocabulary list. They are the two or three discernments that dissolve that misconception:

- Natural selection works through death and reproduction, through who survives to reproduce, and how much, not through who tries hardest, learns, or improves. The mechanism is differential survival and reproduction, not individual striving.
- It is the population that changes across generations, not the individual within a lifetime. No giraffe grew a longer neck. The proportion of longer-necked giraffes in the population shifted, because the shorter-necked ones left fewer offspring.
- The variation was already there before it was needed. Selection sorts existing variation; it does not manufacture the helpful trait on demand.

A student who discerns those has understood natural selection, even if their vocabulary is still rough. A student who has memorized the vocabulary but not discerned them has understood nothing, and will reconstruct the giraffe-stretching story the moment the test is over. That is what I mean by a critical aspect that shapes understanding: not a fact to be covered, but a discernment the rest of the topic depends on.

And then, this is the part that makes the principle a practice rather than a slogan, each critical aspect gets tied to an activity built specifically for discerning that aspect, and built, where I can manage it, as something students play around with rather than something they are told. The aspects above are abstract; the messing-around is what makes them perceptible. For death-and-reproduction-not-striving, I want an activity students run rather than read: colored prey on a colored background, a predator that takes the ones it can see, the survivors reproducing into the next round, so they watch the population's color shift across generations. The thing they cannot help noticing, because it is the only thing the activity lets vary, is that no individual prey ever changed: the population changed, and it changed through who died and who reproduced. The misconception has nowhere to hide, because the activity was designed to isolate exactly the aspect the misconception gets wrong. A different aspect would call for a different activity. The activity is not decoration on the lesson, and it is not there to produce output. It is the vehicle for discerning one specific critical aspect, and it earns its place in the period only if it does that.

This is the discipline that makes "two things per class," later in this chapter, possible. If a lesson has two or three critical aspects and each has one activity built to make it perceivable, the period is full, and full of the right things. Everything else, the extra vocabulary, the supplementary worksheet, the coverage the pacing guide wants, is what gets cut, because it is not load-bearing and the discernments are.

## Ito's phases and the same underlying arc

What struck me, as I worked with Variation Theory and the dialogic register together over a couple of years, was that Mizuko Ito's phases, lurking, messing around, geeking out, describe the same underlying arc that Variation Theory describes, from a different vantage point. One view is the teacher's. The other is the learner's. Both are looking at the same thing.

Lurking corresponds to the whole. Students are present but not yet able to make sense of what they are seeing. They cannot yet differentiate the parts or locate the critical aspect. The whole gives them somewhere to be before they can understand. Messing around corresponds to contrast and separation. Students begin to poke at differences, to notice that this plant does not do what that plant does, to feel the limits of their initial generalization. Geeking out corresponds to fusion. The vocabulary starts to stick; students begin forming and refining concepts; the whole becomes something they can read, use, and explain to each other.

One thing this mapping makes visible is why teacher questions so often fall flat during the messing-around phase. Students hesitate not because they have nothing to say, but because they cannot yet confidently locate the object of the question: they have not had enough contact with the material to know which aspect of it the question is pointing at. A question asked too early, before the aspect has been located, asks the student to be precise about something they cannot yet see. They have no way to answer except by guessing what the teacher wants, which is pseudo-agency by another name.

Seen this way, Variation Theory is not a delivery mechanism for content. It is a design for how to move students through the phases: not by engineering enthusiasm, but by structuring what varies so that discernment becomes possible at each stage. The teacher controls what varies. Students do the seeing. And as students begin to geek out, something else starts to happen without being engineered: they explain to each other.

In fact, we are often anti-engagement precisely by skipping this structure. We demand fusion-level behavior from students who are still in the whole. We ask for precise language from students who have not had the contrast that would make precision possible. We force lurkers to talk and we block messing around, and then we wonder why the room goes quiet. The student with their head down has often been mislabeled disengaged when they were lurking, doing the legitimate early work of orientation in a domain they had not yet entered.

## Perceptual tripping points and other question types

Inside the dialogic register there are specific kinds of moves I have come to rely on, which I want to make explicit because they are part of how the shared object gets pointed at.

One is the perceptual tripping point: a place in the discipline where the conventional naming will mislead any student who is paying attention. Veins and arteries are the clean case. Students learn that veins carry deoxygenated blood and arteries carry oxygenated blood. Then they meet the pulmonary vein, which carries oxygenated blood, and the pulmonary artery, which carries deoxygenated blood. The rule collapses. The rule was wrong. Veins and arteries are named for direction relative to the heart, not for oxygen. A lesson that holds the pulmonary case beside the standard case and asks students to notice what has changed does something a definition cannot: it lets the student catch the trick of the language before the language traps them. The student who has been allowed to catch this once is, in a small but real way, no longer a person who memorizes biology. They have begun to notice that the names in the discipline are doing work the names do not announce.

Another is the perceptual reframe: a question that holds a familiar concept against an unfamiliar case and asks whether the concept still fits. Is an Indian pipe a plant? Is a virus alive? Is fermentation a kind of respiration? These are not gotcha questions. They force the student to articulate what the concept actually means by testing whether it holds at its edge. The answer matters less than the articulation. A student who has worked out, in their own sentence, why we still call an Indian pipe a plant has done conceptual work a student who memorized the definition has not.

A third is the prediction-under-perturbation question: the what-if. Will the rate of photosynthesis increase, decrease, or stay the same if we double the CO₂ and halve the light? These have no answer to copy. They require the student to commit to a position and defend it from inside their working model of the system. They are the moves most open to the dialogic register, because the student's reasoning is on the table. There is no inflection in my voice that signals *warmer*. There is only the student's own reasoning, committed to and defended.

My participation rates at the what-if stage are still low. Students are not yet confident enough to commit to a position in public, especially when there is no right answer to hide behind. But I have stopped reading that silence as disengagement. It is, more often, the silence of real thinking: of students working out what they believe before risking it. That working-out is exactly what I want. The stance comes first in private; with time, and a classroom culture that receives it safely, it begins to surface in public.

None of these question types are mine in any original sense. What is mine is the particular combination and the calibration of each to the students in front of me. Discern a feature. Test a concept at its edge. Reason about a system under perturbation. The intellectual work has the same shape whatever the level; what changes is the assumed background and the language of the prompt, not the cognitive demand.

## The dialogic register, in more detail

What the vehicle cannot do is hear Lezir.

The vehicle can hold the room. It cannot answer his specific question, cannot tell him that his hypothesis about how snakes might have lost their legs is closer to something true than he knows, and cannot register that his question came from his own attempt to make sense of what he was seeing: that it was an epistemic move, not a comprehension failure, not a tangent to be redirected. Only a person can do that, in real time, in the seconds before the moment closes.

The dialogic register is what I run alongside the shared object. I crouch beside students. I take their questions seriously. I tell them when they are close to something true, and when their reasoning has implications they have not yet seen. I let their contributions enter the room as contributions, not as deviations. I do this in the texture of an ongoing class, while the rest of the room continues to work with the shared object. I am not running two lessons in parallel. I am running one classroom in two registers, and the registers are answerable to each other: the shared object gives the dialogic register something specific to be a dialogue about, and the dialogic register gives the shared object the human reception without which a concept stays cold.

There is a reason this pairing is hard for pseudo-agency to survive. You can fake understanding of a worksheet. You cannot fake understanding of a blood spatter pattern when you are trying to make the evidence cohere: the material talks back. A held object resists performance, provided the teacher holds it lightly enough that a student's partial understanding can actually change where the conversation goes. If I already know the answer and am only waiting for someone to arrive at it, students will perform arrival. The object has to be actually open.

Over time, the dialogic register also builds something the shared object cannot build by itself: a knowledge base specific to this room. Lezir's question about Toxicofera and lost legs becomes the moment our class learned that snake limbs are not gone from the genome: they are silenced, and the silencing is itself a thing biology can study. Another student's question about whether snake venom is "really" digestive enzymes becomes the moment our class learned that the categories of digestion and predation share more chemistry than they advertise. A student's offhand mention of her grandmother's garden becomes the example we use for a week when we talk about pollination. Rabbit trails, when I can afford them, provide authorial outlets. These accumulate. By the middle of the year the class has its own corpus: cases, questions, and small disagreements no other classroom will have, because no other classroom contains these students. The knowledge is theirs, partly, because they made it.

## Knower stance — and why inquiry comes last

There is a strand of dialogic pedagogy, running through Freire, and radicalized in Matusov's authorial teaching, that holds real dialogue requires the student's own inquiry to lead. Freire's distinction between banking education, in which the teacher deposits knowledge into students treated as containers, and problem-posing education, in which students are co-investigators, is the version most teachers know. Matusov sharpens it: a dialogue whose endpoint is known in advance, he argues, is at best manipulation toward that endpoint. Aukerman's shared evaluation pedagogy applies the same instinct: a teacher who refuses to evaluate, even when a struggling reader reads the text "wrong," lets the student begin to take up a position as a knower rather than as the receiver of correct answers.[2]

I take from this strand the seriousness about students as knowers, and the refusal of banking education. I do not take the conclusion that the curriculum has to come from the student, or that having an intended object of learning corrupts the dialogue. I bring the conceptual material. I curate the shared object. I have decided, before class begins, what critical aspect this lesson is going to make perceivable. But I refuse the banking position by treating every student as a person whose thinking about that material is real intellectual work: whose partial understandings are partial understandings *of something*, whose divergent answers are evidence of what they are noticing, whose questions enter the room as contributions to what the room now knows. The endpoint of the dialogue is not the answer in my head. It is a student whose own thinking about the concept has developed further than when they walked in, including in directions I did not predict.

Here is the move I think the existing debate has not named, and it is the one I most want to put down plainly, because it is the thing I have been most frustrated by as a teacher. The field treats student inquiry as either a precondition for real learning, the progressive position, follow the child's interest, or an absence to be remedied, the traditional position, motivate them into it. Both are wrong in the same way. Both assume inquiry is something a student either brings or lacks. Neither asks where inquiry comes from. What I have come to see in my own classroom is that the right teaching generates inquiry. Lezir did not walk in wondering about Toxicofera; he developed that question over the course of being taught snake anatomy in a way that treated him as someone who could think about what he was seeing. The inquiry is not where the work starts. The inquiry is what the work produces.

This is not an abstract correction. It is the difference between a pedagogy that can serve my students and one that cannot. If inquiry is a precondition, then the students who don't arrive with it are out of luck, and the teacher is reduced to waiting for a question that will never come, or, worse, to deciding that the children who do not produce questions are not curious. If inquiry is a consequence, then it has prerequisites a teacher can actually build: a perceptible object, a stance that receives the student as a knower, time in the early phases without punishment. Build those, and the questions come: not because you found the right hook, but because thinking, once it has something to work on and somewhere to go, produces questions on its own. That is what inquiry is. It is not the fuel you need to start the engine. It is the exhaust of an engine that is running.

## The four-stage progression

What skill development actually looks like in my classroom, once the perceptual conditions are in place, is a four-stage progression. It does not happen all at once, and trying to rush it defeats the purpose. Each stage has to be fully inhabited before the next is worth attempting, and reading which stage a student is in is part of the perceptual work the rest of this book has been building toward.

The first stage is conceptualizing. Students put the content in their own words, in writing, without being given an answer to copy or a blank to fill. The slides are their reference. What they need is to make sense of what the slides are pointing at. I am watching not for accuracy but for whether students have a working model at all: whether they can produce a sentence that shows real contact with the concept. I calibrate by feel: after a few slides of lurking, do I start to see enough confidence to risk an answer out loud? When answers become more thought-out and less random, we move. When they don't, I don't push. Testing students before they have conceptual footing does not measure understanding. It confirms its absence, which they already know. And the writing comes before conversation on purpose. Conversation is how a teacher learns what meanings students have actually made (Moore-Anderson, *Teaching Meaning*), but students not yet confident enough to converse need a private channel first. The writing is the conversation's on-ramp; later, pulled into class discussion, it becomes shared meaning.

The second stage is accuracy and rephrasing. Once students have a working model, however rough, we refine it. This is where vocabulary starts to become theirs rather than the slide's. I return to their conceptualizations and ask them to tighten the language, to use the discipline's terms more precisely, to say the same thing in a way that would make sense to someone who had not seen the slides. For a while my method felt empty without this: a page to wrap up vocabulary and concepts in their own words, moving from their ideas and conceptions to knowing the words that build the skill, so that when they revise, they begin *from* the skill. It is not too tight and not too loosey-goosey; it harkens back to direct teaching and the student as knower at once. This is not correction in the traditional sense; it is closer to translation work, moving from their intuitive grasp toward the precise language that lets them think with it more powerfully. The goal is that the biological term stops being a label to memorize and becomes a tool they reach for because it does something their everyday language cannot.

The third stage is peer discussion and collaborative sense-making. This is where geeking out becomes social. Students who have reached this stage begin to behave like a study group: not reciting answers at each other but debating, pushing back, asking *but what about this case?* I have found it powerful to have students work through assessments collaboratively at this stage. It is another pass at articulating concepts, and you can hear who is beginning to geek out by listening for the student who is teaching.

The fourth stage is formal assessment, but assessed in a way that does not defeat students before they begin. Many of my students have IEPs. Testing that is inaccessible in its language or overwhelming in its length does not measure biological understanding; it measures tolerance for confusion. I differentiate not by lowering the conceptual bar but by removing the linguistic obstacles: accessible phrasing, manageable length, questions calibrated to what I have actually seen students engage with. I watch for the threshold where the task tips from challenging into defeating. Too hard and they stop; too easy and they are bored. The window is narrow and it moves, and reading it is the perceptual work this book has been building toward.

What makes the progression cohere, and keeps it from being a ladder of hoops, is that each stage is itself an act of becoming more skilled, and the student is positioned as a knower throughout. The first stage does not say: you are not yet a knower because you cannot produce a polished sentence. It says: you are a knower whose first attempt is what I want to see. The second does not say your first attempt was deficient; it says it was real, and now let's give it a sharper edge. The progression is the skill development, made structural.

## The skull station

In my biology classroom there are a few objects left behind by previous teachers and a few I have laminated and arranged myself: a set of evolutionary skulls along one counter, and a timeline of life laminated from a Montessori-style production, alongside a simpler version with blanks. I have set up that counter the way a Montessori classroom sets up a shelf, the full information visible alongside the blank version, the contrast built directly into the materials rather than added by a lesson script. It is the Montessori logic again, made into furniture. During the unit on evolution, when something on the slides triggers a connection to one of those objects, I invite students over individually to come look. It is not a hard sell. The skulls are simply interesting; they read as cool before they read as biology.

Once a student is at the counter, I ask what I have come to call "off the top of my head" questions. Look at those teeth: what do you think they were for? Why do you think this head is so big? What's in there making it that big? These are not questions I have an answer key to. They are the questions I would ask if I were the one wondering, which I often am. The student can ask back, and I can notice things, and they can notice things, and the back-and-forth gets going. Pretty soon the student who walked over to look at a skull is doing the thing: engaging with the material as a knower, sometimes saying something they would not have risked in front of the class.

What the skull station offers, materially, is contrast, the full Montessori card next to the blank, which is the form Variation Theory describes, except the contrast is built into the artifact pair itself. The student can move their eyes between the two versions and feel the structure of what they are looking at. The shared object is right there on the counter, and the dialogic register comes from me: the "off the top of my head" questions that treat the student as someone whose thinking is worth standing at the counter with.

The skull station also offers the form of differentiation I have come to think actually matters, which I would call respect-based rather than easier/harder. The standard differentiation language is calibrated by perceived ability and produces visibly different tasks for visibly different students, which telegraphs to everyone in the room which students have been judged less capable. Respect-based differentiation asks a different question: what does this student need in order to be respected as a knower right now? For a student who would be overwhelmed by too much at once, the simpler version of the timeline is a way in: not less, but the version that lets them be a knower in this moment. For the smart, turned-off student who has been bored and insulted by school for years, the harder version, lining up the time periods against the characteristics and pictures, working it out from the timeline text, is a way of saying I see what you are capable of and I am not pretending otherwise. For students already geeking out, I sometimes offer what looks like a task that helps the classroom, organizing or making materials, but is really a way of meeting the level they are working at. I tell them: you can say no. You can stop when you want. The agency stays theirs.

Jair, this semester, is a version of this differentiation in negative. He is intelligent and earns good grades, but when the tasks are not complex he is done early and spends the period joking with friends, because there is nothing in the room that has yet met him. I told him, mildly but plainly, that he has learned he is smart and gets stuff done but does not challenge himself, that my classroom does make challenges available, and that I expect him to take them. His friend Christian, meanwhile, had been off on his own examining the tree-of-life simulation I put up at the start of class and got into the evolutionary-periods task cards on his own. Today I put him on the cladogram activity, the hardest thing I have available, and he is doing it. The difference between Jair's response and Christian's, on the same day in the same room, is the difference between a student who has not yet been given a reason to take the harder task and a student who has and is taking it. Much of teaching is making the harder thing available without forcing it, and reading which student is ready to reach for it on which day.

What happens in the conversations at the counter, and this is the part that matters most for the perceptual argument, is that students surface partial knowledge they would not risk in front of the full class. A student offers an idea they are not sure about, half-finished, sometimes wrong in a way more interesting than being right. They sometimes propose an epistemic revision I had not thought of, and I am surprised, and I tell them so. The look that crosses their face then, the one I have come to think of as the "got me" look, is one of the most consequential things that happens in my teaching. It is the look of someone who has just found out that what they think actually matters to the person they are thinking with. That look is what the rest of the perceptual vocabulary of this book has been for.

The skull station is not novel as a pedagogical idea; it can sound like a thousand constructivist setups proposed across the past century. What is worth being careful about is the institutional condition under which it can actually happen in a public Title I classroom. The reason it works is that I am free to be at the counter: not preparing tomorrow's lesson, not formatting feedback, not writing IEP-accessible versions of an assessment in that moment. How I came to have that freedom, what made the production work of teaching stop consuming the attention the presence requires, is the subject of the next chapter, and I will hold it until then. Here I want only to mark what the freedom is for.

Because this is the equity argument the rest of this book has been circling, and I want to state it directly. What the skull station is, the full card next to the blank, the individually invited student, the "off the top of my head" questions, the respect-based differentiation, the side conversation where partial knowledge can surface, the protected option to say no, is exactly what expensive private schools with a tutorial, Socratic orientation have always provided. It is what progressive schools have offered by following individual children's interests. It is what Montessori classrooms build into the materials themselves. The wealthy and the privately educated have had access to this kind of teaching for as long as the model has existed. What I am claiming, on the basis of what I now see in my own classroom, is that these things can be delivered in a public school that serves high-poverty students. Intellectual apprenticeship is not a privilege some students bring and others lack; it is a form of access a school can either reproduce or withhold. The barrier was never that teachers in Title I classrooms did not know how to teach this way. The barrier was that the institutional conditions did not give them the time to be present in this way.

I want to be careful not to overclaim. Pedagogy cannot correct structural problems. It can mitigate them, a teacher with the right tools and conditions can do a great deal, but the structural problems are real and require structural responses. The Germantown study documented this in one direction; Alabama recently illustrated it in another, where a coordinated, sustained, accountable state effort, targeted investment in early-grade reading and math, teachers retrained in research-based instruction, dedicated coaches in schools, more support steered to the schools that struggled most, produced measurable change at scale: on the 2024 NAEP, Alabama posted the nation's largest gain in fourth-grade math and was one of only a handful of states to recover to pre-pandemic levels (PARCA, 2025; Alabama State Department of Education, 2025). [VERIFY: Alabama citations — the verify-list in the back matter covers these.] Pedagogy is necessary and not sufficient. The work I describe here cannot replace changing how schools are organized, funded, staffed, and held accountable. It can mitigate where the structures are not yet changed, and it can show, in the meantime, that the human conditions our most under-resourced students need are not impossible to deliver. They have only been impossibly expensive.

## Vehicles: the sim, the activity, the lab

Most of the rest of what I do, sims, activities, labs, is best understood as the choice of a vehicle for making a specific critical aspect perceivable. The question for each is the same: does this vehicle make the critical aspect of this lesson perceivable to these students, and is it the best available vehicle for that purpose?

The sim. Some of my best lessons now turn on something students can play with for ten minutes: a simulation, usually digital, that lets them manipulate one variable that matches the critical aspect. They run it, see what happens, change one thing, see what happens, make a guess and test it. What makes the sim work is not that it is interactive or digital. It is that the variable they manipulate is the critical aspect itself. If the critical aspect is that the dark reactions require ATP and NADPH from the light reactions, the sim must let the student turn the light off and watch the downstream chemistry stall. They cannot help noticing what the variation makes visible. They are not being asked to memorize a dependency; they are given an object that lets them feel it. The sim does for the lesson what messing around does for any domain learned voluntarily: it lets them poke at something until they have a feel for it.

Activities for noticing. Most of the activities I design are not for output. They are for noticing. I want students to handle something, notice something about it, and have a place to record what they noticed. The activity exists to put the critical aspect under their fingers and in front of their eyes. The output, if any, is a small note about what changed, what surprised them, what they have a question about. Drawing, when it serves the noticing, is a legitimate form. Slide annotation, letting them mark and write directly on what they are seeing, works because the thinking happens in the same place as the content. A worksheet fits meaning to a form the student has not made. A noticing activity gives the student something to make meaning of.

Labs done soberly. Labs cost time, setup, and class periods. I do them when the lab is the right way to teach the critical aspect: not because the curriculum prescribed a lab this week, and not because the lab is the photogenic part of biology. The question I ask before scheduling one is whether the embodied nature of the lab actually matters for what I want students to discern. If yes, it is worth the time. If no, the lab is theater that takes time from the real lesson, and I have learned to refuse the theater. This is the most boring claim in the chapter and the one most contested in practice. Schools love labs. But labs are pedagogically expensive, and many do far less work than the time they consume can justify. I used to treat labs like they were being prepared for FDA approval: serious steps, fine-print directions. Now I am messier. Directions are scant. If someone does it wrong, that is something to think about, not a failure to remediate. The lab is more honest when the student's noticing is what the lab is for, rather than adherence to a procedure.

## Two things per class

Once I understood what the perceptual conditions required, the design problem became one of simplification. Most curricula attempt too many critical aspects per lesson. Most teachers, including me when I started, used to do four or six things in a class period, because we have been trained that more coverage is more learning, and because we are anxious about looking like we are doing too little. The result is a class in which several concepts get mentioned and none get learned to discernment.

I decided to do two things per class. Not four. Not six. Two. A lesson built around a critical aspect or two, where students had to actually think: not copy, not guess from context. And an activity or lab focused on the same critical aspect, not on completing a procedure for its own sake. The rest, the elaborate dialogue scaffolds, the supplementary worksheets, went.

This sounds like a reduction of ambition. It is the opposite. Doing two things well takes more pedagogical work, not less. The two have to be the right two. The vehicle has to be designed to make the critical aspect perceivable. The thought question has to require thinking that cannot be retrieved. The cuts are pedagogical decisions, not laziness, and only the teacher can make them, because they require judgment about what these students in this room on this day actually need.

Remember Toma, from this book's opening pages: the student whose coldness had judged my classroom not worth his energy, until the afternoon I crouched beside his own uncopied sentences. That moment happened inside this structure, and it taught me what the structure was for. The framework was the right one. The entry point was what had needed adjusting: to simplify without reducing, the whole first, one critical aspect at a time, one contrast, a conceptualization in their own words, and to meet students where they could be successful and build from there. Why is that not the assumption for every classroom?

My slides carry a fair amount of text, to support students who were absent or are reviewing, but I scan each one and then talk freely. Reading the slides as written would be deadening. I highlight important words with my marker. I draw where it helps. Inserted question slides ask students to put the previous content in their own words. I ask for a thumbs up when most seem done. I do not go over the answer; that is critical. If I answer it, students just wait for me to. Instead: discuss your answer with a partner, go back and check the slides, or come check with me if you are still uncertain. When I grade their work and find a problem, I hunt them down to talk about it.

I do ask students to annotate the lesson slides as a means of accountability, but I do not evaluate their answers or make them speak aloud. The annotation is an approximation of lurking that serves institutional legibility, the system can see they engaged, but it also takes their hand and begins moving them from lurking toward messing around. They are touching the content with their pen before they are asked to touch it with their voice. By the time the dialogic register reaches them, they have already been in private contact with what we are looking at together.

Word banks instead of blanks when the vocabulary load is high. Slide annotation as a response space, so the thinking happens in the same place as the content. Grades heavy enough to matter; I am not going to pretend ninth-graders will engage purely for intrinsic reasons, and they need the grades to navigate the system. What I was not willing to simplify was the thinking itself. The thought questions do not have answers you can copy; they require students to apply a distinction they have just made to a new case, or to notice where a pattern breaks down. I work to make the questions hard because the thinking is hard, not because the syntax is hard; obscure phrasing creates a pseudo-rigor that tests familiarity with school language rather than grasp of the concept. And before students have the confidence to dialogue in public, they need confidence in their own thinking. I remind them constantly: I care about your thinking, not the answer. "Because" is not an answer. "I don't know" is not an answer. But anything that shows me your thinking is credit-worthy.

What I found, almost immediately, was that students found it doable. And doable turned out to be the condition for real thought.

Priya still talks. But she is also writing now: not notes, not copied answers, but actual sentences that show her working something out. Jaylen started thinking out loud, working something out and saying it to the room, not because I called on her but because she had something she wanted to test. Theo, who does not care about biology and says so freely, started leaning forward at the thought questions: not because he suddenly found DNA fascinating, but because the questions were puzzles, and Theo likes puzzles. Diego called me over to see if his sentences made sense. Because they had time to think during lessons, they began to verbalize more as a class, calling out answers even when they did not have to. They stopped being silent observers and started to participate. Not because I had found the right hook or relationship strategy. Because the lesson had created enough perceptual purchase for them to have something to say.

## Double booking

From the outside, this classroom still looks compliant. Objectives are visible. Vocabulary gets covered. The pacing guide would not immediately flag anything wrong. This is intentional.

I call it double booking, and I want to be precise, because it is a bit of a cynical phrase but an accurate one, and the concept originates with Matusov (2011).[3] Teachers who want to serve students honestly inside constrained systems often have no choice but to run two curricula at once: the one the institution can see, and the one that actually develops in students' minds. The first is the price of operating. The second is the reason to operate. What I produce for the system is a class that looks like vocabulary instruction, lab completion, standards coverage. What I am actually attending to is harder to see: whether students are beginning to experience themselves as thinkers.

And the pacing? I can more or less keep it now, because I demand less compliant paper-pushing and focus directly on students' thinking and the activities that support it. Doing two things well turns out to take less time than doing six things poorly.

Like the Germantown study showed in the library, proximity matters here too. I move among students during activities and labs: talking over aspects together, noticing their ideas, crouching beside someone who is stuck. Students feel less like objects of my teaching and more like workmates in a shared process. That is not a technique; it is a stance.

The work has moved from managing activities to noticing agency. My practices have become simpler: two things per class, real questions, thinking rewarded rather than correctness. What has become more complex is what I notice. I am watching differently now, listening for the beginning of interpretation, for the moment a student starts to revise what they thought, for the places where the room opens and the places where it closes down. Not equity as sameness. Equity as growing capacity.

## Where to start

If you teach, the practical version of this chapter is smaller than the theory, and you do not have to remake everything to begin.

Start where you are. Pick one unit, the next one you have to teach anyway. Before you plan a single activity, ask what the two or three critical aspects of that unit actually are: not the vocabulary, not the standards list, but the discernments the whole topic depends on, which are usually hiding wherever students reliably go wrong. Write each one as the misconception it corrects. For natural selection, mine are death-and-reproduction-not-individual-striving, population-not-individual, and variation-came-first. Yours will be different for your unit, but there will only be two or three, and naming them is most of the work.

Then, for each critical aspect, design one thing students can play around with that makes that exact aspect perceivable: a sim, a sort, a contrast they can manipulate, an artifact they can handle. The test for the activity is narrow: does it isolate the aspect, so that the thing the misconception gets wrong is the one thing the student cannot help noticing? If it does, it earns its place. If it is just an activity, engaging, hands-on, but not built to make a specific aspect perceivable, it is taking time the discernment needs.

Then write thought questions that cannot be answered by copying: questions that ask the student to apply the discernment to a new case, or to find where a pattern breaks. And then cut everything else in the unit that is not serving one of those two or three aspects. That is the hard part, and it is a pedagogical decision only you can make, because it requires knowing what these students in this room actually need.

That is the whole entry point: one unit, its two or three shaping aspects, one play-around activity for each, questions that require thinking, and the discipline to cut the rest. The framework is general; the place to begin is whatever you have to teach on Monday.

## Where inquiry comes from

I began with Lezir's question, and I want to end where it points. The reason the question matters is not that it was brilliant; it was an ordinary question, the kind a curious fifteen-year-old asks when he can finally see what he is looking at. It matters because of where it came from. It did not come from Lezir's prior interest, because he had none. It did not have to be motivated out of him. It was produced: by a perceptible object, a stance that received him as someone who could think, and enough time in the early phases that he was not punished for being where he was. Build those conditions and the questions come. That is the whole of it.

This is what I most want other teachers to take, because it is the thing I was never told and spent years failing without. We are told to teach from inquiry. We are not told that inquiry has prerequisites, or that those prerequisites are exactly what our most under-served students were never given, or that building them is the actual work. Inquiry is not the fuel you need to start. It is what a mind produces once it has something to work on and somewhere for the work to go. Everything in this chapter, the perceptible object, the two registers, the question types, the four stages, the skull station, the two-things discipline, is in service of that one reversal.

So the chapter lands on three things at once. Inquiry is not something students simply bring. Dialogue is not just talk. And the teacher's work is to construct a shared object and a shared practice of attention so that more students can enter the work as knowers; perception is where responsibility begins, not where it ends. One distinction is worth seating here because the chapters ahead depend on it: a student shaping the conditions *around* their learning, the pace, the format, the task, is exercising something adjacent to the agency this book is after; a student shaping meaning *within* the learning is exercising the thing itself. Both matter. Only the second is what the apprenticeship finally builds, and participation alone does not guarantee it.

There is a name for what all of it adds up to, which I take up fully later in the book: an apprenticeship in intellectual authorship, or more simply, an intellectual apprenticeship. The other names I have reached for, the knower-stance, the two-register design, the curated object, are not competing labels. They are the actions that make the apprenticeship happen. What the apprenticeship requires first, and what makes it possible at the scale a public classroom demands, is the subject of the chapter that follows.

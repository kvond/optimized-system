## Opening: two first graders

My grandson attended a private school built on exploration and play. In the same period, while accompanying university education majors to an inner-city after-school program, I watched a first grader work through a worksheet on cowboys: vocabulary like spurs and stirrups. I asked if he had ever seen a cowboy movie. He shook his head. Cowboy books? Cowboy museum? Cowboy costume? All no. He was bored as he worked at finishing the worksheet. My grandson, meanwhile, had listened to cowboy stories read aloud, visited a museum, seen a movie, and played cowboy, hands-on, at his school. By the time he was asked to fill out a worksheet, it was fun. The words were already his.

The difference between those two first graders isn't motivation. It isn't intelligence. One had adults and experiences that made the words his before the worksheet arrived; the other was handed the worksheet in place of all of it. Everything this chapter diagnoses is packed into that difference, because the second boy's school will go on handing him worksheets, reading his boredom as a motivation problem, and pacing him through a curriculum built for the first boy.

What makes these forms of agency chronic? What structural pressures in schools make this disconnection nearly inevitable rather than exceptional, and especially prevalent in high-poverty schools? (And yes, phones, pandemic, and poverty play a role; I am only addressing what teachers can change.)

The diagnosis that follows is not only about pseudo-agency. Dysfunctional agency is produced too: refusal and anger are often the rational residue of the same structural conditions, resistance to being treated as a container rather than a character defect (Freire). The chapter's claim is that shadow forms are manufactured by the system and surfaced in the student. I will keep naming structure without absolving or indicting individuals, myself included.

There is a structural contributor that gets far less attention. For two decades, the most celebrated response to underachievement in high-poverty schools has been the highly structured no-excuses model and its many imitations. I want to be careful with the word that usually attaches here, direct instruction, because the fault line in what follows is not directness. My own teaching is direct: I bring the conceptual material, I curate the object students will perceive, I decide before class what a lesson must make discernible, and I teach it explicitly. Direct, explicit instruction is compatible with everything this book argues for; it is part of the apprenticeship.

The fault line is whether the student's own thinking about the content is centered and received as real intellectual work, or merely transmitted and performed back. What I am naming is the second kind: directness without the student's thinking in it.

And directness earns its keep most where the learning is a skill. Mississippi's recent gains in early reading came from exactly this: a structured, explicit, direct approach to a skill so foundational that everything downstream of it depends on it [VERIFY: Mississippi NAEP reading gains; 2013 literacy legislation]. It is not so different from the Suzuki method I taught my children violin with, and some of them now teach with. A violin is hard to learn. It is a skill you cannot fake. And skills carry a real gap between the student's thinking and the outcome: no amount of a beginner's individual expression will make the instrument play in tune, so the teaching is direct, structured, and repetitive, and it is honest work, because everyone in the room knows what it is for. Then the skill arrives, and the student can improvise, play with artistic expression, collaborate with others. The apprenticeship completes by handing the instrument over.

The trouble I am naming is different: the model that never hands the instrument over. Directness as a permanent mode, with the student's thinking never invited in. And I want to be precise about its record, because the strongest versions of this model succeed at what they target. KIPP students graduate, behave, and enroll in college at higher rates than comparison peers [VERIFY: Mathematica — Demers et al., 2023; see the research list in the back matter]. But look at what the success is made of. Even KIPP, the strongest case, lifts college completion by bolting on intensive external support after graduation, KIPP Through College, rather than by having built self-direction in its students, and the absolute completion numbers stay modest. The far more common, less-resourced imitations produce the same training in compliance without the scaffolding that catches students afterward, so the students' downstream cost is worse, not better.

The mechanism is the point, and it is the reason this book studies agency rather than motivation. A model built this way can produce order, compliance, even test gains, while leaving entirely undeveloped the capacity to author one's own thinking: not because the instruction is teacher-led, but because the student's thinking is never treated as real intellectual work. That undeveloped capacity surfaces later, when the structure is removed. Sociologists who have watched these schools from the inside describe graduates trained to monitor themselves, defer to authority, and hold back their opinions; students who then arrive at college without the flexibility, initiative, and ease with adults that navigating it requires (Golann, 2021 [VERIFY]; Martínez & Golann, 2025 [VERIFY]). It is not that these students drop out in droves; the careful causal evidence does not say that, and I won't either. It is that the model is successful and not sufficient: it solves order and accountability, and it does not build the thing college, work, and a thinking life actually run on. [PLACEHOLDER: Katherine's observations from her doctoral students' projects in community colleges — mark clearly whether these are her students' findings or her own observations, so attribution is clean.]

If the pandemic and the phones cannot explain a pattern that predates and outruns them, and if a pedagogy designed to produce order can itself train pseudo-agency, then the right object of study is agency, and beneath it the capacity I will spend this book naming. Not motivation. That is the hook on which everything downstream of this chapter hangs. And let me plant now the distinction the teaching chapters will pay off: the remedy is not to remove the structure or the directness. It is to put the student's thinking inside them. You can teach directly and treat every student as a person whose thinking about the material is real intellectual work. That combination is the apprenticeship.[1]

## More than one driver

There is no single cause. Pseudo-agency is produced by a cluster of forces working in concert: a pacing logic calibrated to a student who does not actually live in most classrooms; a parallel pressure on the output side to advance students whether or not the learning has happened; a legibility regime that prefers what can be quickly counted over what is actually happening in students' minds; the Carnegie Unit and its downward pressure from college admissions through every grade beneath it; the three competing aims of public education that pull schools toward measurable deliverables; and the absence, for many students, of the apprenticeship in learning that makes the curriculum's tasks navigable in the first place. Pacing is the driver teachers feel most acutely every day, so I will start there. But it is the most visible symptom of a deeper structural arrangement.

And the vise does not wait for testing season. It clamps in August. The pacing guide arrives before any test is in view, before I have met a single student, before anyone could know what this group brings or needs, and from that moment the year's bind is set. The test only tightens, much later, what the guide already closed. Nor is this a biology problem, or mine alone: teachers across my building, in subjects far from science, describe the same frustration in nearly the same words. Whether this is a feature of my district alone, or a more general lapse between what administrators believe they must require and what teachers actually need in order to teach for understanding, I do not know, and I am not going to pretend to investigative reporting I haven't done. I can only say that the question deserves reflection in every district: who is the pacing guide for, and what would it take for the people who issue it to find out?

## Pacing, and what it assumes

We often talk about equity in schools in terms of equality: sameness. Same pacing, same assignments, same assessments for everyone. But sameness is not equity. It is equality of delivery of a system built for a certain kind of student. The standard curriculum is organized for students with certain kinds of cultural capital who arrive already fluent in learning, and then treats the gap between those students and high-poverty students as a learning problem, a discipline problem, or a home problem, rather than a systemic design problem.

The standard curriculum is not neutral; it is calibrated to a particular inheritance. Bourdieu called that inheritance cultural capital: the language, dispositions, and ease with institutions that some children acquire at home and others do not, which schools reward while presenting it as plain ability (Bourdieu, 1986). Lareau showed the mechanism up close: middle-class "concerted cultivation" trains children in exactly the habits schools expect, questioning adults, managing institutions, treating them as theirs, while working-class and poor families more often grant an unstructured independence that schools then misread as deficit (Lareau, 2003). Coleman's data set the weight of this inheritance in relief: family background predicted achievement more powerfully than school resources (Coleman, 1966). None of this is destiny. But it means a student who arrives "behind" is mostly standing at the distance between their cultural capital and the curriculum's hidden assumptions, not exhibiting a deficit. The legibility regime then misreads that distance in exactly the three ways this section opened with. I take legibility from James Scott, who described how states make populations governable by simplifying them into countable forms, and how much of real life that simplification cannot see (Scott, 1998).[2]

Let me be precise about what I am and am not claiming here, because cultural-capital talk slides easily into two errors. The first is making this about race. Nothing inherent in race determines school performance. Where race enters this book's mechanism, it enters twice: through economics, because poverty in this country is racialized, and through schooling's own white-centered habits of reading, which receive cultural differences in talk, humor, movement, and reference as off-task or defiant. That second entrance is not an exception to this book's argument. It is the argument: misreading is the machinery, and students of color are run through it at higher rates, which is what the discipline disparities record: the misreading made administrative [VERIFY: discipline-disparity source, e.g., Skiba et al.]. The second error is making this about class as such. Wealth buys margin, and it often buys mentorship, but it confers nothing by itself: wealth without intellectual mentorship fails too, and I have seen the proof up close. A couple of my own children attended a high-end private school on scholarship, and my daughter started its orchestra program there. I remember an administrator telling me that some of her wealthiest students were among her poorest performers: their parents were off on trips and projects, without steady support of their kids. All the margin money can buy, and no one lingering. The Germantown parent had no attention left after survival; these parents had attention to spare and spent it elsewhere. The absence is the same; only the bank balance differs. And remember Mike, who had the cultivation and on whom it didn't take on schedule. Poverty is not a culture. But it tends to create fewer of the experiences schooling silently requires, and shared experience is the ground meaning lands on: the more background a speaker and listener share, the more likely the intended meaning arrives (Moore-Anderson, *Teaching Meaning*). The mechanism runs on margin, not on the character of a race or a class. The variable is not who your parents are. It is whether the economics of a family's life leave margin for the apprenticeship, and whether anyone, at home or at school, supplies it. It's the economy, and it's the mentorship; most of everything else is a proxy.

Every pacing guide is built for an imagined student: one who brings sufficient background knowledge to the unit, sufficient academic vocabulary to access the text, sufficient apprenticeship in disciplined inquiry to do something with open-ended tasks, someone comfortable enough in their conceptual thinking to be willing to dialogue about it. That imagined student is not fictional. She exists even in my school. But in a high-poverty school, she is not the median.

The standard response to the mismatch between student and curriculum is differentiation: simplify the vocabulary, chunk the ideas, ask for less output. Reteach. Give students dialogue roles. These are real accommodations, and teachers do them in good faith. But they address the presentation of the problem while leaving its underbelly intact. Differentiation adjusts the readability of the material. It does not adjust the background the curriculum assumes students have: the background experiences, guided participation, and accumulated encounters with ideas that allow new concepts to attach to a growing network of ideas that is personally theirs. A student who has never encountered a concept outside of a worksheet cannot be scaffolded into understanding it through a simpler version of the same worksheet. What they need is something different in kind, not the same idea in simpler form.

When schools evaluate students primarily through what can be made institutionally legible (grades, pacing, test scores, compliance), they are measuring the fluency in learning students bring with them rather than the capacities they are developing. A student who arrives with background knowledge, and the ability to make sense of new learning, will be read as motivated, engaged, and academically able. Even students whose intellectual seriousness takes forms the school does not recognize, Angie and Nancy from Chapter 1, will be read as disengaged, resistant, or immature.

And the advantage being measured is not only academic. In one longitudinal study of urban high school science students, those who qualified for free or reduced-price lunch perceived their teachers as less supportive of their autonomy and more controlling than their more affluent classmates did (Patall et al., 2022). The students with the least cultural capital are not only handed a curriculum built for someone else; they experience the classroom itself as a more controlled place, which is close to the opposite of the conditions under which anyone learns to author their own thinking.

Consider the implicit norm: engage them → teach the lesson in the pacing guide → prove the lesson was taught. The teacher handles motivation. The curriculum handles content. The assessment handles accountability. The norm doesn't account for the actual pace at which understanding forms; for the fragmentation of real instructional time; for the difference between students performing school and students thinking. It doesn't ask what actually engages students, or how the system measures teachers and how that measurement drives what teachers can afford to notice. It is a system that looks for quick legibility.

When district administrators come through my room, they come to check what the system can see and count. They'll note whether I'm on pace, but they won't ask what I'm seeing in students' thinking that makes the pacing guide's sequence epistemically wrong for this group right now. Some unknown expert somewhere, at some time, decided that for me. They won't ask whether the order of the guide is even the order that makes sense for how understanding of a particular topic actually forms. They'll ask a student, "Why are you doing this activity?" and treat "I don't know" as evidence of poor teaching, rather than as evidence that students have spent years in classrooms where comprehension and purpose were never the point.

The system creates pseudo-agency. When students realize, year after year, that what counts is what can be displayed, many stop trying to understand and simply produce. This is not only my classroom's pattern. Researchers who study high-stakes accountability have documented the same narrowing at scale, and it falls hardest on the schools under the most pressure.[5] Then the same regime that creates this problem comes back and treats pseudo-agency as a student problem that the teacher should fix.

Chapter 2 named pseudo-agency as a system product, not a student failure; here is the machinery. I want to be careful with the word we usually reach for, which is unintentional. David Berliner calls the classroom version of this a rational response to high-stakes testing: teachers narrow what they teach not out of indifference or incompetence but because the incentives make narrowing the sensible move, with the harm to students entirely foreseeable (Berliner, 2011). That is a more uncomfortable thing than an accident. No one need intend the damage for everyone to be able to see it coming. Teachers become unwillingly complicit: pressured to cover curriculum, policed in their own heads by a standardization voice that lives there rent-free even when state tests aren't required. A teacher listening to a student's partial understanding must make the call, follow it or stay on pace. The pacing guide is the third presence in the room.

And notice what the policing does with the gap. It does not merely conceal a missing condition; it converts a missing enabling condition into an apparent intrinsic flaw, and relocates the absence onto a person: the student ("unmotivated") or the teacher ("poor teaching"). Mills called this turning public issues into private troubles.[3] The move functions this way whether or not anyone designs it, and it is the same relocation the school-models chapter traced onto teachers. The test to keep running: not every binary is false, but when the either/or is produced by a removable missing support, the person carrying the blame is carrying the structure's debt.

Students rewarded for pseudo-agency long enough begin to see themselves as doers rather than knowers. That is, those that don't become simply angry or disengage entirely. They stop asking what something means and start asking what the teacher wants. The cognitive growth that real effort is meant to build lies dormant: not because the students can't do it, but because the system stopped asking them to.

## Beneath the pacing: the legibility regime and the goals of public education

This legibility regime has a structure underneath it worth understanding, because the bureaucratic version of schooling didn't arrive from nowhere. It arrived from somewhere honestly humane, and understanding that makes it less evil but no less wrong. (Though colleagues more cynically suspect the system is structured to ensure that someone will deliver the pizza; in other words, that it is working as intended rather than malfunctioning in ways we can repair.) The problem is not the aspiration to provide a quality education for all, or even the emphasis on effort, which reflects something true about the world: much of adult life is effortful, not everything is inherently engaging, and effort gets you through the hard and boring parts. The danger is a relentless onslaught of hard and boring tasks without meaning, without discernment, without legitimate participation.

The problem is what happens when humane aspirations get translated into bureaucratic systems that demand legible evidence that can be managed, audited, compared, and defended. Under the pull of Labaree's three competing goals (Chapter 3), schools reach for measurable deliverables that can stand as proof the aims are being met: pacing as evidence of equitable coverage, standardized testing as a proxy for equitable learning, vocational pathways for social efficiency, grades to measure and to distinguish.

This logic is embedded in the basic credit structure of secondary schooling. The Carnegie Unit is designed to meet what colleges expect. It standardizes what counts as "enough" learning by tying it to hours and course labels, which then shapes what middle schools pace toward, which shapes elementary schools. A fixed list of course expectations stands in for actual intellectual development. Even progressive-school teachers feel pressured to translate deep learning into legible pacing and comparable outcomes, because the transition to credit-bearing high school courses is always looming.

## What this looked like in my classroom

My district's pacing guide seems, as best I can tell, an artifact of a textbook that no longer exists: a sequence of topics inherited from an organizational logic that predates current standards, loosely crosswalked to NGSS benchmarks, each of which would require, if taught honestly, far more time than the guide allows. What remains is not really a curriculum. It is a calendar with labels on it. On a given week in March: teach the structure of DNA. Teach "Breeding Corn" in February. What does that even mean? Science standards are listed loosely on the side, presumably to explain it all, while the teacher is expected to create the curriculum.

Besides its incomprehensibility, this pacing naively assumes that time is mapped as planned, without acknowledging the fire drill that ate twenty minutes on Tuesday, the five students absent for the lab, the ten minutes lost to a late bus, the student who went out of the country for a month. Real classrooms operate in fragments. Teachers stop revisiting partially understood ideas because returning "slows the lesson." They teach toward what can be recognized quickly as proof of learning, even though they know this undercuts actual conceptual understanding.

I know because I did it. I answered questions before students had time to think through them. I moved on, because moving on was what the calendar asked. I rewarded expected answers, answers that tended to be too simple, easily acknowledged as correct. I chose activities that made students look engaged but produced nothing in the way of deepening thought.

What I produced was legible. Students had notes. They turned things in. From the outside, the room looked like a working biology class. But most of my students were operating in pseudo-agency mode: some giving me words that sounded like biology without any contact with what those words meant; most sitting in silence. And I had, in my effort to keep up with the pacing guide and stay out of trouble, confirmed that they were right to do so.

I have been guilty as a teacher of scheduling too many things to get done within a class. If I couldn't keep the kids reliably interested, at least I could keep them reliably moving. That was my hope, which didn't actually happen with kids who were sick of the same strategy.

Interest builds when people think about ideas and discuss them with others, and think more deeply over time. That is a pretty reliable human way to build interest, for most kids most of the time. Which means I needed to slow down the flood of papers and activities meant to look like active learning was taking place, and give students time to really think with the lesson, and trust that human nature actually finds that interesting.

Reading the room for actual, not pseudo, agency means noticing not only when it is emerging, but also when one's own practice is suppressing it. That is a painful thing to see. It requires sitting with the possibility that what you are doing, even with good intentions, even with care, even after striving for good relationships, is actively narrowing students' capacity to think. Not because you are a bad teacher. Because the system has colonized you, and some of the compromises you are inevitably forced to make are more expensive for students than you permitted yourself to see. This is the teacher-side mirror of Marcus's "trying to save him" and the chalkboard "coercive teaching" episode: the strain the system produces gets charged to you as a personal failing unless you can name the structure.

And it is my belief, I could be wrong, that students want value from their education. If they cannot find any value in what they are learning and how they are being taught, they think school is pointless, and some become angry. When I was in high school, one of my teachers would get up in front of the room and read the textbook in a monotone. I remember having had enough of it, and pitching the textbook on the floor: "I can read this for myself!" And storming out of the room to guidance, daring them to fail to graduate me if I didn't finish that required course. To be fair, the teacher was going through a painful divorce. She was just trying to survive. I think this also because, as I work with students on their conceptual understanding, I see that many are less angry.

I am not writing this work for teachers to launch into heroic rebellion. I have a job to protect, and you probably do too. I am not economically free either. I am writing about what happened when I finally let myself see what my compliance was doing to my students, and decided I couldn't keep doing it, and tried to find a way to teach that at least allowed me to live with myself, and also, hopefully, better support students' learning.

## The classroom-level causes: how the daily exchange manufactures the same disengagement

The structural drivers are pacing, legibility, and passing. But the same disengagement is manufactured at a much smaller scale: in the ordinary, well-intentioned moves of a single exchange. None of these is villainous. They are what pacing pressure and legibility habits feel like from inside a teacher's minute-to-minute choices. I name them because I caught myself doing every one.

**a · Deflecting the student's own thought.** A student offers a partial, sideways, or not-yet-legible thought, and the teacher, needing to stay on pace or not recognizing the offer as a bid, redirects to the answer the lesson wanted. The student learns their thinking is not the currency of the room. This is the behavioral micro-version of the perception failure the whole book is about: the bid goes unreceived. The tell that separates deflection from legitimate redirection (because sometimes the thought really is off-track) is whether the student's origination was registered before being set aside. **b · Rewarding trivial, quickly legible answers.** Teachers accept, and often fish for, the easy, expected answer because it keeps the lesson moving and reads as participation. I rewarded expected answers: too simple, easily acknowledged as correct. This trains pseudo-agency directly, at the scale of a single question. Students learn the game is *say the obvious thing the teacher is waiting for*, not *think*. This is not an argument against checking for understanding or against right answers; it is about the displacement of thinking by the performance of a correct-sounding response. [VIGNETTE — yours.]

**c · No time to metabolize: copying versus concept-formation.** Students copy notes and transcribe slides instead of restating concepts in their own words, because no time is built in to metabolize. A concept put into the student's own language becomes part of their thinking; a copied note does not, and is far harder to retain. Memory holds concepts, not transcription. This is why coverage produces nothing durable: it is not just rushed, it is structurally non-encoding. [VERIFY, optional endnote: the generation effect and self-explanation effect literature.] The claim stands on what I watch happen; the research is support, not load-bearing.

**d · The analogy and word-use mismatch.** The textbook's analogies, register, and vocabulary are calibrated to the imagined, already-fluent student. The actual student's analogies and words for the same idea are different, and when only the text's framing is treated as legitimate, the student's own route to the concept is cut off. This is the language-policing mechanism from the school-models chapter operating at the level of concept access, and it is why (c) and (d) are the same coin: the student's words are the encoding path. This is not anti-vocabulary; disciplinary language is a real acquisition target. The point is sequence, own words first and disciplinary words built on them, not either/or. **e · The home-as-workplace reality.** We assume students will metabolize at home: read, rewrite, consolidate, the way I did in high school. But for many students home is a workplace: sports, jobs, younger siblings to mind, unstable or crowded housing, abrupt changes in household. The work we offload to "homework" presupposes a margin many students don't have: what is left of a teenager's attention after practice, shifts, and siblings is the same subtraction Germantown documents in their parents. So the metabolizing has to happen in class or it doesn't happen, which makes the time pacing eats an equity loss, not just a pedagogical one. I had sports, jobs, younger kids at home, and periods of abrupt housing change myself, and I still had the apprenticeship that let me do the home-metabolizing; I may see this partly because I lived a version of it. ## Passing without learning

Pacing presses on instruction from one side. From the other side comes a pressure that looks like its opposite but works toward the same end. Schools are held accountable for graduation and dropout rates, and they are right to worry about them, especially where those rates fall along lines of race and poverty. But the imperative to keep students moving through the system, course by course and credit by credit, can become, without anyone ever deciding it, an imperative to pass them whether or not the learning happened. Credit-recovery programs of uneven rigor, generous grading, the tacit understanding that holding too many students back will surface in the wrong column of a report: these are real forces in a high-poverty school, and they sit directly on top of the credit structure I just described. The Carnegie Unit defines what counts as enough; the pressure to advance ensures the unit gets awarded.

I want to be careful about the claim I am making, because the evidence here is more contested than it is for pacing. I am not saying that rising graduation rates are a fiction; researchers who have looked closely find that much of the increase reflects real gains rather than lowered bars (Harris, 2020). The claim I am making is narrower, and I think harder to dispute, because I watch it happen: whatever the aggregate numbers show, individual students learn, year after year, that passing is largely automatic. They learn that if they produce enough visible proof and tolerate the boredom, advancement will come. This is the lesson Priya had already absorbed, that turning in the paper was the point, and the passing logic confirms it for her at the scale of the whole institution.

A student who knew they'd pass no matter what; a credit-recovery situation; pressure to keep failure numbers down; a student who stopped trying because trying and not-trying led to the same place.]

Put the two pressures together and you have a vise, though the jaws are not pacing and passing. Pacing is what sets the vise in place. Once it has produced a disengaged student, the system has two habitual ways of coping with that student, and both avoid the one thing that would help. It passes him along, and it controls him. Passing waves him through; control bears down. Either way he is shuttled rather than addressed. A student held between those jaws learns something corrosive and entirely rational: that school is a sequence of producible proofs and tolerated boredom, not a place where his thinking is required. The credit is what you get for paying the boredom's price.

This is where I plant a flag for later in the book. Pacing does not only distort what gets taught; it consumes the time that building real understanding would take, the slow apprenticeship into noticing, questioning, and making meaning that the curriculum silently assumes students already had. When that apprenticeship is missing, and the time to build it has gone to coverage, the disengagement that follows is no mystery. Passing is the first of the system's two answers to that disengagement. The second, the disciplinary machinery that meets the bored and disengaged student with ever-tighter control, gets its own chapter later on: the other jaw. For now it is enough to see that both are ways of managing a student the system has failed to apprentice, and that neither is the apprenticeship itself.

## The apprenticeship gap: the Germantown study

As I said earlier, the pacing and standardization of the curriculum is organized for students who arrive already fluent in learning. Susan Neuman and Donna Celano's research on Philadelphia branch libraries, what I will refer to as the Germantown study, helps explain why this is also an equity problem, and why differentiation alone cannot solve it (Neuman & Celano, 2012).

A major foundation gift modernized dozens of neighborhood branch libraries across Philadelphia. The theory was simple and humane: improve access to books and inviting spaces, and you can level the playing field across income levels. Researchers tracked not only whether families used the libraries, because they did, but how they used them, and what kinds of participation the same resources actually produced over time.

In the middle-income branch, many children arrived with an adult who treated the library like it was personally owned. Book choosing wasn't random; it was coached. Adults helped children calibrate difficulty, too easy, too hard, then lingered long enough to find a book that fit. They asked librarians for recommendations. They narrated selection as a practice: This one looks right for you. This one will stretch you. Let's bring these home. The visits could be brief and purposeful, or slow and intimate: an adult and child together reading books, sharing thoughts, pointing at pictures, building sustained attention together.

In the low-income branch, the pattern was often different: not because children didn't want books or because caregivers didn't care, but because the attention caregivers could sustain with children varied. Very often parents came in and sought some down time. They had gotten the child to the library. That was the bandwidth they had left after working two or more jobs and contending with the constant effort it takes to survive while poor.

A four-year-old girl flips through board books in a bin and stops at a cover: *Do Not Open*. For a few seconds she is mesmerized, fascinated by the promise of what might be behind it. No one is nearby to read with her, to enter the story with her, to help her stay long enough for curiosity to become comprehension. Before long, she drops the book and scoots out of the area.

Learning isn't just about access. It isn't only about motivation. And it is not a failure of love. What decides the outcome is whether the child receives the small forms of guided participation that turn a moment of interest into engaged inquiry: Let's open it together. What do you think will happen? Let's read the first page and see. The proximal guidance that turns wandering into participation, and participation into learning, is not something parents in poverty are withholding.

I know what the other side of this looks like, because I lived it as a homeschooling parent. When we studied ancient Greece, we studied it at home and again in our co-op: projects, oral reports, children explaining what they had found to a room of adults and other kids who asked them questions. Ruth Beechick said it simply: she called it mind to mind [VERIFY: Beechick source]. That is the feel of an intellectual apprenticeship from the inside, and it is the counter-picture to the four-year-old alone with *Do Not Open*. Not wealth. Attention, dialogue, and somewhere for a child's finding-out to land.

I want to stop and name a trap directly, because this is the place in the argument where a careful reader should feel it. A developmental claim about poverty can slide, in careless hands, into a deficit story: poor children lack something, and school must supply what their homes failed to give. That is not the claim, and I want to refuse it in both of its forms. The deficit is not in the child. And it is not in the parents. What the Germantown study documents is not a failure of parental love or parental values; it is the arithmetic of exhaustion, what is left of an adult's attention after the constant effort it takes to survive while poor. Being a parent should mean being able to rely on the preschool and the school system to do its job. When the apprenticeship doesn't happen, that is a failure of the institutions charged with providing it, not of parenting. The deficit is in opportunity and apprenticeship the system owes: a deprivation of something withheld, never a deficiency in the student or the family. Nor am I describing two kinds of families; the same guidance appears and disappears with the margin available, in every income bracket, and the point is the guidance, not the population.

And the counter-evidence deserves to be said clearly, strongly, and with examples. Many high-poverty families and communities do apprentice their children into authorship, the attention, the back-and-forth, the adult who lingers, and have always done so. Deborah Meier built Central Park East on exactly that conviction, and its graduates vindicated her [VERIFY: Meier, The Power of Their Ideas, 1995]. [PLACEHOLDER: an example or two of high-poverty families or communities that apprentice their children well — yours to supply.] The capacity this book traces is unevenly distributed by conditions, and that is a claim about structures and opportunities, never about the worth or potential of any child.

My friend Mike is useful here for the opposite reason. He had the concerted cultivation Lareau describes, and it still didn't take at the expected time; academics were just not where he was going at that point in his life. Science kicked in for him much later; he now wants to be a physician assistant. Cultivation is opportunity, not guarantee. Development is not linear; interest has its own timetable; and between four and fourteen there is a whole streak of developmental action that no single input controls. Which cuts both ways: if concerted cultivation doesn't guarantee academic interest, then its absence doesn't fully explain disengagement either. The school's job is not to replay what Mike's parents provided. It is to keep the apprenticeship on offer so it is there when the student is ready.

What is missing in the Germantown picture has a name, and it is the book's central missing ingredient: a **dialogue partner**. The adult who lingers, the guided participation, the proximal guidance all name one thing, the social other who shares the child's attention and turns a flash of interest into understanding. It is deeper than scaffolding, and it is what both camps omit: the no-excuses model gives structure without a dialogue partner, and the autonomy model gives freedom without one. Thinking is dialogic before it is solo.

Private schools built on small, Socratic-style class sizes, along with progressive schools, Montessori schools, and some newer charter models, follow that participatory pathway. Unfortunately for working-class families, these schools are either out of reach or out of range.

The Germantown library offered children the freedom to explore, make autonomous choices, be hands-on with computers, and access a high-quality, diverse range of resources. The structural argument the study makes is that even with a large grant and computers in every branch, the differences it documents are produced by economic conditions that limit parental margin. It is the economic inequality families face that exhausts their energy. What the study shows, in microcosm, is exactly what many students in high-poverty classrooms lack: the consistent presence of an adult in close proximity, drawing attention to what matters, naming what they see, and inviting the child to linger. An apprenticeship in learning.

High-poverty schools, in the name of equity, are expected to fill this gap. They do not reliably provide the guided participation and experiential substrate that give concepts their meaning. Even autonomy and quality materials are insufficient without that participatory piece.

This is the gap the two first graders at the top of this chapter embody. The cowboy worksheet was the same task for both boys; what differed was everything that had happened before it: the stories read aloud, the museum, the movie, the hands-on play, the familiarity between teachers and students, the experiences that build background knowledge ready to gather more to itself. One boy's words were already his. The other boy was being asked to start from the worksheet, which is to say, from nothing.

On a recent district biology assessment emphasizing conceptual understanding, about 9% of students at our high-poverty public school reached proficiency, compared to roughly 60% at a wealthier charter school across town. If I had to point to one difference, I would suggest: wealthier students had friendly adults around who offered them wider, more varied experiences that let them lurk and mess around, both at home and in their wealthier schools, than our lower-income students did. Yet both schools pace the curriculum across the same calendar days. Who are they pacing it for?

## More hands-on, more exploration, more choice — not enough

The standard response to the lack of background experiences, give them more experiences, make it hands-on, let them explore, runs into the same limit the Germantown study exposes. Those children had access: books, computers, an inviting space, and the freedom to explore. And yet it wasn't enough: not because children lacked curiosity or caregivers didn't care, but because exploration without someone to translate the experience into meaning with them doesn't reliably become learning.

What students need is not simply more experiences. They need experiences worked into meaning through dialogue: through an adult who draws attention to what varies, names what matters, and creates the perceptual conditions under which a moment of noticing becomes a durable understanding. This is a direct challenge to the hands-on learning orthodoxy, which assumes the activity itself does the teaching, and to the autonomy-centric approaches that assume choosing empowers drive. Autonomy and activity are inert without the dialogue that targets the critical aspects, especially in the early years.

There is a Vygotskian point hiding in this that the teaching chapters will need. The zone of proximal development assumes the learner has some foothold, a spontaneous concept, a lived experience, a partial understanding, that the teacher can build from. When a student's participation history is thin in a domain, there may be no spontaneous concept to scaffold; the teacher isn't building from partial understanding but from absence. That makes the equity problem structural and pedagogical at once. Structural, because the pacing guide assumes the foothold exists. Pedagogical, because the teacher's job becomes not just scaffolding understanding but creating the experiential substrate that makes scaffolding possible.

This is what people with privilege miss in their conceptualization of what makes school interesting. They benefited from autonomy and hands-on activities because they had that early apprenticeship in learning that became the base for their ability to make use of such resources. If they were fortunate enough to also attend one of the private progressive schools, they experienced the continuity of that apprenticeship, so that by the time they got to high school, they were able to do the internal work of making something interesting for themselves, even if on the outside it wasn't.

My son Ben entered public middle school after years of homeschooling. His experience in the two contexts offered him a sharp contrast in observation. He initially saw that his classmates were grade levels below where they should be. What he thought held students back was, as he put it, "They're spoken at. But they're not spoken to." He saw there was no shared object, no back-and-forth, no checking on what students could make sense of, what meanings they found. The talk happened sometimes with, but more often without, the students' equal participation. Ben had experienced a different apprenticeship in learning.

## Autonomy without footing

The Germantown study also challenges the effectiveness of autonomy alone. There is a counterintuitive finding in Erika Patall's research on autonomy support that sharpens this point considerably. Patall and her colleagues have shown across multiple studies that choice and freedom enhance intrinsic motivation, effort, and performance when students have the internal resources to make use of them.[6] The 2010 study found that the benefits of choice provision are real but conditional: choice enhanced intrinsic motivation, effort, task performance, and perceived competence most reliably when students already felt some competence in the domain, that is, when students had adequate background knowledge. Competence, it turns out, is a prerequisite for choice, not a product of it.

This matters enormously for how we think about equity. Patall identifies autonomy-supportive practices such as providing rationales for what's being learned, incorporating students' perspectives on the work they are doing, and building the relational conditions under which students can make use of openness (Patall & Zambrano, 2019). For students who haven't yet developed the internal footing to direct their own learning, who don't have background experiences, who don't yet know enough to know what they want to know, who haven't built the participatory relationships with adults that make open-ended tasks navigable, more freedom can produce the opposite of what is intended: anxiety and less learning. Choice requires a substrate. You have to have some footing in experiences with learning, scaffolded by kind caretakers, before you can direct your own path through it.

This is why students who benefit most from open inquiry and student-directed learning tend to have apprenticeships in learning and background experiences. They arrive with histories that give them confidence and experience from exploration. Students without those histories need something different first: guided participation that lays the foundation for real choice. Giving them freedom before they have that footing is not empowerment; for many students, it is the anxiety of not knowing how to begin.

## What we should be aiming for

The classroom ecology that produces pseudo-agency sits at the intersection of institutional legibility demands, curricular pacing calibrated to students in wealthier classrooms, and the absence of the proximal guidance that builds participation histories. Teachers are handed a chain, engage, teach, prove, and the weight of it feels impossible.

The teacher is expected to close the gap between the student and the curriculum, a gap shaped by years of structural and systemic disadvantage. We saddle teachers with a kind of equity labor that rightly belongs upstream of the profession. And here the relocation move completes itself: the system relocates upstream labor onto the teacher, then reads the teacher's inability to do the impossible as a personal failing.

Teachers in high-poverty schools are forced to collapse conceptual understanding into rote memorization, because students lack the formative background experiences and apprenticeships in learning that the pace assumes. Coverage becomes the only thing that fits in the pacing available: not dialogue, not complex and interesting problems, not teacher-curated materials that effectively teach each lesson's critical aspects. And coverage, as we have seen, produces pseudo-agency almost by design.

Now put the taxonomy from Chapter 2 next to this machinery, and the equity claim gets its bite. Low-income students are not "lacking agency." They are misread across the whole map. Their engaged origination is read as disengagement: Malik, lurking and messing around. Their epistemic reframing is read as off-task behavior or defiance. Their authorial goal-setting is read as immaturity or lack of interest in school: Marcus. And their strategic compliance, suppressing origination to survive, is read as good behavior. The system doesn't just fail to develop their agency. It systematically miscategorizes the agency they already have. The taxonomy is a perceptual correction device.

Low-income students are not less agentic; they are differently agentic, and their origination takes forms the school's time-world cannot read. The school's response is not to develop that agency but to manufacture pseudo-agency, compliance and performance, as a substitute. The equity injury is double: the misreading first, then the substitution. And much of what looks like help, more structure, more motivation, more choice, deepens the injury by demanding legible performance before agency has been recognized.

So when pacing and standardized testing are offered as the means of producing equity, the result is not equity at all. It is the measurement of advantage dressed as meritocracy.

I frame the problem for my doctoral students as dimensions: a critical aspect to perceive, a meaning-making conversation to have about it, and a resourced environment to have it in. The problem is not solved until all the dimensions are addressed. Fix the materials without the dialogue and you have Germantown; fix the dialogue without the critical aspect and you have talk without an object; fix both inside a pacing regime that leaves no time and you have fixed nothing.

It would be better to aim for students to become more capable of noticing, interpreting, questioning, revising, and authoring their own understanding of the world. That aim requires teachers to read past what the walkthrough is looking for, past compliance, past legibility through standardization, past the performance of studenting, toward the fragile and partial beginnings that are the actual starting point for thinking. A more honest educational aim asks not whether students can perform the school's paced curriculum, but whether they are becoming more agentic as learners. And it requires us to understand, structurally, why classrooms so reliably produce the opposite.

## How engagement unfolds, revisited

Chapter 2 traced how engagement develops in phases, lurking, messing around, geeking out, and how deepening engagement changes the form of agency a student expresses. What belongs here is what those phases mean under the structural conditions this chapter has described.

Each school model, when engagement breaks down, reaches for its signature tool, and each misreads what the student actually needed. The traditional model reads the lingerer's silence as resistance and offers more structure and accountability; what the student needed was time to orient before being asked to perform. The progressive model reads emerging engagement that doesn't yet look like participation as a problem and offers more choice and activity; what the student needed was permission to be in an early phase without being redirected out of it. The democratic model reads the student who wants structure as not yet self-directed and offers more autonomy; what the student needed was a guide willing to get close enough to say: here, start with this.

In Ito's research, in Montessori classrooms, in the middle-income branch of the Germantown study, the phases develop at different rates in different students, which means there is almost always someone a little further along, willing to explain, close and relational, directing their newly formed understanding at someone with an actual question. The transmission chain forms naturally because students arrive with varied participation histories. The diversity of phases in the room is itself a natural resource.

In under-resourced schools, students tend to arrive at the same phase simultaneously. Their participation histories are more similar: similarly thin in most academic domains, similarly shaped by years of compliance-oriented schooling that trained out messing-around behaviors. Everyone is lurking at once. No one is yet geeking out. The transmission chain that forms naturally in more resourced settings doesn't materialize.

This has a specific classroom consequence that is easy to miss: when everyone is lurking simultaneously, pushing dialogue, asking students to talk publicly about ideas they haven't yet had the chance to privately orient toward, doesn't accelerate learning. It produces performance or fear. Students, some, few, will say something, because the classroom asks them to say something. But what they say will be a guess about what the teacher wants, not an expression of emerging understanding. The demand for visible participation before students have had time to lurk is one of the more reliable ways to produce pseudo-agency, and it is the same engine as the deflection and trivial-answer fishing named above, now applied to the whole room at once. It is also one of the more common things teachers are told to do.

The equity argument, then, is not only that under-resourced students need more experiences, though they do. It is that they need the phases to be legitimized. Lurking needs to count. Messing around needs to be protected rather than corrected. And geeking out needs to be recognized even when it doesn't look like the school's preferred form of expertise: when it shows up as hands-on manipulation rather than written explanation, as off-topic connection rather than on-task response, as competence in a domain the pacing guide didn't schedule.

## The bridge to the next chapter

Building those conditions requires something the pacing guide cannot provide and motivation strategies cannot produce. It requires a teaching stance that receives students where they actually are: that treats partial, emergent, even wrong engagement as a legitimate beginning rather than a problem to be moved past.

That stance has a name, and it is the one this chapter has already introduced: the dialogue partner. It is the subject of the next chapter, which reorients classroom dialogue away from outcomes and toward the student's individual knowing.

The dialogue teachers are often told to create, structured academic discussion, think-pair-share, Socratic seminar, is not the same thing as students being in dialogue as knowers. The first asks students to perform talk around a learning object. The second asks the learning object to be open: uncertain enough, and held lightly enough, that a student's partial understanding can actually change where the conversation goes.

How does authentic dialogue support the forms of agency this chapter and Chapter 1 have been building toward? And what does it ask of the teacher: to hold space for engaged agency, the student's effort to understand from their own position, without correcting it into compliance, and to receive an epistemic bid, a student's attempt to reshape the frame itself, without letting the curriculum close around it?

The accumulation this book is after is observable wherever the conditions are right; you can watch it happen in progressive schools. The SAT-income correlation is not just cultural capital; it is years of accumulated encounters in which origination was received and developed. The wealthy progressive-school student isn't smarter. They've had more apprenticeship in being a knower. My argument is that this accumulation shouldn't require wealth. It should be structurally possible in public Title I classrooms: the teacher's perceptual skill, the dialogic orientation, the curated object are portable conditions that can produce the same accumulation without the same resources. The conditions that produce agency are currently gated by wealth, but they don't have to be.

Those questions are what the next chapter takes up.

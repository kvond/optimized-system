# Every school model polices its teachers

When I suggested to administrators that an independent study could put Marcus's accomplishments on his high school transcript, I was told I was "trying to save him."

Marcus was a student in my biology class; well-behaved, if quiet disconnection counts as good behavior. He had no interest in my assignments, and one day I asked him plainly why. "I don't care about biology," he said, and after I flinched and recovered, I asked what he did care about. A different Marcus came into view: up at 4 a.m. to work out, researching stocks and making real money at it, building small business ventures. By any psychological measure he was motivated and disciplined. His school could not read any of it, and his GPA said so. When I proposed a path that would let a college see what I saw, the phrase "trying to save him" landed on me like a diagnosis — a teacher who had lost professional distance, on a personal crusade. The school had no path for a student like Marcus, but the absence got rewritten as my defect.

That moment is one of the reasons I wrote the book this newsletter draws from, and it is the heart of what I want to show you in this piece: each of the three main school models polices its teachers as much as its students. Most of us have heard of the alternatives: the Montessori school across town, the free school a colleague's child attends. What we are rarely taught, in teacher preparation or anywhere after, is how to analyze what each model values: which forms of student agency it surfaces, which it buries, and what each choice costs the learner and the teacher standing in the room.

## The traditional model

Traditional schooling optimizes for what James Scott called legibility: rendering learning into standardized, countable, comparable outputs. When district administrators walk through my classroom, they check whether I am on pace; they do not ask what I am seeing in students' thinking that makes the pacing guide's sequence wrong for this group right now. The system reads output, not source. Marcus produced no output it could count, so he was filed under lazy, unmotivated, immature.

Kant named the contradiction this model runs on: how shall I cultivate freedom under conditions of compulsion? Compulsion first, freedom after seems sensible; anyone who has had a two-year-old knows children do not arrive as rational planners. But "later" keeps receding. First you receive the curriculum; someday, as an adult, you get to think.

To be fair, the traditional model persists because it holds real goods: structure, safety, teachers who care, direct teaching through curated content. Even Marcus thought his English and math courses had value to him. The trouble is narrower and worse: the model has no language for origination, a student's effort to understand from their own position and toward their own ends. What a model cannot name, its teachers are not permitted to act on. That is the policing.

## The progressive model

The progressive model is the one I align with most: Dewey, Holt, Montessori. It builds classrooms around experience and sense-making, and it is the reason a student like Angie, who refused to divide fractions by a memorized jingle she could not understand, would at least have room to construct something. Its paradox is quieter than compulsion: individually constructed knowledge aimed at standardized endpoints. Students build, manipulate, and explore, but toward the answer the teacher already holds.

My sharpest example of progressive policing is one I did to myself, and to my own child. I kept pressing my son Brian to use manipulatives in math, certain the hands-on way was the only true sign of understanding. He finally had to tell me I was frustrating him: the manipulatives slowed him down. The orthodoxy was so much mine that I had stopped seeing the learner in front of me, even though he was my own child, and served the method instead. Nobody walked through my homeschool room with a clipboard. The police lived in my head.

A family friend, Mike, was educated in a progressive school and put the equity problem in one line: "Only a slice of kids really succeeded in that environment." The ones who arrived already academically inclined thrived; he and many classmates floundered, though he credits the school for the autonomy all of them learned to exercise. Freedom without scaffolding does not land evenly.

## The democratic model

Democratic schools (Sudbury Valley is the clearest American form) hand students real authority over what to learn and over the rules of shared life in the school itself. The philosophical anchor is that students are already motivated, and the adult's job is to protect the conditions under which that motivation runs its own course.

I learned how tightly this model polices its adults in person. At the democratic school where I did my research, Angie and her friends drifted over to a chalkboard conversation about fractions: students who were not required to be anywhere, pulled into a lesson nobody assigned. Staff took me aside afterward and said I had skated dangerously close to "coercive teaching." I had assigned nothing, graded nothing, required no one to stay. But in a school organized around non-interference, an adult who draws students into an inquiry they did not initiate is suspect by definition.

Here is the model's paradox: maximum freedom can undermine the autonomy it promises. Middle schoolers at that school would leave for a year, surrendering their freedom to get more structure for their learning, and then return to use what they had gained at their own pace. They were asking for scaffolding, and the model had no legitimate way to offer it.

## What the three have in common

I want to be careful not to overclaim here; no real school runs one model purely, and your school's mileage will vary from the extracted forms I have drawn. But across all three, watch the identical move. The structural absence gets relocated onto a person. The traditional school did not say "we have no path for Marcus"; it said I was trying to save him. The democratic school did not say "our model has no room for invitation"; it said I had been nearly coercive. And I did not say "this method is not serving Brian"; I concluded, for too long, that the problem was his resistance or my slipping ideals.

Why does this matter for how you teach on Monday? Because a model's language decides what you are able to see. A teacher who has words for what students are doing (origination, a bid to reframe the problem) can respond to what they intuit instead of correcting it and moving on. A teacher who can also name the structure she works inside can decide honestly whether she wants to teach differently or needs to teach somewhere else. That is not a small choice. It is the difference between carrying a school's blind spot as your personal failing and setting it down.

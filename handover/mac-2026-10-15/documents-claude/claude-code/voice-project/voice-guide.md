# Katherine von Duyke — Voice Guide

**Rule zero: apply these markers at their natural rate and never all at once. A draft that uses every marker in every paragraph fails, the same as a draft that uses none of them.**

Evidence base: verified-clean manuscript prose (Introduction, Ch. 1, Ch. 2 of *Answerable Teaching*, confirmed no-AI-prose in the provenance log) plus passages in Ch. 3–11 independently judged authentic; all AI-drafted or AI-scaffold passages excluded. Rates below were measured on the verified corpus (7,330 words, 92 paragraphs, 344 sentences).

## Markers

**1. A named person carries the argument.** Concepts arrive seated in a specific person — a student, one of her sons, a colleague — introduced abruptly, in scene, before any thesis. The anecdote is the evidence, not decoration.
- "Angie could produce the answers with the procedure, but it always bothered her."
- "In my biology class, Marcus was well-behaved; if quiet disconnection counts as good behavior."
- *Rate: at least one extended named-person anecdote per section; a named person appears in roughly half of all paragraphs. Never a generic anecdote ("a teacher once…").*

**2. Self-implication before system-indictment.** She confesses her own complicity first — her classroom, her parenting, her orthodoxies — and only then generalizes.
- "My sharpest example of progressive policing is one I did to myself, and to my own 4th grade child."
- "We look at our own classrooms and consider the ways we are complicit in a system that deadens many students."
- *Rate: about once per article or major section — not in every anecdote.*

**3. Scale-check honesty asides.** She meters her own claims mid-argument: "I want to be honest about…", "I could be wrong", "it's worth being precise here, because it is easy to overclaim."
- "Here is what is worth being precise about, because it is easy to overclaim. I am not saying Maya consciously set out to contest the criteria for division."
- "To be fair, the traditional model persists because it has some positive goods."
- *Rate: about once per section; roughly one paragraph in ten.*

**4. Sparse dashes; colon and semicolon hinges.** CORRECTED (Katherine's ruling, Aug 2026): the manuscript's em-dash density is residue from AI editing sweeps, not her hand. Her dictated raw prose has zero em dashes in 682 words; she types a dash as `--` and uses it sparingly. Her real mid-sentence hinges are the colon (13% of sentences), semicolon chains inside long builds, stacked comma clauses, and parentheses.
- "In my biology class, Marcus was well-behaved; if quiet disconnection counts as good behavior."
- "It becomes school as theater: a performance in which both student and teacher play their parts."
- *Rate: at most two or three dashes per article (≈3 per 1,000 words). A draft glittering with em dashes reads as a machine sweep — see tic T22.*

**5. Long accretive build, then a short punch or fragment.** Sentence length swings hard: one-fifth of her sentences run past 30 words (stacked clauses, semicolon chains, "and…and" narration), and they land on fragments or 3–6-word verdicts. When a habit is ambiguous, resolve against polish: anything that reads like AI is not her.
- "So teachers end up running the same circle: when students aren't engaged, motivate them; when motivation feels hollow, engage them; when engagement looks thin, make thinking visible; and when that fails, invoke voice, choice, ownership…"
- "Origination vs production is key here."
- *Rate: keep sentence-length standard deviation high (hers ≈ 13–15 words; the AI default is ≈ 9). At least one 30+-word sentence and one deliberate fragment or sub-8-word verdict per section.*

**6. Questions fired at the reader or the system.** Real rhetorical questions, often closing a paragraph or pivoting a section; sometimes a clipped self-interrogative.
- "What the division actually asks is: how many ¾-cups fit inside half a cup?"
- "Is the trade off a true either/or intrinsic, or is it produced by a missing support?"
- *Rate: roughly one paragraph in six or seven carries a question; about 4% of sentences. (AI default: under 2%.)*

**7. Coined terms, glossed on arrival — sometimes with a confession about their tone.** She names her own concepts mid-paragraph and immediately says what they mean, occasionally flagging the coinage as cynical or borrowed.
- "This is what I'll call **engaged agency**: a student's effort to understand from their own position, through their own thinking, however partial or wrong."
- "I call it double booking, and I want to be precise, because it is a bit of a cynical phrase but an accurate one."
- *Rate: one or two coinage-plus-gloss moves per article. Bold (or italics) marks the term at first use only — bolded key terms in about a third of paragraphs is her manuscript ceiling; for an article, a handful total.*

**8. Verbatim speech, register intact, treated as data.** Students, family, administrators quoted in their own words — colloquial, sometimes without quotation marks — and then read closely, like evidence.
- "I hate it when teachers don't listen to your ideas and just make you do it their way."
- "One student said it plainly: __I don't know who gets the remainder, but I know what the teacher wants.__ That sentence should stop us."
- *Rate: quoted speech in about a fifth of paragraphs; at least one quoted line per anecdote.*

**9. Theory cashed into classroom particulars, citations subordinate.** Scholars are woven mid-sentence to name a mechanism, then immediately grounded in something physical — a walkthrough, pennies, a chalkboard. Never a literature review, never authority display.
- "The traditional model especially optimizes for what Scott (1998) called **legibility**: rendering learning into standardized, countable, comparable outputs."
- "When district administrators come through my classroom, they check for legibility — what the system can read, where outcomes stand in as a proxy for learning."
- *Rate: inline citations in about 1 paragraph in 20 for trade/Substack prose; every theoretical term must touch a concrete classroom object within two sentences.*

**10. The signature vocabulary.** Knower / unseated as knowers; origination (vs. production); demotivation (vs. motivation); answerable / answerability; legible / legibility; pseudo-agency and the agency taxonomy; sense-making vs. meaning-making; policing (models police teachers); footing; apprenticeship; "reading the room."
- "It was **de**motivation: something alive that the lesson extinguished."
- "Each model polices its teachers as much as its students."
- *Rate: two or three of these load-bearing terms per article, used precisely — not the whole lexicon at once.*

**11. The concrete reversal.** "That is not X. It is Z." — but her Z is concrete, physical, or metaphoric, never a paired abstraction. (The abstract-to-abstract version is an AI tic and is banned; see tics list.)
- "It is not the fuel you need to start the engine. It is the exhaust of an engine that is running."
- "The problem was not a lack of motivation. It was **de**motivation: something alive that the lesson extinguished."
- *Rate: at most once or twice per article, and only when the right-hand term is something you can picture.*

**12. Concession to the other side, mid-argument.** A fairness reflex: she interrupts her own critique to grant what the criticized model gets right, then returns.
- "To be fair, the traditional model persists because it has some positive goods: relative low cost compared to private schools, sufficient structure for safety, teachers who often care, and direct teaching through curated content."
- "Sometimes it helps. Usually it helps for a day."
- *Rate: about once per major argument; roughly one section in two.*

## Secondary flavor (use sparingly, never more than one per article)

- **Pop-culture / folk reference as framing** — Princess Bride's Miracle Max, Animal Farm ("some schools are more equal than others"), Ferris Bueller. *At most one per article.*
- **Physical teacher verbs** — crouch beside, hunt down, pitch the textbook on the floor. *Where the scene calls for it.*
- **Sardonic one-liner** dropped into serious argument. *At most one per article.*

## Mechanics note

Fix mechanics always: spelling, agreement, doubled words, misused homophones ("gas lite" → "gaslit"). Her voice is in the rhythm, the anecdotes, and the vocabulary — not in the typos. But do not machine-polish the syntax: keep the stacked clauses, the asides, the fragments, the occasional sentence that bends around a citation.

# Preface

AI hasn't created a teaching problem — it has exposed it. Modern schooling teaches for completion, not for cultivating thinkers. This creates a gap between students completing assignments and those assignments successfully developing the thinker. This also leads to a practice gap for teachers. This book is an attempt to close that gap. It couldn't be more timely: current use of AI in education threatens to deepen exactly the compliance our schooling inadvertently produces, and yet it can support solving it.

The practice gap is visceral. You feel when a room has gone quietly compliant, when a student who is "participating" has actually checked out and is instead copying their neighbor's or an AI's answers — there's no thinking there. We need to cultivate the thinking of students without making our jobs harder — and doing so makes teaching more satisfying.

By some estimates, teachers already have to make a decision every three seconds.¹ We need teaching decisions framed before lessons start. By the end of the book you should be able to name which kind of thinking students present with, and what kind of response it calls for. In fact, you will be able to support student agency, which I define as capabilities + decision-making + origination + answerability. You will also come to see which forms schools currently make visible and which they keep hidden.

I've worked under many different educational conditions: as a homeschooling parent, a Montessori cooperative founder, a researcher in democratic schools, an adjunct in university classrooms, and a high school science teacher. This has given me a broad perspective on what students can do and how teachers can support them.

Years of chronic illness forced me to think iteratively: notice carefully, change one thing, observe, revise my understanding. Pain keeps you honest; you cannot prescribe a solution before you have observed the problem, nor can you invoke a solution because it's ideal or has been sold to you. That habit of attention — holding the tension between what you want to happen and what is actually happening — is something teachers do daily. At the same time we do so while surveilled (pacing guides, walkthroughs, standardized tests), which deepen — and, I'll argue, often create — the practice gap. The constraints are real.

*Answerable Teaching* argues that the crisis commonly called student disengagement is often a crisis of recognition. Students make different kinds of bids to act as knowers — through partial explanations, reframings, refusals, social claims, and self-authored pursuits — but schools tend to recognize only those that produce legible, curriculum-aligned answers. Drawing on classroom cases across public, progressive, and democratic schools, Katherine von Duyke offers a taxonomy of agency and a new ethic of teaching: teachers must become answerable to the forms of thinking students actually bring.

A note on the students in these pages. Every name is a pseudonym, and identifying details have been changed. Cases drawn from research were collected under appropriate consent arrangements; cases from my own classrooms are described with details altered to prevent identification. I have tried throughout to render what each student was doing accurately, even where accuracy is less flattering to me than a smoother version would be. They are not here to illustrate my successes. They are here because they illustrate a form of thinking that taught me how to respond better.

Whether you are in a graduate seminar, in a for-credit professional development course, founding a school, or reading to better support your own children, you will find yourself nodding your head at familiar and frustrating scenarios. The first chapters give you the framework. The middle chapters give you the teaching moves (and I'll frame how AI can make these doable and offer you tools). The final chapters consider structural changes to school contexts making this work more possible for teachers.

— Katherine von Duyke, Wilmington, Delaware

# Introduction

Every teacher knows the Sunday scaries.

It's not just, *I have work tomorrow*. It's the specific anxiety of walking into a room where, surprisingly, students aren't all waiting for you bright-eyed with their hands folded on their desks (voluntarily). There's the blank stare, the non-stop talker, the copier, the ones who will go through the motions of being a student without actually thinking about what you're trying to teach. Not exactly shocking, right?

And almost every version of that anxiety gets translated into the same pressure: *Come up with something that will motivate them to engage.*

Teachers are constantly told to motivate students toward educational goals — not how, just pressured. If students aren't engaged, we're told to hook them: connect to their interests, make it relevant, make it fun, make it more like real life. Usually, it helps for a day. When much of the engagement in schools is really an attempt to produce visible learning without building the conditions that make learning meaningful, it becomes school-as-theater: a performance around a learning object that students and teachers dance to daily.

When district administrators come through the room, they check legibility — what the system can see and count. They'll note whether I'm on pace, but they won't ask what I'm seeing in students' thinking that makes the pacing guide's sequence wrong for this group of students right now. They'll ask a student, "Why are you doing this activity?" looking for a science standard, and treat "I don't know" as evidence of failed teaching rather than that students have spent years in classrooms where comprehensibility was never the point. It shames teachers without supporting them.

The system that evaluates learning through quick, legible signs is, in my view, what creates the gap problem it then asks teachers to solve. Professional development cycles through various fixes:[1] when learning doesn't show up, teachers are told to motivate students. When motivation feels coercive or hollow, teachers are told to engage students. When engagement looks thin, teachers are told to make thinking visible. And when that still fails, the system invokes voice, choice, ownership — autonomy, without realizing how this fails some students. Each step tries to repair what the previous step damaged. The goal is typically: I teach it → they practice it → they get it. But what happens between "I teach it" and "they get it" is treated like a black box. The practice gap lying between instruction and outcomes is left largely unaddressed. Students are actually always thinking, but we only recognize it when it matches curricular outcomes. The central ethical task of teaching is to recognize student thinking, respond to it, and then cultivate students' agency into increasingly powerful forms.

This seems obvious, yet I've been angered by the first goal since I took up teaching science in a high-poverty public high school.

None of my frustration is new. Magdalene Lampert showed decades ago what taking up student thinking can look like; what her practice never solved is doability for an ordinary teacher with a full roster, and it presupposes students who arrive predisposed to discuss their ideas out loud.[2] We recognize that a student is thinking, but we often lack the discursive moves to stay in dialogue with that thinking.

The students in this book are also cases I have observed across the diverse educational models I've been part of, and they made me ask: why were this student's actions acceptable in this situation but not those of this student in a different schooling context? Those contexts differ widely — homeschooling, Montessori schools, democratic schools, public schools, and university classrooms — and moving among them made me keen to notice and question their different receptions to students' thinking and agency.

The central argument of this book is 1) that we have built systems that systematically disconnect students from themselves as knowers,[3] which is demotivating, and then try to repair that demotivation with engagement strategies that nevertheless fail to solve the root causes: we stop students precisely when they are thinking, and instead ask them to participate in a dialogue they have no genuine stake in. Freire named it half a century ago:[4] the banking model — knowledge gets deposited into students' heads, as if minds were empty containers waiting to be filled instead of full of their own ideas. The problem isn't just structural (pacing guides, accountability) but epistemic — the system literally doesn't recognize certain kinds of knowing as knowing. And 2) student agency is highly discussed, but discussed as if it comes in one form only — and especially not what it costs students when those alternative forms are ignored.

I did all the teacher techniques, foolishly believing they would solve my classroom problems only to have them fail — believing that some other teacher or some other students succeeded and I lacked the magic. I believe teacher burnout is as much about the constant anxiety and shame teachers face as anything else. I stopped believing the schtick and began to pay attention to where my own classroom practices were actually suppressing student engagement. I now understand engagement as a combination of agency and thinking. I also don't think engineering humans to be what you want them to be is ever possible. Now, I start with students as legitimate knowers whose partial, messy, sometimes wrong beginnings are the actual starting point for teaching. The way that knowing shows up can look very strange without understanding how students use their agency[5] to develop their capabilities. This makes me, their teacher, answerable to the students' version of knowing, rather than simply the curricular outcomes as right and wrong responses. I learned to stop listening for the expected outcome, something anyone in the room could say, and to listen for what students actually said. Treating students as knowers also means students feel inherently answerable for their responses — not just right or wrong, because it is their ideas, not a parroting of mine. What I mean by answerable teaching is a reciprocal responsibility between student and teacher. Students answer for their ideas; teachers are answerable to address them.

I know at this point I sound like every other educational idealism you've been subjected to. The question rarely addressed in professional development or elsewhere is how you actually get students to authentically engage in classroom practice. Telling teachers what the ideal is, without providing a doable mechanism for getting there, is actually workplace abuse in my view. I may be more cynical than most writers, perhaps because what often works in other classrooms rarely takes in mine, but teachers from wealthier schools tell me my experiences aren't all that different from theirs. I promise you this book is different, and an authentic reflection of my teaching. I don't want to saddle you with useless ideals, requirements for teacher heroics, or a need for extraordinary charisma. The first few weeks of school, the students distrust me, but they come to see that I see them, I value their thinking, and I want to add value to it through education.

Prior to my teaching change, virtually all of Toma's work was copied from his seatmate. I wanted to see if changing my teaching could help him to engage with his own thinking. Toma had been angry at me for most of the semester. Not loudly — just with the particular coldness of a student who has decided a classroom is not worth his energy. When I crouched beside him one afternoon after a lesson and looked at what he had written — his own sentences, his own working-out, nothing copied — I said: Hey, all this you wrote in your own words? It's very good. Please continue. He looked at the page. Then a small smile crossed his face, there and gone. I recognized it. It was the smile of someone whose identity had shifted and was seen. Toma is the teacher's reward: you know you have served students well when they connect the value you are trying to communicate with their own sense of value. Recognizing and responding to student thinking is not just ethically better and more fun; it is less exhausting than performing engagement theater.

Teachers perceive a great deal about their students — this has been documented since at least 1994.[6] Noticing has never been the bottleneck. What is missing is what to do next, under too-fast pacing guides, ridiculous ideas of what beginners in a domain can do, and standardized accountability pressures that shape what teachers can afford to act on. Any honest account of teaching must address what the structure of schooling rewards, and what it makes difficult.

Bakhtin had a word for these factors: chronotope.[7] Literally, a time-place. He used it for novels — the way a setting is more than its location, but also its rhythms, its conventions, who can speak to whom about what. Classrooms also have predictable rhythms and routines, what counts as legitimate dialogue and what doesn't, what the teacher aims toward and what the teacher pushes past, what students will say in front of each other and what they won't. It shapes what can be learned and how, and who anyone can be while learning it.

This book is organized around four intertwined questions: What changes in teaching will support students as knowers? What do we believe about student agency and its importance for learning? How do schooling chronotopes make some forms of agency more or less possible? And what language of agency do teachers need to support students' cultivation of their own agency?

Across the chapters, exemplar students reveal the different forms agency can take; Chapter 2 brings those forms together as a taxonomy. Students continually make bids to act as knowers. I map the forms those bids take and the ways classroom conditions recognize, redirect, or suppress them. The teacher's role, then, is to take up student thinking discursively. Agency appears in discourse — as a question, model, objection, interpretation, refusal, or reframing — and develops according to how that contribution is received. It's not more paperwork; it's just recognizing which form of agency is alive in a student at any given time and knowing what moves to take to nourish it.

Accounts of student empowerment often ignore how institutional structures legitimize what learning should look like. Books about instructional strategy often skip over what teachers need to notice in student thinking. Books about school reform address systemic problems but offer no way to act within them. This book tries to hold all three together — because in reality, teachers must contend with each.

In one sentence the book is about students being responsible to answer for their ideas; teachers are answerable to address them.

AI technology used without addressing the gap in practice is likely to intensify the issue of missing students' origination. I'll get to that in Chapter 6.

First: two students who show what unread thinking looks like, and the map that names it. Then I take up what in the structure of schooling makes disconnecting students from their knowledge nearly inevitable rather than exceptional — and why its pressures are distributed so unequally.

# Chapter 1 — Disconnect

*When dividing fractions, don't ask why — just flip the sucker and multiply.*

Math teachers, of course, know this isn't optimal. Students who follow the procedure produce correct answers, and for a teacher under pressure to cover a curriculum, correct answers are the deliverables. This chapter is about two students who show why focusing on outcomes shortchanges students.

## Angie

Angie could produce correct answers with the procedure, but it always bothered her. When I met her, she had just left traditional school for one of the democratic schools where I was doing my Ph.D. research. When I asked her why she switched, she didn't hesitate: "I hate it when teachers don't listen to your ideas and just make you do it their way."¹ She'd been socializing with her friends, who were nearby. The moment we started talking and drawing her ideas on the chalkboard, not only was she engaged, but her friends drifted over to follow our conversation. These are all students who weren't required to pay attention, yet were pulled into a lesson they hadn't asked for, and completely free to leave whenever they chose to.

Angie reasoned that in dividing fractions, if you divide across the top and then the bottom numbers you get to the right answer: ½ ÷ ½ = 1. That's right, but locally bounded: dividing across top and bottom (1÷1 over 2÷2 = 1) works whenever the numerators match and the denominators match, because dividing equal numbers gives 1. It breaks at a case like ½ ÷ ¾. Angie hadn't yet tested her rule against enough cases to find its limits, which would have been the teacher's follow-up had she taken Angie's idea and worked with her on it.

But look at what her reasoning was doing. She was drawing on what she knew about division, and thinking mathematically even when her procedure only worked in limited cases. Her teacher, instead, saw Angie draw out the wrong procedure, re-explained the algorithm, and moved on. What Angie learned was: *your way of solving this doesn't count.* Her working model was treated as an error to fix rather than the beginning of an inquiry.

Angie exhibited what I'll call engaged agency: a student's effort to understand from their own position as a knower, through their own thinking, however partial or wrong. The origination of the idea is key here. Was the student doing the procedure as assigned, or was the student the originator of their own thinking? Their beginning thinking rarely looks like mastery; it often looks messy, partial, or wrong. But note, Angie did not arrive at the lesson unengaged. She came motivated, and then it was squashed. Angie was demotivated by the teacher's very typical classroom instruction. How often do we try to motivate a disengaged student? We try to fix it; supply more of it — relevance, incentives, activities, rapport — rather than ask, "What sucked the air out of this student's motivation?"

It's worth seeing exactly what the jingle drops mathematically. The procedure preserves the number's value and erases the unit that gives it meaning. Take ½ cup ÷ ¾ cup. The algorithm yields ⅔ — correct, but ⅔ of what? What the division actually asks is: how many ¾ cups fit in half a cup? The answer is ⅔ of a ¾-cup. Restore the unit and the relationship is intelligible; drop it, and the student is left holding a number that they can't visualize in a real-life process. Mathematically Angie's version is partly accurate because units cancel units, but Angie's resistance was an attempt to tie the symbols back to something she could see and make sense of.

## Nancy

Nancy's story shows the other side of the same problem — not a student whose partial thinking was visible, but one whose thinking was illegible to her teacher.

Nancy was a fourth-grader in a mixed third- and fourth-grade classroom at a progressive school that prized hands-on learning.² Her teacher, John, posed a yard-sale problem to the class: three students earned a dollar and had to split it evenly. He handed each child 100 pennies, allowed them to trade pennies for other coins from his bin, and then circulated to see if they successfully solved it. Students worked in several ways: some made three piles, some made strands and counted off, and some exchanged pennies for higher-value coins. Nancy traded pennies for quarters and then arranged her coins into three symmetrical groups of thirty-three cents, with a single penny at the center.

When John reached her, he was unsettled; he was looking for something that matched 100 ÷ 3 = 33 r 1. He asked Nancy, "Where is the remainder?" She first looked startled, then searched her mat, and began counting on her fingers. Her flower model didn't have a remainder — the center penny anchored a balanced arrangement. Because Nancy was known as being artistic, John assumed she'd drifted into art and forgot she was doing math. He moved on.

Nancy's arrangement inherently carried a mathematical orientation — a geometric one — whether or not she could yet name it. The coins were amounts, but they were also things she could arrange, so the relationships between them changed: the total value held steady while the configuration made "evenly" mean radial and balanced rather than a count with a leftover. What was missing was a teacher to surface that difference.

Surfacing it would have brightened Nancy rather than startled her — and would have shown her, and the class, that her way of seeing through art can also be understood as mathematical. Instead, she learned to file art under "not math," and reproduce the lesson.

Nancy was not the only student with a paradigmatic difference with John's intention. Several of her classmates brought out a different aspect of the math problem: social fairness. Taking the three-friends framing seriously, they asked: if it's split equally, who gets the extra penny? Do you rotate it? Give it to the youngest? These weren't off-task questions.

I went home and experimented on my then five-year-old youngest, who had not experienced school division yet. She had the same dilemma. We put out three teddy bears, I gave her the coins, and after dividing them, she held the last penny; who gets it? The biggest? Mommy bear? A remainder that's obvious on paper becomes a real question once you ask to whom it belongs. One student said it plainly: *I don't know who gets the remainder, but I know what the teacher wants.* That sentence should stop us. A child who has set aside his questions has already begun to disconnect from himself as a knower in order to give the teacher the thinking he wants (von Duyke & Matusov, 2016).

Nancy's flower and the students' social questions could have been part of the lesson: Does Nancy's arrangement show something mathematical? Is the center penny a remainder? What does "evenly" mean here? The answer 33 r 1 becomes sensible and memorable precisely when a class has worked through the questions students are already asking about it. John's lesson worked, but only in the narrow sense — students were able to construct an answer, but their questions were left hanging. To be fair to John, he did hesitate over Nancy's mat, precisely because he saw that her arrangement did seem to work. But the constructivist training he likely carried had no move for what she had done; it takes a dialogic lens to hear that she'd changed the question (to be discussed in Chapter 5).²ᵇ

With classroom discussion, Nancy and her classmates would've been able to move between their perceptual orientations (aesthetic, algorithmic, social) and choose which is appropriate for which problem, and note the limitations in the algorithm alone. Nancy's aesthetic orientation would have been built on as a legitimate mathematical move. She could have connected it to the social and algorithmic models in the rest of the class. The class would be exposed to differences in understanding, surfacing questions that hadn't yet been formulated.

What Nancy was doing, in the taxonomy I present in Chapter 2, was a bid towards epistemic agency: not engagement inside the teacher's frame, but a change of the frame. Where Angie's engaged agency worked inside the teacher's question, Nancy's move proposed a different one — that "evenly" could be a pattern rather than a count. Epistemic agency is easy to miss because it often looks like off-task behavior, and because an epistemic bid only develops in class when it is recognized by the teacher. The thinking pattern exists without teacher recognition, but an unread bid leaves the student with no confirmation and no added value to her ideas. The rest of this book builds a language for seeing these differences in student thinking, so that what happened to Angie and Nancy becomes less likely — not because teachers try harder to engage students, but because they can recognize what has already engaged them. Did Nancy learn that an artistic orientation towards math has no value? Did Angie learn that thinking through alternative procedures has no value? When student thinking is discounted often enough, it becomes a kind of epistemic gaslighting.⁵ No wonder Angie was pissed off enough to finally leave.

Nancy's coin arrangement was a proposal from outside the task frame — the kind of frame-shift that only a dialogic lens recognizes, and that even the theories I most admire, radical constructivism and Magdalene Lampert's discourse work, have no move for.⁶

## What both students show

These are not idiosyncratic misses, nor bad teachers; they are predictable outcomes of classrooms organized around producing legible answers rather than interpreting student thinking. Schooling trains students to adapt to the teacher's framing rather than meet learning on their own terms — to fit meaning rather than make it. It teaches them that their questions, models, and frameworks don't count. It's teaching with one foot on the gas and the other on the brake: we stop students exactly when they're engaged, then ask them to engage in a way they aren't curious about.

## What standard practice misses

Standard practice misses three levels of student agency simultaneously:

1. The answer level: it evaluates correctness without examining the reasoning.

2. The agency level: it doesn't recognize bids for engagement, epistemic reframing, or authorial direction.

3. The chronotope level: the misreading is systematized by policy decisions around legible outputs.

When policy demands legibility, teachers can't afford time for agency. When they can't afford agency, they can't see diverse reasoning. When they can't see reasoning, they teach to the answer. The teacher who could read Nancy's flower as mathematics would be doing something schools don't support: developing mathematical thinking from aesthetic perception, not just "allowing" it as a warm-up before getting to the "real" math.

To be a knower is to be received as someone whose sense-making counts — whose partial, half-formed account is treated as the beginning of understanding rather than the absence of it. This opens the door for meaning-making: a different way of understanding, seeing, or personally relating to a lesson. Students originate meanings → they make those meanings available through questions, models, objections, arrangements, and behaviors → classroom discourse either recognizes and develops those contributions or redirects and suppresses them → repeated patterns of failing to take up their ideas shape the engagement students experience. This dialogic approach also requires a shift in what school knowledge is taken to be: not a finished object transferred to students intact, but something a person originates in their own understanding as they engage with it with others.³ Students' ideas can then be tested, revised, and articulated more powerfully — making them answerable to each other.

To be fair, the research literature already uses the term epistemic agency, for students' active participation in inquiry; I use it to denote a different shift — the point at which the student's thinking begins to exceed the lesson frame.⁴

What in the structure of schooling makes this kind of disconnection nearly inevitable rather than exceptional, especially in high-poverty schools?

5. "Gaslighting" captures the experiential quality for the student: not just "my idea was ignored" but "my capacity to trust my own perception is eroded." That's different from, and more damaging than, simple correction. A teacher who corrects a math error while validating the student's reasoning preserves epistemic confidence. A teacher who treats the error as evidence the student wasn't doing math at all erodes it.

6. Lampert's research in mathematics aimed to open the discourse: students are encouraged to conjecture and disagree, but the direction of fit runs one way, toward disciplinary norms. The teacher is answerable to mathematics. Wouldn't it have been better if the teacher were answerable to the student's bid? Lampert's repertoire takes up wrong-but-coherent reasoning within the lesson frame; it has no move for a bid that reframes it. See also the Introduction, note 2.

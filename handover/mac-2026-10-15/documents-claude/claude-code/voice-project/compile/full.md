# Preface

AI hasn't created a teaching problem — it has exposed it. Modern schooling teaches for completion, not for cultivating thinkers. This creates a gap between students completing assignments and those assignments successfully developing the thinker. This also leads to a practice gap for teachers. This book is an attempt to close that gap. It couldn't be more timely: current use of AI in education threatens to deepen exactly the compliance our schooling inadvertently produces, and yet it can support solving it.

The practice gap is visceral. You feel when a room has gone quietly compliant, when a student who is "participating" has actually checked out and is instead copying their neighbor's or an AI's answers — there's no thinking there. We need to cultivate the thinking of students without making our jobs harder — and doing so makes teaching more satisfying.

By some estimates, teachers already have to make a decision every three seconds.¹ We need teaching decisions framed before lessons start. By the end of the book you should be able to name which kind of thinking students present with, and what kind of response it calls for. In fact, you will be able to support student agency, which I define as capabilities + decision-making + origination + answerability. You will also come to see which forms schools currently make visible and which they keep hidden.

I've worked under many different educational conditions: as a homeschooling parent, a Montessori cooperative founder, a researcher in democratic schools, an adjunct in university classrooms, and a high school science teacher. This has given me a broad perspective on what students can do and how teachers can support them.

A note on method, so you know what kind of book you are holding. This is a case study of one teacher: my classrooms, and the sites I have taught and researched in, observed over years and set in dialogue with the theorists I have read along the way. It is a dialogue with them, not a meta-analysis of the literature. I credit where I can, and I have tried not to get lost in historical trails or esoteric side-quarrels. Where researchers have tested something I have only watched, I cite them; where I make a claim from my own room, I say so.

And one thing worth saying plainly, because readers will wonder where I stand. I do not think race or class is the engine of what this book describes; the engine is economic. Nothing inherent in race determines school performance, and class as such confers no protection: wealth without intellectual mentorship fails too. Where race enters, and it does enter, I will show it entering through this book's own mechanism. The full argument is in Chapter 4.

Years of chronic illness forced me to think iteratively: notice carefully, change one thing, observe, revise my understanding. Pain keeps you honest; you cannot prescribe a solution before you have observed the problem, nor can you invoke a solution because it's ideal or has been sold to you. That habit of attention — holding the tension between what you want to happen and what is actually happening — is something teachers do daily. At the same time we do so while surveilled (pacing guides, walkthroughs, standardized tests), which deepen — and, I'll argue, often create — the practice gap. The constraints are real.

*Answerable Teaching* argues that the crisis commonly called student disengagement is often a crisis of recognition. Students make different kinds of bids to act as knowers — through partial explanations, reframings, refusals, social claims, and self-authored pursuits — but schools tend to recognize only those that produce legible, curriculum-aligned answers. Drawing on classroom cases across public, progressive, and democratic schools, Katherine von Duyke offers a taxonomy of agency and a new ethic of teaching: teachers must become answerable to the forms of thinking students actually bring.

A note on the students in these pages. Every name is a pseudonym, and identifying details have been changed. Cases drawn from research were collected under appropriate consent arrangements; cases from my own classrooms are described with details altered to prevent identification. I have tried throughout to render what each student was doing accurately, even where accuracy is less flattering to me than a smoother version would be. They are not here to illustrate my successes. They are here because they illustrate a form of thinking that taught me how to respond better.

Whether you are in a graduate seminar, in a for-credit professional development course, founding a school, or reading to better support your own children, you will find yourself nodding your head at familiar and frustrating scenarios. The first chapters give you the framework. The middle chapters give you the teaching moves (and I'll frame how AI can make these doable and offer you tools). The final chapters consider structural changes to school contexts making this work more possible for teachers.

— Katherine von Duyke, Wilmington, Delaware

# Introduction

Every teacher knows the Sunday scaries.

It's not just, *I have work tomorrow*. It's the specific anxiety of walking into a room where, surprisingly, students aren't all waiting for you bright-eyed with their hands folded on their desks (voluntarily). There's the blank stare, the non-stop talker, the copier, the ones who will go through the motions of being a student without actually thinking about what you're trying to teach. Not exactly shocking, right?

And almost every version of that anxiety gets translated into the same pressure: *Come up with something that will motivate them to engage.*

Teachers are constantly told to motivate students toward educational goals — not how, just pressured. If students aren't engaged, we're told to hook them: connect to their interests, make it relevant, make it fun, make it more like real life. Usually, it helps for a day. When much of the engagement in schools is really an attempt to produce visible learning without building the conditions that make learning meaningful, it becomes school-as-theater: a performance around a learning object that students and teachers dance to daily.

When district administrators come through the room, they check legibility — what the system can see and count. They'll note whether I'm on pace, but they won't ask what I'm seeing in students' thinking that makes the pacing guide's sequence wrong for this group of students right now. They'll ask a student, "Why are you doing this activity?" looking for a science standard, and treat "I don't know" as evidence of failed teaching rather than that students have spent years in classrooms where comprehensibility was never the point. It shames teachers without supporting them.

The system that evaluates learning through quick, legible signs is, in my view, what creates the gap problem it then asks teachers to solve. Professional development cycles through various fixes:[1] when learning doesn't show up, teachers are told to motivate students. When motivation feels coercive or hollow, teachers are told to engage students. When engagement looks thin, teachers are told to make thinking visible. And when that still fails, the system invokes voice, choice, ownership — autonomy, without realizing how this fails some students. Each step tries to repair what the previous step damaged. The goal is typically: I teach it → they practice it → they get it. But what happens between "I teach it" and "they get it" is treated like a black box. The practice gap lying between instruction and outcomes is left largely unaddressed. Students are actually always thinking, but we only recognize it when it matches curricular outcomes. The central ethical task of teaching is to recognize student thinking, respond to it, and then cultivate students' agency into increasingly powerful forms.

This seems obvious, yet I've been angered by the first goal since I took up teaching science in a high-poverty public high school.

None of my frustration is new. Magdalene Lampert showed decades ago what taking up student thinking can look like; what her practice never solved is doability for an ordinary teacher with a full roster, and it presupposes students who arrive predisposed to discuss their ideas out loud.[2] We recognize that a student is thinking, but we often lack the discursive moves to stay in dialogue with that thinking.

The students in this book are also cases I have observed across the diverse educational models I've been part of, and they made me ask: why were this student's actions acceptable in this situation but not those of this student in a different schooling context? Those contexts differ widely — homeschooling, Montessori schools, democratic schools, public schools, and university classrooms — and moving among them made me keen to notice and question their different receptions to students' thinking and agency.

The central argument of this book is 1) that we have built systems that systematically disconnect students from themselves as knowers,[3] which is demotivating, and then try to repair that demotivation with engagement strategies that nevertheless fail to solve the root causes: we stop students precisely when they are thinking, and instead ask them to participate in a dialogue they have no genuine stake in. Freire named it half a century ago:[4] the banking model — knowledge gets deposited into students' heads, as if minds were empty containers waiting to be filled instead of full of their own ideas. The problem isn't just structural (pacing guides, accountability) but epistemic — the system literally doesn't recognize certain kinds of knowing as knowing. And 2) student agency is highly discussed, but discussed as if it comes in one form only — and especially not what it costs students when those alternative forms are ignored.

I did all the teacher techniques, foolishly believing they would solve my classroom problems only to have them fail — believing that some other teacher or some other students succeeded and I lacked the magic. I believe teacher burnout is as much about the constant anxiety and shame teachers face as anything else. I stopped believing the schtick and began to pay attention to where my own classroom practices were actually suppressing student engagement. I now understand engagement as a combination of agency and thinking. I also don't think engineering humans to be what you want them to be is ever possible. Now, I start with students as legitimate knowers whose partial, messy, sometimes wrong beginnings are the actual starting point for teaching. The way that knowing shows up can look very strange without understanding how students use their agency[5] to develop their capabilities. This makes me, their teacher, answerable to the students' version of knowing, rather than simply the curricular outcomes as right and wrong responses. I learned to stop listening for the expected outcome, something anyone in the room could say, and to listen for what students actually said. Treating students as knowers also means students feel inherently answerable for their responses — not just right or wrong, because it is their ideas, not a parroting of mine. What I mean by answerable teaching is a reciprocal responsibility between student and teacher. Students answer for their ideas; teachers are answerable to address them.

I know at this point I sound like every other educational idealism you've been subjected to. The question rarely addressed in professional development or elsewhere is how you actually get students to authentically engage in classroom practice. Telling teachers what the ideal is, without providing a doable mechanism for getting there, is actually workplace abuse in my view. I may be more cynical than most writers, perhaps because what often works in other classrooms rarely takes in mine, but teachers from wealthier schools tell me my experiences aren't all that different from theirs. I promise you this book is different, and an authentic reflection of my teaching. I don't want to saddle you with useless ideals, requirements for teacher heroics, or a need for extraordinary charisma. The first few weeks of school, the students distrust me, but they come to see that I see them, I value their thinking, and I want to add value to it through education.

Prior to my teaching change, virtually all of Toma's work was copied from his seatmate. I wanted to see if changing my teaching could help him to engage with his own thinking. Toma had been angry at me for most of the semester. Not loudly — just with the particular coldness of a student who has decided a classroom is not worth his energy. When I crouched beside him one afternoon after a lesson and looked at what he had written — his own sentences, his own working-out, nothing copied — I said: Hey, all this you wrote in your own words? It's very good. Please continue. He looked at the page. Then a small smile crossed his face, there and gone. I recognized it. It was the smile of someone whose identity had shifted and was seen. Toma is the teacher's reward: you know you have served students well when they connect the value you are trying to communicate with their own sense of value. Recognizing and responding to student thinking is not just ethically better and more fun; it is less exhausting than performing engagement theater.

Teachers perceive a great deal about their students — this has been documented since at least 1994.[6] Noticing has never been the bottleneck. What is missing is what to do next, under too-fast pacing guides, ridiculous ideas of what beginners in a domain can do, and standardized accountability pressures that shape what teachers can afford to act on. Any honest account of teaching must address what the structure of schooling rewards, and what it makes difficult.

Bakhtin had a word for these factors: chronotope.[7] Literally, a time-place. He used it for novels — the way a setting is more than its location, but also its rhythms, its conventions, who can speak to whom about what. Classrooms also have predictable rhythms and routines, what counts as legitimate dialogue and what doesn't, what the teacher aims toward and what the teacher pushes past, what students will say in front of each other and what they won't. It shapes what can be learned and how, and who anyone can be while learning it.

This book is organized around four intertwined questions: What changes in teaching will support students as knowers? What do we believe about student agency and its importance for learning? How do schooling chronotopes make some forms of agency more or less possible? And what language of agency do teachers need to support students' cultivation of their own agency?

Across the chapters, exemplar students reveal the different forms agency can take; Chapter 2 brings those forms together as a taxonomy. Students continually make bids to act as knowers. I map the forms those bids take and the ways classroom conditions recognize, redirect, or suppress them. The teacher's role, then, is to take up student thinking discursively. Agency appears in discourse — as a question, model, objection, interpretation, refusal, or reframing — and develops according to how that contribution is received. It's not more paperwork; it's just recognizing which form of agency is alive in a student at any given time and knowing what moves to take to nourish it.

Accounts of student empowerment often ignore how institutional structures legitimize what learning should look like. Books about instructional strategy often skip over what teachers need to notice in student thinking. Books about school reform address systemic problems but offer no way to act within them. This book tries to hold all three together — because in reality, teachers must contend with each.

In one sentence the book is about students being responsible to answer for their ideas; teachers are answerable to address them.

AI technology used without addressing the gap in practice is likely to intensify the issue of missing students' origination. I'll get to that in Chapter 6.

First: two students who show what unread thinking looks like, and the map that names it. Then I take up what in the structure of schooling makes disconnecting students from their knowledge nearly inevitable rather than exceptional — and why its pressures are distributed so unequally.

# Chapter 1 — Disconnect

*When dividing fractions, don't ask why — just flip the sucker and multiply.*

Math teachers, of course, know this isn't optimal. Students who follow the procedure produce correct answers, and for a teacher under pressure to cover a curriculum, correct answers are the deliverables. This chapter is about two students who show why focusing on outcomes shortchanges students.

## Angie

Angie could produce correct answers with the procedure, but it always bothered her. When I met her, she had just left traditional school for one of the democratic schools where I was doing my Ph.D. research. When I asked her why she switched, she didn't hesitate: "I hate it when teachers don't listen to your ideas and just make you do it their way."¹ She'd been socializing with her friends, who were nearby. The moment we started talking and drawing her ideas on the chalkboard, not only was she engaged, but her friends drifted over to follow our conversation. These are all students who weren't required to pay attention, yet were pulled into a lesson they hadn't asked for, and completely free to leave whenever they chose to.

Angie reasoned that in dividing fractions, if you divide across the top and then the bottom numbers you get to the right answer: ½ ÷ ½ = 1. That's right, but locally bounded: dividing across top and bottom (1÷1 over 2÷2 = 1) works whenever the numerators match and the denominators match, because dividing equal numbers gives 1. It breaks at a case like ½ ÷ ¾. Angie hadn't yet tested her rule against enough cases to find its limits, which would have been the teacher's follow-up had she taken Angie's idea and worked with her on it.

But look at what her reasoning was doing. She was drawing on what she knew about division, and thinking mathematically even when her procedure only worked in limited cases. Her teacher, instead, saw Angie draw out the wrong procedure, re-explained the algorithm, and moved on. What Angie learned was: *your way of solving this doesn't count.* Her working model was treated as an error to fix rather than the beginning of an inquiry.

Angie exhibited what I'll call engaged agency: a student's effort to understand from their own position as a knower, through their own thinking, however partial or wrong. The origination of the idea is key here. Was the student doing the procedure as assigned, or was the student the originator of their own thinking? Their beginning thinking rarely looks like mastery; it often looks messy, partial, or wrong. But note, Angie did not arrive at the lesson unengaged. She came motivated, and then it was squashed. Angie was demotivated by the teacher's very typical classroom instruction. How often do we try to motivate a disengaged student? We try to fix it; supply more of it — relevance, incentives, activities, rapport — rather than ask, "What sucked the air out of this student's motivation?"

It's worth seeing exactly what the jingle drops mathematically. The procedure preserves the number's value and erases the unit that gives it meaning. Take ½ cup ÷ ¾ cup. The algorithm yields ⅔ — correct, but ⅔ of what? What the division actually asks is: how many ¾ cups fit in half a cup? The answer is ⅔ of a ¾-cup. Restore the unit and the relationship is intelligible; drop it, and the student is left holding a number that they can't visualize in a real-life process. Mathematically Angie's version is partly accurate because units cancel units, but Angie's resistance was an attempt to tie the symbols back to something she could see and make sense of.

## Nancy

Nancy's story shows the other side of the same problem — not a student whose partial thinking was visible, but one whose thinking was illegible to her teacher.

Nancy was a fourth-grader in a mixed third- and fourth-grade classroom at a progressive school that prized hands-on learning.² Her teacher, John, posed a yard-sale problem to the class: three students earned a dollar and had to split it evenly. He handed each child 100 pennies, allowed them to trade pennies for other coins from his bin, and then circulated to see if they successfully solved it. Students worked in several ways: some made three piles, some made strands and counted off, and some exchanged pennies for higher-value coins. Nancy traded pennies for quarters and then arranged her coins into three symmetrical groups of thirty-three cents, with a single penny at the center.

When John reached her, he was unsettled; he was looking for something that matched 100 ÷ 3 = 33 r 1. He asked Nancy, "Where is the remainder?" She first looked startled, then searched her mat, and began counting on her fingers. Her flower model didn't have a remainder — the center penny anchored a balanced arrangement. Because Nancy was known as being artistic, John assumed she'd drifted into art and forgot she was doing math. He moved on.

Nancy's arrangement inherently carried a mathematical orientation — a geometric one — whether or not she could yet name it. The coins were amounts, but they were also things she could arrange, so the relationships between them changed: the total value held steady while the configuration made "evenly" mean radial and balanced rather than a count with a leftover. What was missing was a teacher to surface that difference.

Surfacing it would have brightened Nancy rather than startled her — and would have shown her, and the class, that her way of seeing through art can also be understood as mathematical. Instead, she learned to file art under "not math," and reproduce the lesson.

Nancy was not the only student with a paradigmatic difference with John's intention. Several of her classmates brought out a different aspect of the math problem: social fairness. Taking the three-friends framing seriously, they asked: if it's split equally, who gets the extra penny? Do you rotate it? Give it to the youngest? These weren't off-task questions.

I went home and experimented on my then five-year-old youngest, who had not experienced school division yet. She had the same dilemma. We put out three teddy bears, I gave her the coins, and after dividing them, she held the last penny; who gets it? The biggest? Mommy bear? A remainder that's obvious on paper becomes a real question once you ask to whom it belongs. One student said it plainly: *I don't know who gets the remainder, but I know what the teacher wants.* That sentence should stop us. A child who has set aside his questions has already begun to disconnect from himself as a knower in order to give the teacher the thinking he wants (von Duyke & Matusov, 2016).

Nancy's flower and the students' social questions could have been part of the lesson: Does Nancy's arrangement show something mathematical? Is the center penny a remainder? What does "evenly" mean here? The answer 33 r 1 becomes sensible and memorable precisely when a class has worked through the questions students are already asking about it. John's lesson worked, but only in the narrow sense — students were able to construct an answer, but their questions were left hanging. To be fair to John, he did hesitate over Nancy's mat, precisely because he saw that her arrangement did seem to work. But the constructivist training he likely carried had no move for what she had done; it takes a dialogic lens to hear that she'd changed the question (to be discussed in Chapter 5).²ᵇ

With classroom discussion, Nancy and her classmates would've been able to move between their perceptual orientations (aesthetic, algorithmic, social) and choose which is appropriate for which problem, and note the limitations in the algorithm alone. Nancy's aesthetic orientation would have been built on as a legitimate mathematical move. She could have connected it to the social and algorithmic models in the rest of the class. The class would be exposed to differences in understanding, surfacing questions that hadn't yet been formulated.

What Nancy was doing, in the taxonomy I present in Chapter 2, was a bid towards epistemic agency: not engagement inside the teacher's frame, but a change of the frame. Where Angie's engaged agency worked inside the teacher's question, Nancy's move proposed a different one — that "evenly" could be a pattern rather than a count. Epistemic agency is easy to miss because it often looks like off-task behavior, and because an epistemic bid only develops in class when it is recognized by the teacher. The thinking pattern exists without teacher recognition, but an unread bid leaves the student with no confirmation and no added value to her ideas. The rest of this book builds a language for seeing these differences in student thinking, so that what happened to Angie and Nancy becomes less likely — not because teachers try harder to engage students, but because they can recognize what has already engaged them. Did Nancy learn that an artistic orientation towards math has no value? Did Angie learn that thinking through alternative procedures has no value? When student thinking is discounted often enough, it becomes a kind of epistemic gaslighting.⁵ No wonder Angie was pissed off enough to finally leave.

Nancy's coin arrangement was a proposal from outside the task frame — the kind of frame-shift that only a dialogic lens recognizes, and that even the theories I most admire, radical constructivism and Magdalene Lampert's discourse work, have no move for.⁶

## What both students show

These are not idiosyncratic misses, nor bad teachers; they are predictable outcomes of classrooms organized around producing legible answers rather than interpreting student thinking. Schooling trains students to adapt to the teacher's framing rather than meet learning on their own terms — to fit meaning rather than make it. It teaches them that their questions, models, and frameworks don't count. It's teaching with one foot on the gas and the other on the brake: we stop students exactly when they're engaged, then ask them to engage in a way they aren't curious about.

## What standard practice misses

Standard practice misses three levels of student agency simultaneously:

1. The answer level: it evaluates correctness without examining the reasoning.

2. The agency level: it doesn't recognize bids for engagement, epistemic reframing, or authorial direction.

3. The chronotope level: the misreading is systematized by policy decisions around legible outputs.

When policy demands legibility, teachers can't afford time for agency. When they can't afford agency, they can't see diverse reasoning. When they can't see reasoning, they teach to the answer. The teacher who could read Nancy's flower as mathematics would be doing something schools don't support: developing mathematical thinking from aesthetic perception, not just "allowing" it as a warm-up before getting to the "real" math.

To be a knower is to be received as someone whose sense-making counts — whose partial, half-formed account is treated as the beginning of understanding rather than the absence of it. This opens the door for meaning-making: a different way of understanding, seeing, or personally relating to a lesson. Students originate meanings → they make those meanings available through questions, models, objections, arrangements, and behaviors → classroom discourse either recognizes and develops those contributions or redirects and suppresses them → repeated patterns of failing to take up their ideas shape the engagement students experience. This dialogic approach also requires a shift in what school knowledge is taken to be: not a finished object transferred to students intact, but something a person originates in their own understanding as they engage with it with others.³ Students' ideas can then be tested, revised, and articulated more powerfully — making them answerable to each other.

To be fair, the research literature already uses the term epistemic agency, for students' active participation in inquiry; I use it to denote a different shift — the point at which the student's thinking begins to exceed the lesson frame.⁴

What in the structure of schooling makes this kind of disconnection nearly inevitable rather than exceptional, especially in high-poverty schools?

The distinction matters because the literature often conflates student activity and teacher response under the assumption that the teacher is always shaping the student toward the lesson outcomes, rather than using student understandings to deepen or even reconsider lesson outcomes. That's different from, and more damaging than, simple correction. A teacher who corrects a math error while validating the student's reasoning preserves epistemic confidence. A teacher who treats the error as evidence the student wasn't doing math at all erodes it.

The teacher is answerable to mathematics. Wouldn't it have been better if the teacher were answerable to the student's bid? # Chapter 2 — What Is Agency? Why Do We Want It?

Agency = capabilities + decision-making + origination + answerability.

> – *Capabilities + decision-making + origination, without answerability: a high-end art thief.*[1]
> – *Decision-making + origination + answerability, without capabilities: an Amazon influencer.*
> – *Decision-making + capabilities + answerability, without origination: a middle manager.*
> – *Origination + capabilities + answerability, without decision-making: a classroom teacher.*

The thief is skilled, decisive, and inventive, and answerable to no one. The influencer has plenty of voice, and stands behind it in public; the products are someone else's. The middle manager is competent, empowered, and accountable, executing a vision that was never theirs. And the last: that is a teacher under a pacing guide, inventive, expert, answerable to everyone, while the big decisions are made somewhere else.

Agency, at least as I am using the concept in the context of the classroom, is origination of and answerability for a move on an object of knowing, within a context and its constraints. Origination, decision-making, and capability describe something about the actor's capacity to act; answerability describes what happens when that action enters a world containing other people, knowledge, consequences, and responses.

In the classroom, **origination** defines whether an answer came from a student's own thinking, or was copied from lecture notes, from friends, or from AI. Can they originate actions in the shared conditions of the classroom? Can they originate their path of learning itself?

**Decision-making** involves voice, but it also requires the personal capacity to make a choice, and having the amount of informed knowledge required to make that choice.

**Capabilities**, as agency, means becoming more skilled, more knowledgeable, and increasingly sought after for expertise.

**Answerability** is the teacher answering the student, the student answering the teacher and the class, and each taking responsibility for their position. By "responsible" I mean that the person's identity and sense of self are present in their words, rather than the words simply telling the teacher what she wants to hear.

So my definition of agency as I am using it applies only in the context of learning: agency is the student's origination of and answerability for a move on the object of knowing, within a specified context and its constraints, including the classroom's people, relations, and the collaborative formation of knowledge. In a Bakhtinian sense this also includes texts and historical others, since we argue in our heads with them as well, even if we don't say it out loud.

The table below organizes the ways I have watched learning show up across classrooms and homeschooling.

| Form | Student's object | What the student is doing | Common teacher misreading | Productive response |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Effortful | Teacher-assigned outcome | Works persistently within externally owned aims | "Ideal engagement" | Connect work to purpose; invite judgment |
| Engaged | The assigned object | Makes sense from an owned position | "Wrong" or "off task" | Take up and test the reasoning |
| Epistemic | The framing of the object | Reframes what counts as the question or representation | "Distraction" | Make the reframe discussable |
| Democratic | Conditions of shared learning | Acts on norms, procedures, purposes | "Defiance" or mere preference | Deliberate shared conditions |
| Authorial | A self-originated purpose or path | Generates a sustained direction of study | "Extracurricular" | Legitimize, resource, and credential it |

As the table suggests, agency is not one thing. There is no hierarchy here; it is more about how the person shows up, or can show up, in a given context. In this chapter I argue for swapping the term *motivation* for a clearer language of agency: recognize students as knowers, and learn the patterns of agency that make these forms legible to the teacher.

My daughter, Heidi, once told me she disliked her high school art class. This surprised me because, as a homeschooler, she could lose herself in the color and rhythm of drawing for hours. What she didn't love was the way art class was being taught. "She's too directive. I wish she'd show us a skill, maybe a few examples, and then just let us play: give us time to get good at it, time to make more decisions about what we want to make, and time to craft at our own pace."

Heidi articulated what many learners feel but rarely name: the need for guidance with space, structure with freedom. She didn't want to be left entirely on her own, nor told exactly what to do. She wanted to be taught *with*, not *at*. To learn from someone more experienced while still steering her own creative direction. She wasn't rejecting instruction; she was rejecting instruction that left no room for her emerging agency in art. Her teacher, probably under pressure to meet curricular pacing requirements, emphasized coverage over ownership.

Over the years I've met students with very different relationships to learning: students like Angie, who needed to think in her own way; like Heidi, who needed room to originate; students like Marcus, whose learning was obvious everywhere except inside school; and students like Nancy, who had a startling re-visioning of what the lesson even was. They weren't more or less capable. They were differently oriented. Motivation language is insufficient because it can't name what students are doing as knowers.

## How focusing on motivation unseats knowers

When teachers say we want students to be "motivated" or "self-directed," what do we actually mean? Motivated to comply, or to create? Self-directed, or directed within whose boundaries? Vague language obscures students' lived experience and weakens our ability to design environments that support their development as knowers. And if you support teachers, through coaching, walkthroughs, or policy, be aware that vague language doesn't just obscure, it limits perception.

Often in educational research, a term means one thing in psychology and something different in education. Edward Deci (1971) defined intrinsic motivation as "the doing of an activity for its inherent satisfactions rather than for some separable consequence," distinguishing it from extrinsic motivation. Later, Deci and Ryan (1994) tried to humanize classroom motivation by showing how students can come to endorse what school asks of them, especially when teachers support autonomy and students engage with a sense of choice and personal commitment. This is what they called identified regulation. That is, the student identifies what the teacher is doing as their own choice.

That distinction matters. In the day-to-day logic of schooling, intrinsic motivation often gets collapsed into identified regulation: endorsing what school assigns. In school-improvement talk the two get treated as interchangeable; if a student "buys in," we call it a win. But even when internalization occurs, students' motivation can remain bound by someone else's timing, goals, and values. Some critics, like Matusov, describe this as "benevolent control." Students solve problems; they don't pose them. Schooling trains students to adapt rather than to encounter, to *fit* meaning rather than *make* it. Identified regulation can explain endorsement, but it does not account for how students might question, revise, or reinvent curricular purposes. Leadership can confirm a room looks orderly and "on task," and it can still be a room where knowers are being unseated.

Schooling's central problem is its curriculum return-on-investment logic: coverage, pacing, deliverables. In that frame, what can be counted is what gets named, and what can't be counted gets treated as noise. This is not a teacher character issue. It's a systems issue, because pacing guides, benchmark calendars, and evaluation categories define what teachers can afford to notice. Teachers may be given some language for formative thinking, for partial models and "not-yet" explanations, but rarely the time or permission to cultivate those beginnings as learning. So motivation becomes the lever. When we don't have time for thinking, we reach for compliance. When we can't cultivate knowing, we manage behavior and call it engagement.

Nancy's case makes the disconnect visible. Nancy was engaged, careful, deeply absorbed. But because she had reframed the task inside her own interpretive frame, the teacher could not read what she was doing as math. He read it as a distraction. And once a teacher reads a student that way, "motivation" becomes the story: she got sidetracked; she didn't stay with the task. When teachers treat such moments as errors or distractions, they unintentionally teach students that their own ways of seeing are not important. In truth, these moments can be the beginning of rich discussion.

This is why I reach for the concept of agency, with its aspects of origination, decision-making, capabilities, and answerability. Agency, as I use the term in this book, is a developing capacity to act, with meaning and effect, towards an object of learning or its context, through which learners can shape purposes, participation, and possibilities. Agency arises between learner, task, and classroom community.

Students who have not experienced themselves as successful in school sometimes build confidence by practicing agency elsewhere: in work, sports, caregiving, or creative pursuits. That practice can support academic engagement; that's how powerful agency is for the individual. Often the life of agency is happening somewhere off-campus, a job, babysitting siblings, their own interest pursuits, and schools can either recruit it or punish it.

## Genres of agency

In this section I map seven recurring genres of agency that I've noted across school contexts: four generative forms (engaged, epistemic, democratic, and authorial) and three shadows that differ in kind. Dysfunctional agency is the non-example; pseudo-agency is a decay; effortful agency is constrained, and sometimes useful. Each genre has a signature relationship to its school model's chronotope of culture, power, and historical sediment[2]: its rhythms, its authority, its definitions of legitimacy. Students may resist, survive, or thrive depending on how we set those conditions.

As I defined it earlier, agency is not the same thing as motivation or autonomy; endorsement does not equal authorship. Each genre is a pattern between learner, task, and classroom, and each exhibits one or more aspects of origination, decision-making, capabilities, and answerability.

The taxonomy is core to the book's purpose, and what is critically different about it is the focus: how student agency *shows up* in the classroom, not deciding whether individuals have or lack these forms. Being able to perceive the forms as they appear in student behaviors, discussions, or writing is what allows a teacher to take appropriate teaching action. Discriminating among them depends on where a move originates and what its object is.

There is a second way to say this, in the vocabulary of a theory I lean on later in the book. Variation Theory calls a lesson's target the object of learning (Marton; Moore-Anderson, *Teaching Meaning*), and my forms sort by their relation to it. Angie was messing around with how a concept worked: engaged, inside the object of learning. Nancy was in a different form of math altogether: epistemic, a different object for the same task. Marcus was driven by his own objects of learning: authorial. And democratic agency returns agency to students outside the object of learning, in the conditions that surround it. The map exists because we need to make sense of students' meaning-making, and meaning-making always has an object.

## The Map

The first three agencies are shadow forms: what agency becomes when students as knowers are chronically unseated. (I focus on what we do have control over, our own classrooms, without suggesting classrooms are the whole or even primary cause of this displacement.) The last four are the forms student agency takes when the object is sense-making or meaning-making.[3] You have already met two of them in Angie and Nancy in Chapter 1.

**Dysfunctional agency** is the non-example that marks the definition's edge. The student *is* showing agency: origination, decisions, often considerable capability. What's misaimed is the object: the move points away from the object of learning, as refusal, disruption, sabotage, or disengagement. The capacity is fully intact, and it works against the student's own education, at least the education offered in the classroom.

**Pseudo-agency** is the appearance of agency without its substance: learning performed without sense-making, with an emphasis on maximal minimal effort. It is the decayed version of effortful agency.

**Effortful agency** is the high achiever doing real work toward goals they never set for themselves and don't care about. It gets good test scores and engages in sense-making, at least in the short term. Classrooms read it as virtue, but it leaves students divorced from their own subjectivity (Matusov, von Duyke, & Kayumova, 2016). And it is sometimes useful: persistence is a real capacity. The trouble comes when it is normalized as learning. Often the object of traditional school models.

**Engaged agency** is the student's effort to understand from their own position, still inside the teacher's lesson frame. It is easily misread, because it often presents in partial, frequently wrong, but authentic ways. Angie in Chapter 1. This form has three outward behavioral aspects, lurking, playing, and geeking out, which we will get to. It is the object of progressive school models.

**Epistemic agency** involves the student reframing the problem, proposing a different question, or making a counterclaim about what counts. It is meaning-making, not just sense-making. Nancy in Chapter 1. This rarely comes up in discussion in educational circles.

**Democratic agency** is student contribution toward the shared conditions and purposes of the classroom or the object of learning. It is voice acting on the world with others, not just voice as choice among limited options. Abundant in democratic-school settings.

**Authorial agency** is the student as author of their own intellectual life: authoring not just what they want to learn but how they want to learn it and how much. Problem posing, not just problem answering. Agents of their own subjectivities. The purpose of democratic school models. Marcus.

Please note that these forms exceed any single classroom's jurisdiction. The point is not to expect every teacher to host all of them, but to consider where an awareness of each might serve your students and your content. Engaged and epistemic agencies take the content as their object, and responding to them is what moves a class beyond delivering a curriculum toward developing the conversations that support students to metabolize meaning. Democratic agency addresses the shared conditions of learning, and classrooms differ in the affordances they offer for it. Authorial agency centers on the self across the school setting; your classroom may or may not meet a student on that journey, but a whole school might. These forms are bigger than one course, one teacher, one year. Awareness of them, for teachers and for students, opens possibilities.

## Shadow forms of agency

### The zombification of student agency

When we attempt to solve disengagement through motivation, we fail to ask the prior question: what is happening in classrooms that disengages?

Teachers, parents, and students have long sensed that something about schooling can feel off, and a vast body of research confirms their intuition. The institution designed to open the world to students can also stifle their eagerness to explore it. Alienating students from their own knowing can paralyze their capacity for purposeful action, producing what Llewellyn called zombies and I call the **zombification of student agency**: students whose lives have been sucked out of them while being asked to jump through academic hoops. Or as Miracle Max said, "Mostly dead, but not all dead."[4]

These zombies may be all dead (disengagement), mostly dead (agency sickness), or functionally dead (pseudo-agency). They may still be compliant. They may still be "successful." But their relationship to learning has thinned into performance: doing school without being in school as a knower.

Agency breakdown isn't just a personality problem or a motivation problem; it's often a legibility problem. A student's agency does not disappear. It becomes institutionally illegible, unacceptable, or underdeveloped in terms of academic learning, and is misread as defiance, laziness, distraction, immaturity, or "not motivated." It is agency operating under conditions that make voice, meaning-making, or authorship hard to sustain.

I begin with the three shadow forms because they are so common, so easy to misread, and so often produced by the ordinary pressures of schooling. They are still agency, but they are unproductive for classrooms.

### Dysfunctional agency: protective and hostile forms

What often remains unexamined is whether schooling as constructed, even in the best of possible school worlds, is part of the problem itself.

Freire's banking model of education suggests information is "put" into students' heads the way you put money in a bank; in that analogy, resistance can be read as a response to being treated as a container. Angie's agency resisted alienating conditions. In my own 2016 paper, I bracketed resistance and non-normative agency as non-agency. I've come to see that dysfunctional agency extends past that earlier framework: agency is taken when students are displaced as knowers, and it goes somewhere. Disengaged agency looks like the student consistently putting their head down on entering the classroom; being on their phone, gaming, shopping, watching movies, texting, sometimes FaceTiming. More hostile actions include talking loudly in class and arguing about the right to do so, starting fights, and sabotaging classroom resources while remaining committed to not learning.

This is what I mean by institutional legibility. When a student's agency doesn't fit the time-world of school, it becomes institutionally illegible or pathologized: invisible to teachers, uncountable by the system. Disengaged agency resists the classroom most directly, but other dysfunctional forms *seem* functional while being adaptations to dysfunctional conditions. It's performance learning, which, you have to admit, compared to a classroom dominated by dysfunctional behaviors, is pretty darned exciting.

### Pseudo-agency: the illusion of engagement

Pseudo-agency is the appearance of engagement without its substance. It isn't effortful; it's calibrated to be as low-effort as the student can get away with. Marcus sometimes slipped into pseudo-agency, turning in the bare minimum to stay under the radar, but his real energy was elsewhere. Nancy, by contrast, resisted pseudo-agency by reframing the task; her teacher just couldn't see it.

Priya kept talking during my lesson. Not disruptively, just steadily. When I finally said, "If you're talking, you're not thinking about the lesson, and that's what concerns me," she said, "Well, I hear you, so I don't know why this is a problem." She was taking notes, looking at my slides; she always turned in all of her work. Other students looked up in surprise: why was I questioning her learning?

I checked in with her after the lesson to see whether she was really that remarkable at multitasking, and no. She had no idea what the lesson was about. I had a moment of sudden daylight: Priya actually seemed to believe that hearing something was the same thing as learning it. When we moved on to the follow-up work, she gave it equal weight with her ongoing social conversation, copying enough into her worksheet to produce visible proof she was on task while staying tuned to the gossip her friend was sharing. Hearing the lesson, turning in the paper: both, by her lights, counted as doing school. It did not matter where the words came from: my slides, a neighbor, AI. Mentally engaging with the object of learning was not the point. Visible proof, as a proxy for engagement, was.

Priya was not trying to be difficult. She believed she was learning, having spent years in a system that rewards looking like a learner over being one. And to be honest, how many of us have thought that hearing a term was the same thing as learning it? She was not performing engagement cynically; she had internalized her classroom performance so completely that she experienced it as the way learning occurs. Conveniently, she was so unengaged with the learning that she could also maintain a long, complicated, newsy dialogue with her friend.

That is how we get what I call **pseudo-agency**: performative studenting without learning. Students perform the rituals of learning but never make real contact with the ideas, and over time they learn to comply with surface demands while disengaged internally. Not through one bad teacher or one bad year, but through the accumulated experience of schooling that consistently rewards the performance of learning over the thing itself. This is especially prevalent in high-poverty schools, as we will see. And when pseudo-agency becomes the norm, when whole classrooms operate in it, teachers reward it, and walk-through administrators are happy with it, it creates a culture that confirms everyone's belief.

Pseudo-agency students remind me of the patients in Oliver Sacks's *Awakenings*. His patients had lost the neurological capacity to initiate action; they could respond to external cues, a ball thrown, a song played, but could not begin movement on their own. The analogy captures the reactive quality of pseudo-agency: reactive paper production, not generative learning.

Pseudo-agency is the predictable outcome of systems that prioritize coverage over meaning, pacing over mastery, memorization over reasoning, and standardization over curiosity. One antidote is teaching with complexity: richer questions, authentic inquiry, real cognitive struggle. But complexity collapses under the logic of pacing. When teachers are pressured to cover curriculum and students are rewarded for compliance, both parties collude, consciously or not, in a charade of learning.

Pseudo-agency often looks like success: on-task behavior, turned-in assignments, decent grades. What it represents is the substitution of completion for comprehension. Students come to see themselves as doers, not knowers, and when intellectual curiosity shuts down, the very cognitive growth all that effort is meant to stimulate lies dormant.

Christian Moore-Anderson, watching the same thing in his own science classroom, calls it skilful coping: students can skilfully cope with a task and still not learn what we hoped, because they are good at learning the contextual cues that produce the right words at the right time, and task completion can resemble understanding, especially when every student is expected to give the same answer (*Teaching Meaning*). Priya was coping skilfully.

### Effortful agency: autonomy constrained, agency under surveillance

Now the most institutionally rewarded form: **effortful agency**, the kind that keeps classrooms orderly and students busy. It is often mistaken for the whole of motivation. It is often encouraged through extrinsic rewards.

My son Ben and his friends in middle school grew tired of the endless stream of external rewards: tickets, candy, prizes dangled in front of students to motivate "good behavior." Tired of the manipulation and the behaviors it sought to produce, the boys began forging bootleg tickets and using their counterfeits to fund small student-run enterprises: hand-knitted scarves, homemade jewelry, snacks. In other words, they rewarded authentically chosen activities over being manipulated into activities the teacher wanted. Agency can emerge even under restrictive systems; it just doesn't always emerge in sanctioned ways.

Students who are consistently unseated from their knowing develop workarounds. They learn to read what the teacher wants and produce it, without necessarily understanding it, decoding the hidden question beneath the teacher's question: not *how do I make sense of this problem?* but *what does the teacher want me to say?* They participate in the forms of learning, discussion, note-taking, answering questions, without making personal meaning of any of it, performing the cognitive labor of translating their thoughts into teacher-expected outcomes.

Matusov, von Duyke, and Kayumova (2016) coined effortful agency: a mode of action grounded in compliance, persistence, and self-discipline within predefined boundaries. These students color-code their notes, never disrupt class, memorize and regurgitate, and push through confusion. Their effort is admirable, sometimes heroic.

Educators love effortful agency. A former school administration of mine wanted to increase learning efforts, so they showed a clip from a football movie: exhausted players sweating, running, then crawling, forcing the ball into the end zone for the winning score. I remember thinking that no student should, or could, live like that every day to learn. It was an example of what I call **effort porn**, revealing more about what educators fantasize teenagers should do than about what adolescents actually need.

Effortful agency shows initiative, but there is an ethical question at its core. As Howard Blumenthal asked in a recent conversation on education: if students spend their entire lives learning only what others assign, when do they get to define themselves through learning? (Hargadon, 2021.)

The psychological costs can be high. Research on valedictorians, the ultimate effortful achievers, suggests they are no more successful, and often less fulfilled, than their peers after graduation. I remember running into a former high school valedictorian working full-time at Staples. I expected her to be doing great things in the world by now; instead she told me she did not know what she wanted to do with her life, and until she did, she needed to pay the bills. Barker (2017) makes the point bluntly: the students who perform best on the system's terms are not, as a group, the ones who go on to change the world. They have become experts at the game without developing a reason of their own for playing it.

Effortful agency is a particularly U.S. virtue; the valorization of relentless striving has roots in Weber's Protestant ethic, shows up plainly in Millennial hustle culture, and is increasingly rejected by the more sensitive and artistic Gen Z'ers. In effortful agency the student becomes a self-managing ideal worker: constantly evaluating, adjusting, and optimizing themselves to complete teacher tasks, rarely pausing to ask why the tasks should matter to them. Anderson and Winthrop (2025) name this same pattern Achiever Mode: external goals internalized without being integrated into any coherent sense of self.

At its core, effortful agency is agency under surveillance: the appearance of self-direction sustained by external expectation. Schools that prioritize effort sometimes aestheticize struggle itself. Effort can keep classrooms functioning, and it builds real capacities for persistence. But when schools treat effort as the only available form of agency, they reward discipline while leaving meaning-making, and the student's developing self, thin.

It is worth pausing on the relationship between pseudo-agency and effortful agency, since the two often look alike. Effortful agency is the student who is heroically compliant, who works hard at whatever the teacher assigns. Pseudo-agency sits on the same continuum but further from learning: effortful agency still concerns itself with learning, while pseudo-agency only resembles it, sometimes even to the student. Priya gives us the diffuse, easygoing version; the valedictorian gives us the disciplined one. What they share is the loss of contact with their own thinking as the object of school. Why these forms are so prevalent, particularly in high-poverty schools, is diagnosed in the chapters ahead.

## Engaged agency

### Meaningfulness within structure

Effortful agency describes disciplined action under imposed goals. Engaged agency is participation that means something to the student, inside a shared structure. Students don't merely comply; they shape their experience of the work while staying in relationship to the task, the teacher, and the classroom norms. In its strictest sense it is Deci and Ryan's identified regulation.

Remember Heidi, who wanted her art teacher to "show us a skill, maybe a few examples, and then just let us play"? That's engaged agency. She wasn't rejecting instruction; she wanted guidance with space, to learn from someone more experienced while still expressing her own creative ideas. She's not dissimilar to Angie.

Engaged agency is not freedom *from* structure. It is agency exercised *inside* structure, when the classroom ecology legitimates students acting on instruction with their own purposes as it unfolds. Students name what isn't landing, request a different representation, practice new information in their own way, propose a method, or redirect attention toward a question that matters to them, all without leaving the lesson behind. Reeve and Tseng's (2011) concept of agentic engagement names the observable behaviors students use to contribute to instruction as it unfolds: asking, proposing, requesting, resisting, redirecting. Resistance being an important signal to the teacher that their lesson is not flying.

And Patall (2024) offers a detailed account of engaged agency and the conditions under which students experience it: classroom activities that are relevant, self-endorsed, and worth caring about. She discusses the importance of each in autonomy-supportive teaching. Patall's caveat is: autonomy support does not land evenly. Students' prior experiences of success, belonging, and recognition shape their ability to use autonomy supports at all. Those who have felt competent and recognized elsewhere often thrive in autonomy-supportive classrooms; those who have not may need real scaffolding before they can act independently. That's a cautionary warning to think about.

Engaged agency takes three distinct forms as learning progresses.

### The shape of early engagement

#### 1 · The frame: learning we can't recognize

Oregon Trail. Two education professors once observed a nine-year-old boy at an after-school program interacting with *The Oregon Trail*. The student seemed to click around randomly, glance around, doodle, and comment to friends. One professor concluded the child was mindlessly pressing buttons and needed instruction. Two days later, on his own, the second professor came across the same child playing with focus and proficiency. Within weeks he was highly competent.

The paradox isn't that the child learned quickly. It's that a trained education professor couldn't recognize learning while it was happening, because it didn't look like the way engagement is expected to occur in a classroom.

When children learn a new computer program, they rarely begin by reading the manual. They watch others, get a feel for what the program does, and then poke around: testing buttons, discovering limits, figuring out which actions produce which effects. This is how learning often unfolds: exposure, partial understanding, exploration, coherence, transfer, and sometimes invention. Children tolerate the anxiety inherent in learning, and their own early clumsiness, when conditions allow low stakes, time to linger, social safety, and a sense of play. Over time competence builds, and eventually understanding transfers: they learn new programs faster, customize tools, and are sought out by others as "experts".

In school, we often punish the behaviors that support engagement but precede competence.

#### 2 · The three models each misread early engagement

Every school model has a theory of engagement. Traditional schools believe effort precedes interest: work hard enough and understanding arrives, with interest following. Progressive schools believe interest precedes effort: give students real choice and interesting work and they will engage willingly. Democratic schools believe self-direction precedes both: let students own their learning entirely and effort and interest take care of themselves.

Each theory has something true in it, and when engagement breaks down, each reaches for its signature tool: more structure, more choice, or more freedom. What none of them fully reckons with is that engagement doesn't arrive whole. It develops through phases, and the phases don't look like learning to eyes trained to find outputs.

#### 3 · Ito's phases: a language for the evolution of engagement

In *Hanging Out, Messing Around, and Geeking Out* (2010), Mizuko Ito and her colleagues give us a language for these phases. They tracked young people's participation in voluntary, social, youth-driven learning contexts and found a recurring pattern in how interest deepens in the wild. "In the wild" is important, because classroom students are captive and often have to structure for engagement differently.[5]

Ito's "agency genres," as she calls them, name the student's observable behaviors at each phase and how each functions in building understanding. The phases are not developmentally sequential; a learner can be lurking in one aspect of a domain while messing around in another. They name an iterative process from orientation toward expertise. I don't list them as genres of agency in my taxonomy because, for my purposes, they sit inside engaged agency and add granularity to it. And the point of the phases is worth saying plainly: engagement changes the form of agency that gets expressed. Lurk to geek is the same kid.

#### 4 · Lurking

Behaviorally it looks like students are present but not yet visibly participating. They hover near the action and language of a domain, watching, listening, absorbing. In school this looks like disengagement, because it produces no visible effort or talk. But Ito shows that this phase often marks the beginning of real orientation: What is this activity? Who belongs? What kinds of participation are possible? What is allowed and what is not?

As a homeschooling parent, I watched my younger child learn to read simply by playing near the phonics lessons of their older siblings. They got all the information without any pressure of evaluation. And a Montessori colleague told me about a student who refused reading lessons for three years in the multi-year 1st–3rd grade classroom typical of the method. This student always sat nearby, turned away, playing with something, quiet. Then one day he simply picked up a book and read. His teacher had been concerned, but was sensitive enough, as a progressive teacher, to note that something valuable was happening in all that adjacent sitting.

Lurking violates the rules of legibility in traditional schools: we expect thinking to be shown on paper and engagement to be verbally demonstrated. Silent proximity is a precondition for further learning. Like the silent period in early language acquisition, quiet is not disinterest; it is intense interest. The issue isn't whether learners are peripheral. It's whether their peripherality is treated as legitimate (cf. Lave and Wenger) or assumed to be a form of disengagement in need of correcting.

When a student's lurking is not seen as legitimate, they can be redirected in ways that pull their attention away from the very learning that is underway. Or, as teachers are often instructed, to assign collaborative roles, which means lurking students are expected to talk before they feel ready.

#### 5 · Messing around

Behaviorally, participation becomes playful and exploratory. Learners poke at the learning thing, trying approaches, testing limits, learning through low-stakes failure. Messing around depends on time, access, room for wrong moves, and help on demand: conditions schools frequently fail to provide. It is a form of play, and like all play it lowers the anxiety of learning (Peter Gray).

The knee-jerk reaction of many teachers is: stop fooling around, or you're off task, without realizing these stop signals are aimed at exactly the behaviors we want. When students explore and fiddle with the materials they are using, say in a lab, they are engaging. Recently I introduced my class to microscope work, with no lesson. Inevitably students fiddled with dials and lights, tried to see a piece of their own hair, then tried to look at anything they could find; we had to bring out a stereoscope and a scalpel to see specimens in different ways. This is perfect. A lot of learning happens without a lot of direction or speech. The same goes for digital and balance scales, things on my desk, teaching tools. The classroom is for them, not me.

What I find sad these days is how unfamiliar students are with basic materials. Many of my ninth and tenth graders initially don't know how to use a ruler, among other tools. When I asked about their middle school experience, they told me their teachers didn't let them use the rulers because they didn't want them fooling around. The very behavior that signals engagement became a reason to deprive them of the tool altogether. When we take materials out of students' hands to keep a room manageable, we are not only preventing mischief; we are foreclosing the play stage that later abstraction is built on. The result surfaces in my class as a tenth grader who can't use a ruler.

I get the anxiety. We've had our accidents. But students given a chance to fiddle around within reason will eventually settle, because their curiosity will be satisfied as the forbidden thing in their midst is accessible.

Messing around also occurs in dialogue. Students make what seem like off-topic comments, yet these can be a form of play. In museum education, researchers followed a father and daughter talking their way through a heart exhibit. Their conversation was full of off-topic subject matter, connections, and digressions. Yet in following visitors' retention of their learning, those who had conversations like this not only remembered the information; some expressed greater complexity ten years later than in their immediate post-exhibit understanding. People engage most deeply when they are allowed to bring knowledge into the context of their own lives in order to make sense of it: what I earlier called metabolizing meaning.

The part we get wrong is assuming this is something children grow out of. It is not age-bound, and it is not a type of learner. It is typical of any age when material is new.[6] I will never forget my Upper Elementary Montessori training at the Barrie School in Silver Spring, Maryland. We were five women at the lesson with our male teacher. We had all taken geometry in high school, yet here we were, giggling and gushing over the geometry materials used in problem solving. They let us physically reshape solids into the forms the equations describe, and feel where the volume formula comes from. We all said, "Oh, *now* I understand."

Never did one man make so many women so happy at one time.

I am the same way with new technology. I have to get my hands on it first. I learned how to build AI agents while writing this book: I need to see what a thing does first, and only then do I read the directions, and only the parts I need. It is not hands-on in the literal sense, but it is embodied; I have to act in the thing before the abstraction about it means anything. And typically I learn best by first applying it to my personal life, like creating an agent to grocery shop for me. Seen this way, a tenth grader handling a ruler for the first time and fooling around is not regressing to a childish stage. He is apprehending something new in a natural progression.

This is why I have come to distrust the language of learning styles, which has fallen out of favor for good reason.[7] When we call a student a "kinesthetic learner," we name as a fixed trait what Ito described as an orientation: where he currently stands in relation to this material. A style, if it were real, would be fixed; Ito's work shows how the mode changes as understanding grows.

**Malik: the scene.** Malik's story shows the phases coexisting.

Malik was a biology student who usually came in, lay across a lab counter, and went to sleep, or watched movies on his device. One thing I did notice: when he was awake, he fiddled with anything he could get his hands on. My pen got taken apart and reassembled. A rubber band got stretched and snapped. Lab material got examined from several angles before being used for usually unintended purposes.

So one day I had the Forensics class's materials out on a side table: a test for blood, and a simulation I'd built for blood spatter analysis. I typically cross-seed my students' interests this way. Malik came in, looked at the table as he walked by, then flopped on his favorite counter. I followed him and said, "Do you want to test for blood like a CSI?" He lifted his head. Most kids have seen CSI shows; in that sense they've already lurked.

He was up in thirty seconds, at the table, examining the materials with focused attention. I showed him briefly what each did and left him alone. No paperwork to fill out.

While he worked on the blood test simulation, I continued my ecology lesson with the rest of the class: energy pyramids, the transfer of matter and energy through an ecosystem, the vocabulary that names how a hawk and a mouse and a blade of grass are all part of the same energy economy at different levels. Malik, at the back table with his blood spatter kit, listened.

I didn't know that until later, when I brought a card activity over to him after my lecture: a set of physical materials I'd made that let students build an energy pyramid by placing organisms in the right positions, using the lesson's vocabulary to label each placement. Malik laid the cards out without asking for help, named several positions correctly, and caught many of his own mistakes. When I came over I pointed to where something didn't fit the definitions; he reworked the arrangement twice until I said it was complete. He had been lurking the entire time, tracking the lesson from the counter, absorbing the vocabulary. Letting him mess around with something else had also let him lurk in on my lesson, then be ready to try the materials until they worked. Like the Montessori non-reader, he was learning; it just didn't look like it.

**School models misread lurking and messing around.** The traditional model misreads the student who needs to lurk before engaging, and offers more structure and accountability. The progressive model misreads the lurker, the student fiddling at the edge of a lab, the off-topic connector who needs to mess around before exploration means anything. The democratic model, to its credit, supports children in lurking and playing; it places a high value on both. Watching older children at play is complex and interesting, and so limited in most schools. Yet when democratic educators do provide a requested lesson, they are just as likely to teach it with the same misreadings as the other models. More about school models in the next chapter.

#### 6 · Geeking out

Behaviorally this is where interest develops in dialogue and writing, and finally looks like expected outcomes. Learners seek competence because the domain has begun to matter to them. They pursue mentors, tolerate frustration, refine skills, adopt standards of practice, and determinedly use the vocabulary in their speech and writing. Identity starts to form: someone who knows something about this domain.

Students who have actually worked their learning out tend to share it without being asked; new knowledge wants to be expressed. Peer transmission is one of the most powerful learning mechanisms in a classroom, but only if it is not forced. It comes not as dialogue roles assigned before students are ready, but as the natural overflow of understanding when someone finally has something to say.

A great example is one of my sons-in-law, a carpenter who works much of his day alone. He loves history and politics, so he listens to podcasts and audiobooks about the presidents, capitalism, progressivism, all day. We have come to treat him as the one to check our historical facts on presidents, or to define political terms. He is becoming someone known for knowing something. When he comes home he can't wait to talk about what he has been listening to and thinking about.

Lurking asks: *What's going on?* Messing around asks: *How does this work?* Geeking out asks: *Why does it work this way, and can I explain it to you?*

That sequence matters, because high school routinely demands behavior more like geeking out from students who have never been given time to lurk or mess around. We want students to geek out; yet we cut off the phases that make geeking out possible. We are promoting anti-engagement in practice.

One bridge before moving on. Engaged agency fails in classrooms for students like Angie, who *is* engaged, just not the way the teacher wants her to be: *be engaged in what I'm telling you, not in what comes from your own thinking.* What engaged and epistemic agency ask of a teacher's perception, and of the follow-up move, differ; that difference is where we go next.

## Epistemic agency

### Reshaping knowledge

Partially correct answers, students' "not-yet" understandings, matter enormously for teaching and for engaged agency, but they are not what I mean by epistemic agency.

**Epistemic agency** shows up when a student reframes what counts as the task, what counts as a solution, or what kind of knowledge is being demanded. It is interpretive power: the learner doesn't only work within the knowledge assigned; the learner reshapes what counts as knowledge in this classroom, and sometimes the direction of the lesson itself.[8] Unlike engaged agency, which builds meaning through participation in the teacher's lesson, epistemic agency enacts meaning through reinterpretation of that lesson. You saw this in Nancy's coin flowers in Chapter 1.

Epistemic agency is also a skill, and one a person can learn to love. I did. Many people hit it without ever being supported to carry it through. It's funny: I never thought I was smart or a good student, because my brain always wandered to other possibilities in school. I remember being told in eighth grade that Roman numerals had to be changed because you couldn't do math with them, and I spent the rest of the class trying to work out the four operations in Roman numerals. Could you add? Where exactly did it break? Like Nancy's teacher, mine looked at my work, grunted, and moved on.

## Democratic agency

### Influencing the context

Where epistemic agency reframes meaning within a structure, **democratic agency** turns interpretive power outward, toward the shared conditions of learning in the classroom community itself. Democratic agency is shared authorship of the rules of the game: students help shape norms, procedures, and consequences, and learn to live with one another through deliberation and accountability.

One of my doctoral research sites was The Circle School (TCS), a democratic school on the East Coast now approaching its fourth decade. At TCS, students choose what to learn, whether to learn, and with whom. Their civil liberties are codified in a constitutional school document, and they vote on issues ranging from schedules to discipline to budgets and staff hiring. Children as young as four deliberate alongside teenagers in school meetings. Adults act as stabilizers of the school culture, anchoring norms and modeling judgment without dictating how learning unfolds. The school's approach is described more fully in the next chapter.

## Authorial agency

### Beyond the frame

In effortful, engaged, epistemic, and democratic agency, students are regulating themselves within structures largely framed by adults. **Authorial agency** begins when learners create curricular purposes inside school: when they generate new directions of study and author options rather than choose among them. It extends autonomy beyond selecting from a menu to authoring the menu, within the constraints and opportunities in and outside of a classroom ecology.

I define authorial agency in a bounded way: it begins when learners create curricular purposes for themselves, and I focus on authorial agency that occurs inside school though it isn't limited there. Culturally, we claim to value this deeply; innovation sits at the center of the American narrative. And as artificial intelligence becomes widespread, humans will need to get better at selecting goals worth pursuing, not simply completing tasks efficiently. If students spend their entire lives learning only what others assign, when do they get to define themselves through learning?

Authorial agency stands out in a school setting; outside one, it is ordinary. Almost everyone authors some portion of their life behind the scenes. It appears more readily outside school, in student-led businesses, skilled work, and social media, than within it. In schools it emerges unevenly: through independent study, long projects, mentorship, elective pathways. The teacher functions as curator, coach, or gate-opener rather than director.

There's a greenhouse off my current classroom, long forgotten, until a student, Peter, expressed interest in using it. Over time he moved from plant experiments to entrepreneurial work: growing and selling plants, developing herbal products, and forming a club of other students whom he recruits and leads. He defines his agenda, sets standards for quality, recruits collaborators, and decides the requirements they should meet. Peter's work receives one credit, Teacher's Assistant, though it certainly deserves more; the school has no independent-study pathway. He should receive three credits for special project work, then maintain a portfolio as his ongoing record; but the school's credit structures weren't designed for learning that students author. They were designed for learning that teachers assign. His entrepreneurial learning will live on his transcript's margins.

Authorial agency reminds us that learning is not only about understanding the world but about creating it. It is learning that doesn't easily standardize. Our task as educators is not to direct that impulse but to notice it, make room for it to take form, and get our schools to legitimize and credit it.

Multiple agencies can coexist in a single student's experience. A learner may express effortful agency in one subject and engaged agency in another; may demonstrate democratic agency among peers but retreat from authorial expression in a classroom that treats learning as compliance. The next chapter traces how the different schooling traditions make some genres visible and others invisible.

## Key takeaways: the forms, in the students who showed them

| Form | In one line | The moment that showed it |
|---|---|---|
| Dysfunctional (non-example) | Full agency, aimed away from the object of learning | The phones-out back row; every movie classroom |
| Pseudo (a decay) | The appearance of agency without its substance | Priya's worksheet: visible proof of being on task, no contact with the lesson |
| Effortful (sometimes useful) | Real work toward goals never their own | The valedictorian at Staples: expert at the game, no reason of her own for playing it |
| Engaged | Authentic participation inside the frame | Angie at the chalkboard; Heidi's "show us a skill, then let us play"; Malik lurking from the counter |
| Epistemic | Reshaping the frame | Nancy's coin flower: "evenly" remade as balance instead of a count |
| Democratic | Co-authoring the context | The four-year-old at TCS, deliberating budgets alongside teenagers |
| Authorial | Originating purposes and paths | Marcus's 4 a.m. life; Peter's greenhouse |

The non-example label on dysfunctional agency is doing real work: the student is showing agency, every component present, just not toward the object of learning. That is what makes it diagnostic rather than damning. It matters because the object of learning is never treated neutrally in a classroom: it is elevated to a moral height or vilified, so agency aimed away from it gets read as character rather than as aim. The three shadows differ in kind, not just in shade: a non-example, a decay, and a constrained form with real uses. The four generative rows are what the definition looks like when it lands on the object of learning.

Read the names as moments, not labels. The taxonomy classifies what showed up in the moment. It never classifies the student.

# Chapter 3 — The Three School Models

*Each model polices its teachers as much as its students.*

You met Angie and Nancy in two very different schools, and that difference matters. Angie was in a democratic school; Nancy was in a progressive one. That difference is the reason their agencies could surface where I could see them at all. In a traditional classroom, their thinking would have stayed underground.

Many of us have *heard* of these alternatives: the Montessori school across town, the free school a colleague's child attends, the project-based charter from a conference presentation. What we are rarely taught to do, in teacher preparation or anywhere after, is analyze what each model values: which forms of agency it surfaces, which it buries, and what each choice costs the learner and the teacher.

I present the models as pure forms for the sake of analysis, but no real school is pure. We all borrow pieces of these models, and anyone can mistakenly import a model's failure mode along with its strengths, assuming the strengths will land evenly on all students and all kinds of coursework. They will not. The primary model you teach in also shapes what *you* can see, because a model's language supports you in defining what you see. So this chapter maps the three main school models: what each surfaces and buries, the founding paradox each runs on, and the way each one polices the teacher as much as the student. Rarely does any school follow one model faithfully, whatever the mission statement promises; your school's mileage may vary from the extracted forms I present here.

## Traditional schooling: agency as a capacity to be delivered

The traditional model optimizes for what Scott (1998) called **legibility**:[1] rendering learning into standardized, countable, comparable outputs. A fixed curriculum, segmented time, standardized assessments; a teacher who delivers the curriculum and a student who receives it. Its roots are in Enlightenment-era values: every child, regardless of background, gets access to the same body of knowledge in order to participate in democratic society. Those values are part of why traditional schooling persists. Corporations would also like to offload training onto state funding, and parents want their children to have a leg up in life via diplomas, but the founding aspiration was the cultivated citizen.

Classical liberal education, the *artes liberales*, was the education befitting a free person.[2] It aimed at a flexible, independent intellect with the capacity to think for oneself; content was grist for that cultivation, not the endpoint. It is close to Freire's stance that all students begin as knowers, not empty vessels to fill, and it understood that knowledge has no endpoint. Thinking was held to be foundational to a democratic society. But it was, from the start, an education for the wealthy leisured class: for those with the standing and free time to be cultivated, at a time when democracy transparently meant a slice of society rather than all of it.

When that ideal was universalized into mass public schooling, the *rhetoric* of cultivation survived; for most students, the cultivation of the self did not. The curriculum, not the person, became the center. Standardized transmission now stands as a proxy both for education and for equity, while intellectual cultivation stays concentrated where cultural privilege already is. School tax money is never distributed evenly.

Nussbaum's capabilities approach attempts to revive the classical intent: liberty is not rights on paper; individuals must be positioned to exercise their rights, which means sufficient standing and income to have a voice.[3] Equity support becomes *internal* to becoming a rational thinker. But in the shell game of educational terms, the cultivated individual gets traded for future economic capability. I take this up in Chapter 11.

It helps to see *public* traditional education as contested ground between three modern goals. Labaree (1997) argues that public schooling is pulled in three incompatible directions: **democratic equality** (preparing citizens for shared civic life through equal access to a common curriculum), **social efficiency** (producing the workers an economy needs), and **social mobility** (giving individuals a credential to get ahead).[6] The first wants a shared, equalizing experience. The second wants service to the GDP. The third wants sortable, competitive difference.

Democratic equality has motivated a school system meant to be not only public but equitable, a goal highly contested in the Trump era. Special-needs students are provided more services; public schools are held to a common set of standards. Outcomes-based learning and No Child Left Behind draw on this goal, and it was the argument behind Brown v. Board of Education, support for LGBTQ+ youth, and bilingual education. As Anyon (1980) showed, though, the outcomes of social efficiency are not evenly distributed: some schools prepare students to be executives, others for middle management, still others for service jobs, and the neighborhood school you attend predicts your future employment. This has prompted parents to find ways, or the political means, to choice their children into schools in better neighborhoods; to establish charters that allow greater exclusion (equal in the front door, unequal through the back door of expulsion); and to flee to private schools offering tutorial and Socratic environments. To misquote *Animal Farm*: some schools are more equal than others. The third goal echoes the Enlightenment aspiration of rational, autonomous, self-actualized adults, and in public schools it often decays toward credentialism and career-pathway options.

Private, alternative, religious, and charter schools pull these goals toward one value or another: to afford the decay, to avoid equity, or to change the method of delivery. Some are founded in opposition to the values themselves, as instruments of racial, immigrant, or identity segregation; some to promote methods that are project-based, progressive, Socratic, or real-world in nature.

Kant named the contradiction the traditional model runs on: how shall I cultivate freedom under conditions of compulsion?[7] Compulsion first, freedom after seems to make sense; anyone who has had a two-year-old understands that children do not arrive as rational planners. It seems to make sense, that is, until you think of agency as developmental.

Here is the model's deeper cost. Focused on outcomes, it lacks language for **origination**: a student's effort to understand from their own perception of the world, their own thinking as a knower, their own differences with the teacher, their own curricular ends. Without the language, these things become unintelligible, or at least unimportant. And that lack of language effectively polices teachers into an orientation toward outcomes. The traditional model sees student agency as a capacity to be built *for later*: first you receive the standardized curriculum; then, as an adult, you get economic power with it. This is the orientation that unseated Angie's knowing, and its characteristic pitfall is what Bakhtin called **monologism**:[5] knowledge arrives finished, and a student's working model is an error to be corrected rather than a beginning to be explored.

Marcus's story is one of the reasons I felt I needed to write this book. In my biology class, Marcus was well-behaved; if quiet disconnection counts as good behavior. When he wasn't asleep, he at least wasn't disruptive. He simply had little interest, if not zero, in my biology assignments. So one day I approached him in a friendly way and asked plainly, "Why aren't you interested in any of my assignments?"

"I don't care about biology," he said. I flinched, recovered, and asked, "What do you care about?" As he talked, a different Marcus came into view: not a disengaged student but a purposeful young man. He was getting up at 4 a.m. to work out. He was researching stocks, making significant money at it, and building small business ventures. He had a fierce drive for mastery, just not the kind his school wanted him to show. By any psychological measure Marcus was motivated, disciplined, purposeful. Within school, his motivation did not align with institutional goals, so he appeared disengaged or even resistant. Motivation can be strong and still be institutionally illegible. The traditional regime reads output, not source; it has no routine use for the question "What do you care about?" when there is no official artifact to read. Marcus displayed what Chapter 2 defined as authorial agency.

When I suggested to administrators that an independent study could put Marcus's accomplishments on his high school transcript, because on his GPA alone a college would not see him as an attractive candidate, I was told I was *trying to save him*. The phrase accused me of a personal crusade, a missionary stance toward this student. I felt gaslit: I was being labeled as someone who had lost professional distance, while the administrator labeled him lazy, unmotivated, and immature, finalizing him as a student not quite worth anyone's time. Marcus did eventually graduate with a good GPA, because he later realized college was something he wanted. It was not hard for him to achieve once he had a personal reason.

Unlike Marcus, Priya, whom you met in the last chapter, performed school without actually learning. This model often rewards the faking and erases real origination at the same time.

To be fair, the traditional model persists because it holds real goods: relatively low cost, sufficient structure for safety, teachers who often care, direct teaching through curated content. These have their place for some students and for some content; even Marcus thought his English and math courses had value to him. Had Marcus gone to a democratic school, he might have gained in origination but lost on academic scaffolding. Hold that trade-off; it is the hinge of this chapter.

## Progressive schooling: agency as constructed experience

The progressive model, historically most associated with Dewey and Montessori, optimizes for experience meant to support origination and autonomy: active learning, inquiry, projects, choice-making, the classroom as a laboratory of democracy.[8] It is built to cultivate engaged agency, students making sense of real phenomena rather than receiving inert facts. Just as Angie wanted to work out the division of fractions without the jingle, Dewey argued that learning had to begin where knowledge itself begins. In *The Child and the Curriculum* (1902) he wrote that the curriculum "must be restored to the experience from which it has been abstracted. It needs to be psychologized; turned over, translated into the immediate and individual experiencing within which it has its origin and significance." Dewey understood that the procedural form of dividing fractions is shorthand for getting the right answer, and that the shorthand does not illustrate what is meant by it.

What Dewey saw is that students need the opportunity for **sense-making**, the substrate of engaged agency. This is why John's students stayed engaged even on a day the lesson wasn't doing that work: most days, their sense-making is satisfied, so one opaque lesson did not tip them into disengagement. **Meaning-making** goes further. It begins from one's own perceptual standpoint, taking a position toward the idea while staying answerable to the subject under study.[4] The student is still focused on the teacher's content but has another way of seeing it; this is the ground on which epistemic agency can begin. Nancy is the clean case. Her classmates asking *who gets the extra penny?* were sense-making; Nancy, giving the problem an aesthetic order, had crossed into meaning-making.

That is where the progressive model finds its own paradox. Not compulsion-now-for-freedom-later, but *individually constructed knowledge aimed at standardized endpoints*. Students build, manipulate, and explore, but toward the answer the teacher already holds; their construction funnels to a preset destination. John felt something important in Nancy's work, as he told me later, but he had no language to articulate to himself what it might be. So he corrected her and moved on. Because progressivism values sense-making, it lacks the language for epistemic bids, even though these teachers, often more attuned to their students, regularly *sense* them.

The progressive model is the one I align with most, inspired by Dewey, Holt, and Montessori, so my sharpest example of progressive policing is one I did to myself, and to my own fourth-grade child. I kept pressing my oldest son, Brian, to use manipulatives in math, certain the hands-on way was the only true sign of understanding. He finally had to tell me I was frustrating him: the manipulatives slowed him down. The orthodoxy was so much mine that I had stopped seeing the learner in front of me, even though he was my own child, and served the method instead. The progressive police lived in my own head, coupled with a traditional education of my own that had taught me knowledge has known endpoints.

There is another blind spot in progressivism, and it is an equity issue. Progressive education assumes the kind of cultural capital Lareau calls concerted cultivation, and even among children who have it, some are simply more academically interested during their school years than others. Because the model values autonomy while holding known endpoints, the expectation (mine included, once) is that given sufficient freedom and material, students will become academically interested and proficient at reaching those endpoints. On the axis running from origination to academic scaffolding, the students who need direct instruction or other scaffolds can be left to themselves. Autonomy without scaffolding can produce inequity. We will later look at Patall's work, and at a study of Philadelphia library branches, to see how autonomy and resources alone are insufficient for developing engagement even in what students choose to learn.

I was discussing these matters recently with a family friend, Mike, who saw this from the inside of the progressive school he attended. "Only a slice of kids really succeeded in that environment," he said: the ones who came in with a strong bent toward academic learning. He and many classmates floundered. The positive, he added, is that all of them became far better at exercising autonomy than the public-school students he later observed.

Yong Zhao, in *World Class Learners* (2012), argues the mirror image about the traditional model: a standards-and-testing regime can teach only what is prescribed, so students talented or curious outside it never get the chance to discover those capacities. Countries whose schools are less authoritarian, he notes, show greater creativity and entrepreneurship. Right diagnosis; but as we will see, "more freedom" is not by itself the cure.[15]

So, like the traditional model, the progressive model holds real goods: the development of autonomy and confidence, the psychologizing of learning. Its downside is that the endpoint disposition gets smuggled in as an entry condition. The traditional model suppresses origination; naive progressivism presupposes it; neither *cultivates* it. For Marcus, a progressive school would probably have left him still sleeping at his desk, with administrators sympathetic to my concerns rather than gaslighting me, and the same outcome for Marcus.

## Democratic schooling: agency as self-governance

The democratic model, of which the Sudbury schools are the clearest American form, optimizes for individual freedom through self-governance: students set the rules, choose what to learn, and share real authority over the conditions of school life. Its philosophical anchor is that students are *already* motivated, and the adult's job is to protect the conditions under which that motivation can run its own course. Greenberg distinguishes democratic from progressive education by observing that the progressive teacher who spots a child's first glimmer of interest and rushes over with books is a "turn-off to curiosity" (Greenberg, 1985). It is also the kind of place where Angie's thinking could surface and her friends could gather around a chalkboard, because they were not obliged to be somewhere else.

The American democratic form developed from two intertwined strands. A libertarian branch ran through the UK: Homer Lane's Little Commonwealth (1913), then A. S. Neill's Summerhill (1921), with emotional well-being first and optional class attendance, but no student-authored curriculum. An anarchist branch began with Francesc Ferrer's Modern School (Barcelona, 1901), which crossed to the US as the Ferrer Modern School and closed in 1953; it had a *directed* curriculum that was non-coercive in method. The free-school wave of the 1960s and 70s, catalyzed by John Holt's *Growing Without Schooling* newsletter, led to the decisive American turn, both non-directive and student-governed: Sudbury Valley School (1968, Daniel Greenberg and colleagues), with no classes or set subjects, town-meeting governance, formal law with a student judiciary, and "staff" rather than "teachers." The student is treated as the **auctor**, literally the originator, of their own learning; what I called **authorial autonomy**: control over what to learn, for how long, and to what standard (von Duyke, 2013). In this model learning is, as it so often is in life, largely incidental and emergent: through the school meeting, judicial committees, requested classes, tutorials, watching peers. They like to quote Dewey: "education is life."

The democratic critique of the other models runs like this: they fail to distinguish autonomy as a psychological good in one's life from autonomy in the classroom, and real classroom autonomy must include the choice *not* to partake of any of the options. Autonomy cannot be said to exist without the freedom to decline. But educational models in general, the democratic one included, fail at a further distinction: autonomy-as-disposition (real, transferable, valuable regardless of its object) versus its academic outworking, which may need scaffolding. In practice the democratic model tends not to include the freedom to *ask for* extrinsic structure within its account of autonomy.

That leaves Sudbury-model schooling with its own paradox: maximum freedom can undermine autonomy. Withholding structure in the name of freedom leaves the student to direct their own learning without a curator, and the same self-direction that would liberate a Marcus can leave other students feeling bereft. At the Sudbury-model school I researched, middle schoolers would leave the school for a year, giving up their freedom to acquire more scaffolding for their learning, and then return to regain their autonomy and use the new scaffolding at a direction and pace they found valuable. What does it tell us when children must exit a model of freedom in order to get what their learning needs? Self-direction works best for students who arrive with unusual focus, or already fluent in the cultural capital the model assumes.[9] It is not a universal good you can pour into any classroom.[10] Autonomy support, as Patall's work shows, is conditional; it most benefits those who already have footing. Freedom without sufficient support can reproduce the very inequity it was meant to level. And Nancy's epistemic bid would probably have gone unread here too: the Sudbury school in my research held that staff have no duty to teach, and her flower arrangement would have been read as play.

I learned how tightly the democratic model polices its own adults in person. The chalkboard conversation with Angie and her friends, the one I opened this book with, leaned hard into the borders of the model's value system. Staff took me aside and said I had skated dangerously close to *coercive teaching*. I had assigned nothing, graded nothing, required no one to stay; the students had drifted over on their own and stayed because the conversation interested them. But in a school organized around non-interference, an adult who draws students into an inquiry they did not initiate is suspect by definition. The very moment a student was showing me how her engaged agency had been squashed was, inside that model's value system, a transgression.

## What the three have in common

Watch what happened in each story. A lack of language can hide the fact that alternatives are possible rather than foreclosed, and social policing then relocates the structural absence into a pathology of the person:[11] I was *coercive* in the democratic school; I was *trying to save him* in the traditional one; and in my progressive homeschool room I policed myself with the ideology of a method. Each model polices its teachers as much as its students. That is why the models must be understood as structural: teachers act inside constraints that reward some moves and punish others, and a missing vocabulary is itself a form of policing.

Notice, too, the identical relocation onto the person.[12] The administration did not admit the institution had no path for Marcus; I was accused of trying to save him. The democratic staff did not confess the model has no room for drawing a student into inquiry; I had "skated close to coercion." And I did not tell myself this method is not serving Brian; I concluded I was a bad teacher for wanting to teach directly. In each case a structural absence got rewritten as a personal defect. And if the defect is always personal, who is left to question the structure? Each model, in its own way, forces a choice between origination and educational scaffolding, and what goes missing is the intellectual apprenticeship that results when origination and rigor coexist.

Language fails teachers in two distinct ways, and it is worth pulling them apart.

**The word does not exist (absence).** The concept is unavailable, so the phenomenon cannot become an object of attention. John sensed something important in Nancy's penny arrangement, but he had no term for meaning-making versus sense-making, no notion of an epistemic bid, so he could not respond to what he intuited. He corrected her and moved on. The missing word did not just leave her pattern unnamed; it made it unseeable as a bid for a different way of understanding the problem.

**The word exists but is ruled illegitimate (suppression).**[13] The Sudbury staff had ruled an entire register out of bounds: "teach," "curriculum," "intervene," interest-as-a-hook. When Angie and I began our chalkboard inquiry, the only concept available for what I had done was the delegitimizing one, coercive teaching. Off-doctrine views got the same treatment applied to students: ideas that differed from staff's were relabeled "absurdist," implying the students lacked rational thought, and some of those students disengaged from every curricular activity for the rest of their enrollment. Nancy, in her school, was merely "off topic."

Language is powerful because it can make a set of behaviors into a comprehensible concept, and a comprehensible concept is diagnostic and answerable.[14] Naming reorganizes perception: "wait time" let teachers notice the one-second gap they were leaving after questions; "funds of knowledge" let a child's home knowledge register as an asset instead of a deficit. The absent word and the forbidden word produce the same incapacity to act, and both then charge the person, student or teacher, with the gap.

Not every binary is false. But we have to ask of each one: is the trade-off intrinsic, a true either/or, or is it produced by a missing support?[15] It may be that public education cannot afford the teaching power required to support student origination; money is a real limiter, though only in the sense of what political budgets prioritize. Technology is sometimes offered as the answer, but I suspect our sense of what is pedagogically possible is largely independent of it: the chalkboard has been with us since the 1800s, and we have not used two centuries of technical advancement in remarkably different pedagogical ways since. Our understanding of how students learn is still shaped by educational outcomes, and the research literature still mostly describes autonomy and agency as binaries a classroom or a student either has or lacks, their cultivation assigned to home life (Lareau) rather than counted among the responsibilities of schooling.

Chapter 2 gave us names for more classroom thinking like Nancy's and Angie's: engaged, epistemic, democratic, and authorial agency, and the shadow forms that appear when agency is chronically unseated. Teachers who have language for what they intuit in student actions, and for the structures that confine them, can then decide honestly whether they want to teach differently or need to teach somewhere else. What comes next is the why: how the daily machinery of schooling manufactures the shadow forms, and why that pressure lands hardest in high-poverty schools.

# Chapter 4 — The Manufacture of Pseudo-Agency

*The person carrying the blame is carrying the structure's debt.*

## Opening: two first graders

My grandson attended a private school built on exploration and play. In the same period, while accompanying university education majors to an inner-city after-school program, I watched a first grader work through a worksheet on cowboys: vocabulary like spurs and stirrups. I asked if he had ever seen a cowboy movie. He shook his head. Cowboy books? Cowboy museum? Cowboy costume? All no. He was bored as he worked at finishing the worksheet. My grandson, meanwhile, had listened to cowboy stories read aloud, visited a museum, seen a movie, and played cowboy, hands-on, at his school. By the time he was asked to fill out a worksheet, it was fun. The words were already his.

The difference between those two first graders isn't motivation. It isn't intelligence. One had adults and experiences that made the words his before the worksheet arrived; the other was handed the worksheet in place of all of it. Everything this chapter diagnoses is packed into that difference, because the second boy's school will go on handing him worksheets, reading his boredom as a motivation problem, and pacing him through a curriculum built for the first boy.

What makes these forms of agency chronic? What structural pressures in schools make this disconnection nearly inevitable rather than exceptional, and especially prevalent in high-poverty schools? (And yes, phones, pandemic, and poverty play a role; I am only addressing what teachers can change.)

The diagnosis that follows is not only about pseudo-agency. Dysfunctional agency is produced too: refusal and anger are often the rational residue of the same structural conditions, resistance to being treated as a container rather than a character defect (Freire). The chapter's claim is that shadow forms are manufactured by the system and surfaced in the student. I will keep naming structure without absolving or indicting individuals, myself included.

There is a structural contributor that gets far less attention. For two decades, the most celebrated response to underachievement in high-poverty schools has been the highly structured no-excuses model and its many imitations. I want to be careful with the word that usually attaches here, direct instruction, because the fault line in what follows is not directness. My own teaching is direct: I bring the conceptual material, I curate the object students will perceive, I decide before class what a lesson must make discernible, and I teach it explicitly. Direct, explicit instruction is compatible with everything this book argues for; it is part of the apprenticeship.

The fault line is whether the student's own thinking about the content is centered and received as real intellectual work, or merely transmitted and performed back. What I am naming is the second kind: directness without the student's thinking in it.

And directness earns its keep most where the learning is a skill. Mississippi's recent gains in early reading came from exactly this: a structured, explicit, direct approach to a skill so foundational that everything downstream of it depends on it [VERIFY: Mississippi NAEP reading gains; 2013 literacy legislation]. It is not so different from the Suzuki method I taught my children violin with, and some of them now teach with. A violin is hard to learn. It is a skill you cannot fake. And skills carry a real gap between the student's thinking and the outcome: no amount of a beginner's individual expression will make the instrument play in tune, so the teaching is direct, structured, and repetitive, and it is honest work, because everyone in the room knows what it is for. Then the skill arrives, and the student can improvise, play with artistic expression, collaborate with others. The apprenticeship completes by handing the instrument over.

The trouble I am naming is different: the model that never hands the instrument over. Directness as a permanent mode, with the student's thinking never invited in. And I want to be precise about its record, because the strongest versions of this model succeed at what they target. KIPP students graduate, behave, and enroll in college at higher rates than comparison peers [VERIFY: Mathematica — Demers et al., 2023; see the research list in the back matter]. But look at what the success is made of. Even KIPP, the strongest case, lifts college completion by bolting on intensive external support after graduation, KIPP Through College, rather than by having built self-direction in its students, and the absolute completion numbers stay modest. The far more common, less-resourced imitations produce the same training in compliance without the scaffolding that catches students afterward, so the students' downstream cost is worse, not better.

The mechanism is the point, and it is the reason this book studies agency rather than motivation. A model built this way can produce order, compliance, even test gains, while leaving entirely undeveloped the capacity to author one's own thinking: not because the instruction is teacher-led, but because the student's thinking is never treated as real intellectual work. That undeveloped capacity surfaces later, when the structure is removed. Sociologists who have watched these schools from the inside describe graduates trained to monitor themselves, defer to authority, and hold back their opinions; students who then arrive at college without the flexibility, initiative, and ease with adults that navigating it requires (Golann, 2021 [VERIFY]; Martínez & Golann, 2025 [VERIFY]). It is not that these students drop out in droves; the careful causal evidence does not say that, and I won't either. It is that the model is successful and not sufficient: it solves order and accountability, and it does not build the thing college, work, and a thinking life actually run on. [PLACEHOLDER: Katherine's observations from her doctoral students' projects in community colleges — mark clearly whether these are her students' findings or her own observations, so attribution is clean.]

If the pandemic and the phones cannot explain a pattern that predates and outruns them, and if a pedagogy designed to produce order can itself train pseudo-agency, then the right object of study is agency, and beneath it the capacity I will spend this book naming. Not motivation. That is the hook on which everything downstream of this chapter hangs. And let me plant now the distinction the teaching chapters will pay off: the remedy is not to remove the structure or the directness. It is to put the student's thinking inside them. You can teach directly and treat every student as a person whose thinking about the material is real intellectual work. That combination is the apprenticeship.[1]

## More than one driver

There is no single cause. Pseudo-agency is produced by a cluster of forces working in concert: a pacing logic calibrated to a student who does not actually live in most classrooms; a parallel pressure on the output side to advance students whether or not the learning has happened; a legibility regime that prefers what can be quickly counted over what is actually happening in students' minds; the Carnegie Unit and its downward pressure from college admissions through every grade beneath it; the three competing aims of public education that pull schools toward measurable deliverables; and the absence, for many students, of the apprenticeship in learning that makes the curriculum's tasks navigable in the first place. Pacing is the driver teachers feel most acutely every day, so I will start there. But it is the most visible symptom of a deeper structural arrangement.

And the vise does not wait for testing season. It clamps in August. The pacing guide arrives before any test is in view, before I have met a single student, before anyone could know what this group brings or needs, and from that moment the year's bind is set. The test only tightens, much later, what the guide already closed. Nor is this a biology problem, or mine alone: teachers across my building, in subjects far from science, describe the same frustration in nearly the same words. [PLACEHOLDER: a colleague's phrasing or moment here, if wanted.]

Whether this is a feature of my district alone, or a more general lapse between what administrators believe they must require and what teachers actually need in order to teach for understanding, I do not know, and I am not going to pretend to investigative reporting I haven't done. I can only say that the question deserves reflection in every district: who is the pacing guide for, and what would it take for the people who issue it to find out?

## Pacing, and what it assumes

We often talk about equity in schools in terms of equality: sameness. Same pacing, same assignments, same assessments for everyone. But sameness is not equity. It is equality of delivery of a system built for a certain kind of student. The standard curriculum is organized for students with certain kinds of cultural capital who arrive already fluent in learning, and then treats the gap between those students and high-poverty students as a learning problem, a discipline problem, or a home problem, rather than a systemic design problem.

The standard curriculum is not neutral; it is calibrated to a particular inheritance. Bourdieu called that inheritance cultural capital: the language, dispositions, and ease with institutions that some children acquire at home and others do not, which schools reward while presenting it as plain ability (Bourdieu, 1986). Lareau showed the mechanism up close: middle-class "concerted cultivation" trains children in exactly the habits schools expect, questioning adults, managing institutions, treating them as theirs, while working-class and poor families more often grant an unstructured independence that schools then misread as deficit (Lareau, 2003). Coleman's data set the weight of this inheritance in relief: family background predicted achievement more powerfully than school resources (Coleman, 1966). None of this is destiny. But it means a student who arrives "behind" is mostly standing at the distance between their cultural capital and the curriculum's hidden assumptions, not exhibiting a deficit. The legibility regime then misreads that distance in exactly the three ways this section opened with. I take legibility from James Scott, who described how states make populations governable by simplifying them into countable forms, and how much of real life that simplification cannot see (Scott, 1998).[2]

Let me be precise about what I am and am not claiming here, because cultural-capital talk slides easily into two errors. The first is making this about race. Nothing inherent in race determines school performance. Where race enters this book's mechanism, it enters twice: through economics, because poverty in this country is racialized, and through schooling's own white-centered habits of reading, which receive cultural differences in talk, humor, movement, and reference as off-task or defiant. That second entrance is not an exception to this book's argument. It is the argument: misreading is the machinery, and students of color are run through it at higher rates, which is what the discipline disparities record: the misreading made administrative [VERIFY: discipline-disparity source, e.g., Skiba et al.]. The second error is making this about class as such. Wealth buys margin, and it often buys mentorship, but it confers nothing by itself: wealth without intellectual mentorship fails too, and I have seen the proof up close. A couple of my own children attended a high-end private school on scholarship, and my daughter started its orchestra program there. I remember an administrator telling me that some of her wealthiest students were among her poorest performers: their parents were off on trips and projects, without steady support of their kids. All the margin money can buy, and no one lingering. The Germantown parent had no attention left after survival; these parents had attention to spare and spent it elsewhere. The absence is the same; only the bank balance differs. And remember Mike, who had the cultivation and on whom it didn't take on schedule. Poverty is not a culture. But it tends to create fewer of the experiences schooling silently requires, and shared experience is the ground meaning lands on: the more background a speaker and listener share, the more likely the intended meaning arrives (Moore-Anderson, *Teaching Meaning*). The mechanism runs on margin, not on the character of a race or a class. The variable is not who your parents are. It is whether the economics of a family's life leave margin for the apprenticeship, and whether anyone, at home or at school, supplies it. It's the economy, and it's the mentorship; most of everything else is a proxy.

Every pacing guide is built for an imagined student: one who brings sufficient background knowledge to the unit, sufficient academic vocabulary to access the text, sufficient apprenticeship in disciplined inquiry to do something with open-ended tasks, someone comfortable enough in their conceptual thinking to be willing to dialogue about it. That imagined student is not fictional. She exists even in my school. But in a high-poverty school, she is not the median.

The standard response to the mismatch between student and curriculum is differentiation: simplify the vocabulary, chunk the ideas, ask for less output. Reteach. Give students dialogue roles. These are real accommodations, and teachers do them in good faith. But they address the presentation of the problem while leaving its underbelly intact. Differentiation adjusts the readability of the material. It does not adjust the background the curriculum assumes students have: the background experiences, guided participation, and accumulated encounters with ideas that allow new concepts to attach to a growing network of ideas that is personally theirs. A student who has never encountered a concept outside of a worksheet cannot be scaffolded into understanding it through a simpler version of the same worksheet. What they need is something different in kind, not the same idea in simpler form.

When schools evaluate students primarily through what can be made institutionally legible (grades, pacing, test scores, compliance), they are measuring the fluency in learning students bring with them rather than the capacities they are developing. A student who arrives with background knowledge, and the ability to make sense of new learning, will be read as motivated, engaged, and academically able. Even students whose intellectual seriousness takes forms the school does not recognize, Angie and Nancy from Chapter 1, will be read as disengaged, resistant, or immature.

And the advantage being measured is not only academic. In one longitudinal study of urban high school science students, those who qualified for free or reduced-price lunch perceived their teachers as less supportive of their autonomy and more controlling than their more affluent classmates did (Patall et al., 2022). The students with the least cultural capital are not only handed a curriculum built for someone else; they experience the classroom itself as a more controlled place, which is close to the opposite of the conditions under which anyone learns to author their own thinking.

Consider the implicit norm: engage them → teach the lesson in the pacing guide → prove the lesson was taught. The teacher handles motivation. The curriculum handles content. The assessment handles accountability. The norm doesn't account for the actual pace at which understanding forms; for the fragmentation of real instructional time; for the difference between students performing school and students thinking. It doesn't ask what actually engages students, or how the system measures teachers and how that measurement drives what teachers can afford to notice. It is a system that looks for quick legibility.

When district administrators come through my room, they come to check what the system can see and count. They'll note whether I'm on pace, but they won't ask what I'm seeing in students' thinking that makes the pacing guide's sequence epistemically wrong for this group right now. Some unknown expert somewhere, at some time, decided that for me. They won't ask whether the order of the guide is even the order that makes sense for how understanding of a particular topic actually forms. They'll ask a student, "Why are you doing this activity?" and treat "I don't know" as evidence of poor teaching, rather than as evidence that students have spent years in classrooms where comprehension and purpose were never the point.

The system creates pseudo-agency. When students realize, year after year, that what counts is what can be displayed, many stop trying to understand and simply produce. This is not only my classroom's pattern. Researchers who study high-stakes accountability have documented the same narrowing at scale, and it falls hardest on the schools under the most pressure.[5] Then the same regime that creates this problem comes back and treats pseudo-agency as a student problem that the teacher should fix.

Chapter 2 named pseudo-agency as a system product, not a student failure; here is the machinery. I want to be careful with the word we usually reach for, which is unintentional. David Berliner calls the classroom version of this a rational response to high-stakes testing: teachers narrow what they teach not out of indifference or incompetence but because the incentives make narrowing the sensible move, with the harm to students entirely foreseeable (Berliner, 2011). That is a more uncomfortable thing than an accident. No one need intend the damage for everyone to be able to see it coming. Teachers become unwillingly complicit: pressured to cover curriculum, policed in their own heads by a standardization voice that lives there rent-free even when state tests aren't required. A teacher listening to a student's partial understanding must make the call, follow it or stay on pace. The pacing guide is the third presence in the room.

And notice what the policing does with the gap. It does not merely conceal a missing condition; it converts a missing enabling condition into an apparent intrinsic flaw, and relocates the absence onto a person: the student ("unmotivated") or the teacher ("poor teaching"). Mills called this turning public issues into private troubles.[3] The move functions this way whether or not anyone designs it, and it is the same relocation the school-models chapter traced onto teachers. The test to keep running: not every binary is false, but when the either/or is produced by a removable missing support, the person carrying the blame is carrying the structure's debt.

Students rewarded for pseudo-agency long enough begin to see themselves as doers rather than knowers. That is, those that don't become simply angry or disengage entirely. They stop asking what something means and start asking what the teacher wants. The cognitive growth that real effort is meant to build lies dormant: not because the students can't do it, but because the system stopped asking them to.

## Beneath the pacing: the legibility regime and the goals of public education

This legibility regime has a structure underneath it worth understanding, because the bureaucratic version of schooling didn't arrive from nowhere. It arrived from somewhere honestly humane, and understanding that makes it less evil but no less wrong. (Though colleagues more cynically suspect the system is structured to ensure that someone will deliver the pizza; in other words, that it is working as intended rather than malfunctioning in ways we can repair.) The problem is not the aspiration to provide a quality education for all, or even the emphasis on effort, which reflects something true about the world: much of adult life is effortful, not everything is inherently engaging, and effort gets you through the hard and boring parts. The danger is a relentless onslaught of hard and boring tasks without meaning, without discernment, without legitimate participation.

The problem is what happens when humane aspirations get translated into bureaucratic systems that demand legible evidence that can be managed, audited, compared, and defended. Under the pull of Labaree's three competing goals (Chapter 3), schools reach for measurable deliverables that can stand as proof the aims are being met: pacing as evidence of equitable coverage, standardized testing as a proxy for equitable learning, vocational pathways for social efficiency, grades to measure and to distinguish.

This logic is embedded in the basic credit structure of secondary schooling. The Carnegie Unit is designed to meet what colleges expect. It standardizes what counts as "enough" learning by tying it to hours and course labels, which then shapes what middle schools pace toward, which shapes elementary schools. A fixed list of course expectations stands in for actual intellectual development. Even progressive-school teachers feel pressured to translate deep learning into legible pacing and comparable outcomes, because the transition to credit-bearing high school courses is always looming.

## What this looked like in my classroom

My district's pacing guide seems, as best I can tell, an artifact of a textbook that no longer exists: a sequence of topics inherited from an organizational logic that predates current standards, loosely crosswalked to NGSS benchmarks, each of which would require, if taught honestly, far more time than the guide allows. What remains is not really a curriculum. It is a calendar with labels on it. On a given week in March: teach the structure of DNA. Teach "Breeding Corn" in February. What does that even mean? Science standards are listed loosely on the side, presumably to explain it all, while the teacher is expected to create the curriculum.

Besides its incomprehensibility, this pacing naively assumes that time is mapped as planned, without acknowledging the fire drill that ate twenty minutes on Tuesday, the five students absent for the lab, the ten minutes lost to a late bus, the student who went out of the country for a month. Real classrooms operate in fragments. Teachers stop revisiting partially understood ideas because returning "slows the lesson." They teach toward what can be recognized quickly as proof of learning, even though they know this undercuts actual conceptual understanding.

I know because I did it. I answered questions before students had time to think through them. I moved on, because moving on was what the calendar asked. I rewarded expected answers, answers that tended to be too simple, easily acknowledged as correct. I chose activities that made students look engaged but produced nothing in the way of deepening thought.

What I produced was legible. Students had notes. They turned things in. From the outside, the room looked like a working biology class. But most of my students were operating in pseudo-agency mode: some giving me words that sounded like biology without any contact with what those words meant; most sitting in silence. And I had, in my effort to keep up with the pacing guide and stay out of trouble, confirmed that they were right to do so.

I have been guilty as a teacher of scheduling too many things to get done within a class. If I couldn't keep the kids reliably interested, at least I could keep them reliably moving. That was my hope, which didn't actually happen with kids who were sick of the same strategy.

Interest builds when people think about ideas and discuss them with others, and think more deeply over time. That is a pretty reliable human way to build interest, for most kids most of the time. Which means I needed to slow down the flood of papers and activities meant to look like active learning was taking place, and give students time to really think with the lesson, and trust that human nature actually finds that interesting.

Reading the room for actual, not pseudo, agency means noticing not only when it is emerging, but also when one's own practice is suppressing it. That is a painful thing to see. It requires sitting with the possibility that what you are doing, even with good intentions, even with care, even after striving for good relationships, is actively narrowing students' capacity to think. Not because you are a bad teacher. Because the system has colonized you, and some of the compromises you are inevitably forced to make are more expensive for students than you permitted yourself to see. This is the teacher-side mirror of Marcus's "trying to save him" and the chalkboard "coercive teaching" episode: the strain the system produces gets charged to you as a personal failing unless you can name the structure.

And it is my belief, I could be wrong, that students want value from their education. If they cannot find any value in what they are learning and how they are being taught, they think school is pointless, and some become angry. When I was in high school, one of my teachers would get up in front of the room and read the textbook in a monotone. I remember having had enough of it, and pitching the textbook on the floor: "I can read this for myself!" And storming out of the room to guidance, daring them to fail to graduate me if I didn't finish that required course. To be fair, the teacher was going through a painful divorce. She was just trying to survive. I think this also because, as I work with students on their conceptual understanding, I see that many are less angry.

I am not writing this work for teachers to launch into heroic rebellion. I have a job to protect, and you probably do too. I am not economically free either. I am writing about what happened when I finally let myself see what my compliance was doing to my students, and decided I couldn't keep doing it, and tried to find a way to teach that at least allowed me to live with myself, and also, hopefully, better support students' learning.

## The classroom-level causes: how the daily exchange manufactures the same disengagement

The structural drivers are pacing, legibility, and passing. But the same disengagement is manufactured at a much smaller scale: in the ordinary, well-intentioned moves of a single exchange. None of these is villainous. They are what pacing pressure and legibility habits feel like from inside a teacher's minute-to-minute choices. I name them because I caught myself doing every one.

**a · Deflecting the student's own thought.** A student offers a partial, sideways, or not-yet-legible thought, and the teacher, needing to stay on pace or not recognizing the offer as a bid, redirects to the answer the lesson wanted. The student learns their thinking is not the currency of the room. This is the behavioral micro-version of the perception failure the whole book is about: the bid goes unreceived. The tell that separates deflection from legitimate redirection (because sometimes the thought really is off-track) is whether the student's origination was registered before being set aside. **b · Rewarding trivial, quickly legible answers.** Teachers accept, and often fish for, the easy, expected answer because it keeps the lesson moving and reads as participation. I rewarded expected answers: too simple, easily acknowledged as correct. This trains pseudo-agency directly, at the scale of a single question. Students learn the game is *say the obvious thing the teacher is waiting for*, not *think*. This is not an argument against checking for understanding or against right answers; it is about the displacement of thinking by the performance of a correct-sounding response. [VIGNETTE — yours.]

**c · No time to metabolize: copying versus concept-formation.** Students copy notes and transcribe slides instead of restating concepts in their own words, because no time is built in to metabolize. A concept put into the student's own language becomes part of their thinking; a copied note does not, and is far harder to retain. Memory holds concepts, not transcription. This is why coverage produces nothing durable: it is not just rushed, it is structurally non-encoding. **d · The analogy and word-use mismatch.** The textbook's analogies, register, and vocabulary are calibrated to the imagined, already-fluent student. The actual student's analogies and words for the same idea are different, and when only the text's framing is treated as legitimate, the student's own route to the concept is cut off. This is the language-policing mechanism from the school-models chapter operating at the level of concept access, and it is why (c) and (d) are the same coin: the student's words are the encoding path. This is not anti-vocabulary; disciplinary language is a real acquisition target. The point is sequence, own words first and disciplinary words built on them, not either/or. **e · The home-as-workplace reality.** We assume students will metabolize at home: read, rewrite, consolidate, the way I did in high school. But for many students home is a workplace: sports, jobs, younger siblings to mind, unstable or crowded housing, abrupt changes in household. The work we offload to "homework" presupposes a margin many students don't have: what is left of a teenager's attention after practice, shifts, and siblings is the same subtraction Germantown documents in their parents. So the metabolizing has to happen in class or it doesn't happen, which makes the time pacing eats an equity loss, not just a pedagogical one. I had sports, jobs, younger kids at home, and periods of abrupt housing change myself, and I still had the apprenticeship that let me do the home-metabolizing; I may see this partly because I lived a version of it. ## Passing without learning

Pacing presses on instruction from one side. From the other side comes a pressure that looks like its opposite but works toward the same end. Schools are held accountable for graduation and dropout rates, and they are right to worry about them, especially where those rates fall along lines of race and poverty. But the imperative to keep students moving through the system, course by course and credit by credit, can become, without anyone ever deciding it, an imperative to pass them whether or not the learning happened. Credit-recovery programs of uneven rigor, generous grading, the tacit understanding that holding too many students back will surface in the wrong column of a report: these are real forces in a high-poverty school, and they sit directly on top of the credit structure I just described. The Carnegie Unit defines what counts as enough; the pressure to advance ensures the unit gets awarded.

I want to be careful about the claim I am making, because the evidence here is more contested than it is for pacing. I am not saying that rising graduation rates are a fiction; researchers who have looked closely find that much of the increase reflects real gains rather than lowered bars (Harris, 2020). The claim I am making is narrower, and I think harder to dispute, because I watch it happen: whatever the aggregate numbers show, individual students learn, year after year, that passing is largely automatic. They learn that if they produce enough visible proof and tolerate the boredom, advancement will come. This is the lesson Priya had already absorbed, that turning in the paper was the point, and the passing logic confirms it for her at the scale of the whole institution.

[GAP — your vignette: a moment where you saw the "passing is automatic" lesson being taught or learned. A student who knew they'd pass no matter what; a credit-recovery situation; pressure to keep failure numbers down; a student who stopped trying because trying and not-trying led to the same place.]

Put the two pressures together and you have a vise, though the jaws are not pacing and passing. Pacing is what sets the vise in place. Once it has produced a disengaged student, the system has two habitual ways of coping with that student, and both avoid the one thing that would help. It passes him along, and it controls him. Passing waves him through; control bears down. Either way he is shuttled rather than addressed. A student held between those jaws learns something corrosive and entirely rational: that school is a sequence of producible proofs and tolerated boredom, not a place where his thinking is required. The credit is what you get for paying the boredom's price.

This is where I plant a flag for later in the book. Pacing does not only distort what gets taught; it consumes the time that building real understanding would take, the slow apprenticeship into noticing, questioning, and making meaning that the curriculum silently assumes students already had. When that apprenticeship is missing, and the time to build it has gone to coverage, the disengagement that follows is no mystery. Passing is the first of the system's two answers to that disengagement. The second, the disciplinary machinery that meets the bored and disengaged student with ever-tighter control, gets its own chapter later on: the other jaw. For now it is enough to see that both are ways of managing a student the system has failed to apprentice, and that neither is the apprenticeship itself.

## The apprenticeship gap: the Germantown study

As I said earlier, the pacing and standardization of the curriculum is organized for students who arrive already fluent in learning. Susan Neuman and Donna Celano's research on Philadelphia branch libraries, what I will refer to as the Germantown study, helps explain why this is also an equity problem, and why differentiation alone cannot solve it (Neuman & Celano, 2012).

A major foundation gift modernized dozens of neighborhood branch libraries across Philadelphia. The theory was simple and humane: improve access to books and inviting spaces, and you can level the playing field across income levels. Researchers tracked not only whether families used the libraries, because they did, but how they used them, and what kinds of participation the same resources actually produced over time.

In the middle-income branch, many children arrived with an adult who treated the library like it was personally owned. Book choosing wasn't random; it was coached. Adults helped children calibrate difficulty, too easy, too hard, then lingered long enough to find a book that fit. They asked librarians for recommendations. They narrated selection as a practice: This one looks right for you. This one will stretch you. Let's bring these home. The visits could be brief and purposeful, or slow and intimate: an adult and child together reading books, sharing thoughts, pointing at pictures, building sustained attention together.

In the low-income branch, the pattern was often different: not because children didn't want books or because caregivers didn't care, but because the attention caregivers could sustain with children varied. Very often parents came in and sought some down time. They had gotten the child to the library. That was the bandwidth they had left after working two or more jobs and contending with the constant effort it takes to survive while poor.

A four-year-old girl flips through board books in a bin and stops at a cover: *Do Not Open*. For a few seconds she is mesmerized, fascinated by the promise of what might be behind it. No one is nearby to read with her, to enter the story with her, to help her stay long enough for curiosity to become comprehension. Before long, she drops the book and scoots out of the area.

Learning isn't just about access. It isn't only about motivation. And it is not a failure of love. What decides the outcome is whether the child receives the small forms of guided participation that turn a moment of interest into engaged inquiry: Let's open it together. What do you think will happen? Let's read the first page and see. The proximal guidance that turns wandering into participation, and participation into learning, is not something parents in poverty are withholding.

I know what the other side of this looks like, because I lived it as a homeschooling parent. When we studied ancient Greece, we studied it at home and again in our co-op: projects, oral reports, children explaining what they had found to a room of adults and other kids who asked them questions. Ruth Beechick said it simply: she called it mind to mind [VERIFY: Beechick source]. That is the feel of an intellectual apprenticeship from the inside, and it is the counter-picture to the four-year-old alone with *Do Not Open*. Not wealth. Attention, dialogue, and somewhere for a child's finding-out to land.

I want to stop and name a trap directly, because this is the place in the argument where a careful reader should feel it. A developmental claim about poverty can slide, in careless hands, into a deficit story: poor children lack something, and school must supply what their homes failed to give. That is not the claim, and I want to refuse it in both of its forms. The deficit is not in the child. And it is not in the parents. What the Germantown study documents is not a failure of parental love or parental values; it is the arithmetic of exhaustion, what is left of an adult's attention after the constant effort it takes to survive while poor. Being a parent should mean being able to rely on the preschool and the school system to do its job. When the apprenticeship doesn't happen, that is a failure of the institutions charged with providing it, not of parenting. The deficit is in opportunity and apprenticeship the system owes: a deprivation of something withheld, never a deficiency in the student or the family. Nor am I describing two kinds of families; the same guidance appears and disappears with the margin available, in every income bracket, and the point is the guidance, not the population.

And the counter-evidence deserves to be said clearly, strongly, and with examples. Many high-poverty families and communities do apprentice their children into authorship, the attention, the back-and-forth, the adult who lingers, and have always done so. Deborah Meier built Central Park East on exactly that conviction, and its graduates vindicated her [VERIFY: Meier, The Power of Their Ideas, 1995]. [PLACEHOLDER: an example or two of high-poverty families or communities that apprentice their children well — yours to supply.] The capacity this book traces is unevenly distributed by conditions, and that is a claim about structures and opportunities, never about the worth or potential of any child.

My friend Mike is useful here for the opposite reason. He had the concerted cultivation Lareau describes, and it still didn't take at the expected time; academics were just not where he was going at that point in his life. Science kicked in for him much later; he now wants to be a physician assistant. Cultivation is opportunity, not guarantee. Development is not linear; interest has its own timetable; and between four and fourteen there is a whole streak of developmental action that no single input controls. Which cuts both ways: if concerted cultivation doesn't guarantee academic interest, then its absence doesn't fully explain disengagement either. The school's job is not to replay what Mike's parents provided. It is to keep the apprenticeship on offer so it is there when the student is ready.

What is missing in the Germantown picture has a name, and it is the book's central missing ingredient: a **dialogue partner**. The adult who lingers, the guided participation, the proximal guidance all name one thing, the social other who shares the child's attention and turns a flash of interest into understanding. It is deeper than scaffolding, and it is what both camps omit: the no-excuses model gives structure without a dialogue partner, and the autonomy model gives freedom without one. Thinking is dialogic before it is solo.

Private schools built on small, Socratic-style class sizes, along with progressive schools, Montessori schools, and some newer charter models, follow that participatory pathway. Unfortunately for working-class families, these schools are either out of reach or out of range.

The Germantown library offered children the freedom to explore, make autonomous choices, be hands-on with computers, and access a high-quality, diverse range of resources. The structural argument the study makes is that even with a large grant and computers in every branch, the differences it documents are produced by economic conditions that limit parental margin. It is the economic inequality families face that exhausts their energy. What the study shows, in microcosm, is exactly what many students in high-poverty classrooms lack: the consistent presence of an adult in close proximity, drawing attention to what matters, naming what they see, and inviting the child to linger. An apprenticeship in learning.

High-poverty schools, in the name of equity, are expected to fill this gap. They do not reliably provide the guided participation and experiential substrate that give concepts their meaning. Even autonomy and quality materials are insufficient without that participatory piece.

This is the gap the two first graders at the top of this chapter embody. The cowboy worksheet was the same task for both boys; what differed was everything that had happened before it: the stories read aloud, the museum, the movie, the hands-on play, the familiarity between teachers and students, the experiences that build background knowledge ready to gather more to itself. One boy's words were already his. The other boy was being asked to start from the worksheet, which is to say, from nothing.

On a recent district biology assessment emphasizing conceptual understanding, about 9% of students at our high-poverty public school reached proficiency, compared to roughly 60% at a wealthier charter school across town. If I had to point to one difference, I would suggest: wealthier students had friendly adults around who offered them wider, more varied experiences that let them lurk and mess around, both at home and in their wealthier schools, than our lower-income students did. Yet both schools pace the curriculum across the same calendar days. Who are they pacing it for?

## More hands-on, more exploration, more choice — not enough

The standard response to the lack of background experiences, give them more experiences, make it hands-on, let them explore, runs into the same limit the Germantown study exposes. Those children had access: books, computers, an inviting space, and the freedom to explore. And yet it wasn't enough: not because children lacked curiosity or caregivers didn't care, but because exploration without someone to translate the experience into meaning with them doesn't reliably become learning.

What students need is not simply more experiences. They need experiences worked into meaning through dialogue: through an adult who draws attention to what varies, names what matters, and creates the perceptual conditions under which a moment of noticing becomes a durable understanding. This is a direct challenge to the hands-on learning orthodoxy, which assumes the activity itself does the teaching, and to the autonomy-centric approaches that assume choosing empowers drive. Autonomy and activity are inert without the dialogue that targets the critical aspects, especially in the early years.

There is a Vygotskian point hiding in this that the teaching chapters will need. The zone of proximal development assumes the learner has some foothold, a spontaneous concept, a lived experience, a partial understanding, that the teacher can build from. When a student's participation history is thin in a domain, there may be no spontaneous concept to scaffold; the teacher isn't building from partial understanding but from absence. That makes the equity problem structural and pedagogical at once. Structural, because the pacing guide assumes the foothold exists. Pedagogical, because the teacher's job becomes not just scaffolding understanding but creating the experiential substrate that makes scaffolding possible.

This is what people with privilege miss in their conceptualization of what makes school interesting. They benefited from autonomy and hands-on activities because they had that early apprenticeship in learning that became the base for their ability to make use of such resources. If they were fortunate enough to also attend one of the private progressive schools, they experienced the continuity of that apprenticeship, so that by the time they got to high school, they were able to do the internal work of making something interesting for themselves, even if on the outside it wasn't.

My son Ben entered public middle school after years of homeschooling. His experience in the two contexts offered him a sharp contrast in observation. He initially saw that his classmates were grade levels below where they should be. What he thought held students back was, as he put it, "They're spoken at. But they're not spoken to." He saw there was no shared object, no back-and-forth, no checking on what students could make sense of, what meanings they found. The talk happened sometimes with, but more often without, the students' equal participation. Ben had experienced a different apprenticeship in learning.

## Autonomy without footing

The Germantown study also challenges the effectiveness of autonomy alone. There is a counterintuitive finding in Erika Patall's research on autonomy support that sharpens this point considerably. Patall and her colleagues have shown across multiple studies that choice and freedom enhance intrinsic motivation, effort, and performance when students have the internal resources to make use of them.[6] The 2010 study found that the benefits of choice provision are real but conditional: choice enhanced intrinsic motivation, effort, task performance, and perceived competence most reliably when students already felt some competence in the domain, that is, when students had adequate background knowledge. Competence, it turns out, is a prerequisite for choice, not a product of it.

This matters enormously for how we think about equity. Patall identifies autonomy-supportive practices such as providing rationales for what's being learned, incorporating students' perspectives on the work they are doing, and building the relational conditions under which students can make use of openness (Patall & Zambrano, 2019). For students who haven't yet developed the internal footing to direct their own learning, who don't have background experiences, who don't yet know enough to know what they want to know, who haven't built the participatory relationships with adults that make open-ended tasks navigable, more freedom can produce the opposite of what is intended: anxiety and less learning. Choice requires a substrate. You have to have some footing in experiences with learning, scaffolded by kind caretakers, before you can direct your own path through it.

This is why students who benefit most from open inquiry and student-directed learning tend to have apprenticeships in learning and background experiences. They arrive with histories that give them confidence and experience from exploration. Students without those histories need something different first: guided participation that lays the foundation for real choice. Giving them freedom before they have that footing is not empowerment; for many students, it is the anxiety of not knowing how to begin.

## What we should be aiming for

The classroom ecology that produces pseudo-agency sits at the intersection of institutional legibility demands, curricular pacing calibrated to students in wealthier classrooms, and the absence of the proximal guidance that builds participation histories. Teachers are handed a chain, engage, teach, prove, and the weight of it feels impossible.

The teacher is expected to close the gap between the student and the curriculum, a gap shaped by years of structural and systemic disadvantage. We saddle teachers with a kind of equity labor that rightly belongs upstream of the profession. And here the relocation move completes itself: the system relocates upstream labor onto the teacher, then reads the teacher's inability to do the impossible as a personal failing.

Teachers in high-poverty schools are forced to collapse conceptual understanding into rote memorization, because students lack the formative background experiences and apprenticeships in learning that the pace assumes. Coverage becomes the only thing that fits in the pacing available: not dialogue, not complex and interesting problems, not teacher-curated materials that effectively teach each lesson's critical aspects. And coverage, as we have seen, produces pseudo-agency almost by design.

Now put the taxonomy from Chapter 2 next to this machinery, and the equity claim gets its bite. Low-income students are not "lacking agency." They are misread across the whole map. Their engaged origination is read as disengagement: Malik, lurking and messing around. Their epistemic reframing is read as off-task behavior or defiance. Their authorial goal-setting is read as immaturity or lack of interest in school: Marcus. And their strategic compliance, suppressing origination to survive, is read as good behavior. The system doesn't just fail to develop their agency. It systematically miscategorizes the agency they already have. The taxonomy is a perceptual correction device.

Low-income students are not less agentic; they are differently agentic, and their origination takes forms the school's time-world cannot read. The school's response is not to develop that agency but to manufacture pseudo-agency, compliance and performance, as a substitute. The equity injury is double: the misreading first, then the substitution. And much of what looks like help, more structure, more motivation, more choice, deepens the injury by demanding legible performance before agency has been recognized.

So when pacing and standardized testing are offered as the means of producing equity, the result is not equity at all. It is the measurement of advantage dressed as meritocracy.

I frame the problem for my doctoral students as dimensions: a critical aspect to perceive, a meaning-making conversation to have about it, and a resourced environment to have it in. The problem is not solved until all the dimensions are addressed. Fix the materials without the dialogue and you have Germantown; fix the dialogue without the critical aspect and you have talk without an object; fix both inside a pacing regime that leaves no time and you have fixed nothing.

It would be better to aim for students to become more capable of noticing, interpreting, questioning, revising, and authoring their own understanding of the world. That aim requires teachers to read past what the walkthrough is looking for, past compliance, past legibility through standardization, past the performance of studenting, toward the fragile and partial beginnings that are the actual starting point for thinking. A more honest educational aim asks not whether students can perform the school's paced curriculum, but whether they are becoming more agentic as learners. And it requires us to understand, structurally, why classrooms so reliably produce the opposite.

## How engagement unfolds, revisited

Chapter 2 traced how engagement develops in phases, lurking, messing around, geeking out, and how deepening engagement changes the form of agency a student expresses. What belongs here is what those phases mean under the structural conditions this chapter has described.

Each school model, when engagement breaks down, reaches for its signature tool, and each misreads what the student actually needed. The traditional model reads the lingerer's silence as resistance and offers more structure and accountability; what the student needed was time to orient before being asked to perform. The progressive model reads emerging engagement that doesn't yet look like participation as a problem and offers more choice and activity; what the student needed was permission to be in an early phase without being redirected out of it. The democratic model reads the student who wants structure as not yet self-directed and offers more autonomy; what the student needed was a guide willing to get close enough to say: here, start with this.

In Ito's research, in Montessori classrooms, in the middle-income branch of the Germantown study, the phases develop at different rates in different students, which means there is almost always someone a little further along, willing to explain, close and relational, directing their newly formed understanding at someone with an actual question. The transmission chain forms naturally because students arrive with varied participation histories. The diversity of phases in the room is itself a natural resource.

In under-resourced schools, students tend to arrive at the same phase simultaneously. Their participation histories are more similar: similarly thin in most academic domains, similarly shaped by years of compliance-oriented schooling that trained out messing-around behaviors. Everyone is lurking at once. No one is yet geeking out. The transmission chain that forms naturally in more resourced settings doesn't materialize.

This has a specific classroom consequence that is easy to miss: when everyone is lurking simultaneously, pushing dialogue, asking students to talk publicly about ideas they haven't yet had the chance to privately orient toward, doesn't accelerate learning. It produces performance or fear. Students, some, few, will say something, because the classroom asks them to say something. But what they say will be a guess about what the teacher wants, not an expression of emerging understanding. The demand for visible participation before students have had time to lurk is one of the more reliable ways to produce pseudo-agency, and it is the same engine as the deflection and trivial-answer fishing named above, now applied to the whole room at once. It is also one of the more common things teachers are told to do.

The equity argument, then, is not only that under-resourced students need more experiences, though they do. It is that they need the phases to be legitimized. Lurking needs to count. Messing around needs to be protected rather than corrected. And geeking out needs to be recognized even when it doesn't look like the school's preferred form of expertise: when it shows up as hands-on manipulation rather than written explanation, as off-topic connection rather than on-task response, as competence in a domain the pacing guide didn't schedule.

## The bridge to the next chapter

Building those conditions requires something the pacing guide cannot provide and motivation strategies cannot produce. It requires a teaching stance that receives students where they actually are: that treats partial, emergent, even wrong engagement as a legitimate beginning rather than a problem to be moved past.

That stance has a name, and it is the one this chapter has already introduced: the dialogue partner. It is the subject of the next chapter, which reorients classroom dialogue away from outcomes and toward the student's individual knowing.

The dialogue teachers are often told to create, structured academic discussion, think-pair-share, Socratic seminar, is not the same thing as students being in dialogue as knowers. The first asks students to perform talk around a learning object. The second asks the learning object to be open: uncertain enough, and held lightly enough, that a student's partial understanding can actually change where the conversation goes.

How does authentic dialogue support the forms of agency this chapter and Chapter 1 have been building toward? And what does it ask of the teacher: to hold space for engaged agency, the student's effort to understand from their own position, without correcting it into compliance, and to receive an epistemic bid, a student's attempt to reshape the frame itself, without letting the curriculum close around it?

The accumulation this book is after is observable wherever the conditions are right; you can watch it happen in progressive schools. The SAT-income correlation is not just cultural capital; it is years of accumulated encounters in which origination was received and developed. The wealthy progressive-school student isn't smarter. They've had more apprenticeship in being a knower. My argument is that this accumulation shouldn't require wealth. It should be structurally possible in public Title I classrooms: the teacher's perceptual skill, the dialogic orientation, the curated object are portable conditions that can produce the same accumulation without the same resources. The conditions that produce agency are currently gated by wealth, but they don't have to be.

Those questions are what the next chapter takes up.

# Chapter 5 — Dialogic Teaching

There's an iconic classroom scene from Ferris Bueller's Day Off. A monotonous economics teacher asks the class a question about tariffs. Silence. Crickets. He then says:

"Anyone? Anyone?"

He answers his own question in a flat, unforgettable monotone.

As a teacher, it's painfully funny because it captures something real: the moment when classroom questioning collapses into empty ritual. The teacher asks. No one answers. The teacher answers. Everyone writes it down. The form of dialogue is present. The substance has left the building.

What makes the scene so recognizable is not that the teacher is cruel or incompetent. He's just doing what the legibility regime asks of him: moving through the material, producing coverage, keeping the ritual going. The question isn't really a question. It's a placeholder, a beat in a sequence that was going to arrive at the same destination regardless of what anyone said.

This is what dialogue becomes under pacing pressure: a performance of inquiry where the teacher already knows what counts, already knows what the right answer is, and is simply waiting for someone to produce it. Students learn this quickly. They learn that the question is not an invitation to think but a signal to guess: to scan the teacher's face, to wait for the inflection that says *warmer*, to produce the word that ends the discomfort and lets the lesson move on.

That is structured talk. It is not the same as students being in dialogue as knowers.

Most of what schools call dialogue is structured talk. Think-pair-share. Socratic seminar. Accountable talk stems. These are not wrong; they can create useful conditions. But they leave the fundamental problem untouched: the teacher's question still has a predetermined destination, the student's job is still to arrive there, and a contribution that redirects inquiry, that proposes a different question or reveals that the shared object of attention isn't yet visible, is still likely to be received as off-task or incorrect.

There is a second thing schools mistake for dialogue as knowers, and it is worth separating out, because the research has taught us to prize it. When a student tells me what she needs, asks for an assignment to work differently, or offers a suggestion that bends the lesson toward her interest, she is doing something real; researchers call it agentic engagement, and the evidence is good that it improves the instruction students receive and the motivation they feel (Reeve, 2013; Patall, Kennedy, et al., 2022). I want my students to do this. But it is not the same act as authoring meaning, and the difference is easy to miss, because both of them look like a student speaking up.

Agentic engagement is the work of shaping the conditions of learning: the activity, the pace, the format. Authoring meaning is the work of making sense of the thing being learned. They can come apart in both directions. A student can negotiate every term of how an assignment is done and still not have one thought of her own about its content. And a student can have no say whatsoever over the curriculum and still author a great deal of meaning inside it, which is what I did in high school, where I chose none of what I studied and yet did all of the thinking. The curriculum was imposed. The thinking was not.

The discourse research, read carefully, draws the same line.[1] A student can speak frequently and still have little agency if the teacher determines the questions, the procedures, the acceptable conclusion, and the meaning of every contribution; and a room can be full of talk, movement, choice, and visible enthusiasm while epistemic authority stays entirely with the teacher or the worksheet. What separates participation from agency is what happens *after* the student speaks. Does the teacher take up the question? Does the group revise its explanation because of the contribution? Is the direction of inquiry altered, or is the idea praised and abandoned? Participation means entering the discourse. Agency means having recognized, consequential authorship within it.

I dwell on this because the rest of the book depends on it. When we measure whether students shape their instruction, we are measuring the outer, visible layer of agency. We are not measuring whether they have become authors of meaning, and we have a long habit of assuming the first stands in for the second. A student's raised hand asking for a different worksheet and a student's quiet, unbidden interpretation of what the worksheet is about are not two amounts of the same thing. They are two different things, and only one of them is the kind of agency this book is finally about.

The chapters before this one have been building toward a different problem: what happens when a student like Angie is trying to make a procedure intelligible from her own working model, or a student like Nancy is proposing a different representation of what the problem is actually asking? Neither of them was off-task. Both of them were doing something the legibility regime couldn't see. What they needed wasn't a better-structured discussion. They needed a classroom in which their contributions could be received as truthful beginnings rather than errors to be corrected past.

That is what dialogism makes possible.

## The truth that must be attended to

This was the phrase I used in my head in graduate school when we discussed dialogism. It helped me orient to a new kind of talk: one not built on the usual categories of argumentation or debate, but on people taking a sounding of their context and deciding what in it is most humane to attend to.

By the truth that must be attended to, I do not mean that students' ideas are already correct, complete, or sufficient. I mean something more modest, and more demanding.

Students' contributions are truthful at the level of their current understanding. They reveal how the phenomenon is showing up for this learner right now: what distinctions they are making, what relationships they are noticing, and where their sense-making is breaking down.

To attend to that truth is not to accept it uncritically; it is to treat it as data, and to recognize that learning does not begin from nowhere. Students' attempts, however partial, awkward, or wrong-headed, are usually organized, not random. There is often a logic inside the confusion. And when we miss that logic, we don't just lose content understanding; we risk knocking students off their footing as knowers.

When students' attempts are received as noise, misconception, or "not trying," they learn to abandon their own perceptions and start guessing what the teacher wants. When those attempts are received as beginnings, inquiry can continue, without humiliating the learner into silence. That shift, from thinking to guessing, is pseudo-agency's engine.

Dialogism is an ethic of attention to the learner's current reality as a knower. It is how partial models become discussable, testable, and revisable rather than hidden. But to understand why this ethic is not merely aspirational, why it is a practical necessity in any content-heavy classroom, it helps to look at what teachers are actually up against when students arrive at a new topic.

## Why school knowledge arrives encrypted

School knowledge most often arrives as curated knowledge: abstract conceptual tools, symbols, representations, algorithms, and explanatory frameworks that allow us to reason about what is not immediately visible or intuitive. By the time scientific or mathematical ideas enter classrooms, they have been compressed into shorthands: formulas, definitions, diagrams, procedures. These representations are powerful. They let us reason about invisible processes and complex systems. But they are opaque to novices.

They are encrypted.

So teaching is not primarily the delivery of information. It is the work of decryption: decompressing the relationships and dynamics that symbols stand for so they become intelligible. That decompression must begin with the learner's current point of perception, what distinctions they can already see, and where their sense-making is at this point. The teacher connects to their thinking and helps decrypt the discipline's compressions.

What looks like a student failing to grasp a concept is often a student encountering a compression that was never made visible: a symbol standing in for a relationship they were never shown. The symbol encodes a finished result whose operational logic runs in a different direction than the way the student will address the problem. 12÷4 reads left to right, but the question you're actually asking is how many times 4 fits; you start from the divisor, not the dividend. The notation and the action point opposite ways.

And not only is encryption often procedurally confusing; in the name of efficiency it removes other approaches, context questions, and different lenses on the problem. It is monologic, not dialogic. It closes meaning rather than opening it.

This is exactly what happened to Angie. The jingle didn't just skip the reasoning. It foreclosed the inquiry. There was nothing left to be dialogic about: the procedure had already decided what the question was and what counted as an answer. Angie's resistance wasn't a learning problem. It was a reasonable response to being handed a compression without a key.

## Dialogism — not dialogue

The ethical stance I've been describing, attending to the truth of a student's current understanding, treating their beginnings as consequential rather than correcting past them, has a theoretical home in Bakhtin's dialogism. And naming that home matters, because dialogism is frequently misunderstood in ways that defang it.

Bakhtin's dialogism is often reduced to "good discussion," or confused with a pedagogy of open-ended talk. Sometimes it's dismissed as "all opinions are equally true."

But dialogism is not dialogue. It is a claim about how meaning emerges.

In Bakhtin's account, meaning is never privately possessed and then simply expressed. It is not finalized. It arises between voices through response, contestation, and re-voicing. Dialogue is not merely talk between people; it is the condition under which understanding becomes possible at all.

Importantly, dialogism does not treat all claims as equally valid. It treats all voices as answerable: open to response, testing, and revision in relation to other voices and to the discipline. Bakhtin's term otvetstvennost carries both meanings at once, answerability and moral responsibility.[2] A student's contribution is not merely an expression of a personal view. It is a bid that enters a shared intellectual space, one where it can be examined, tested, and revised. The student is accountable to the discipline. And the discipline, in a dialogic classroom, is accountable to the student's way of seeing.

A classroom becomes dialogic not because students speak more, but because their utterances matter: because what they say can redirect inquiry, reframe a problem, or alter the shared trajectory of sense-making. Dialogism recognizes students as knowers even when their knowing is provisional. And the perception this asks of a teacher is not only visual. Dialogism is how thinking becomes audible; you don't just see Malik's fidgeting, you hear what it might mean by offering a side door and listening to what comes back.

Nor is this literary theory imported into teaching. Mortimer and Scott carried Bakhtin's contrast directly into secondary science classrooms as communicative approaches, authoritative and dialogic, and showed that meaning-making requires moving deliberately between them: a purely dialogic science classroom never consolidates the canonical concept, and a purely authoritative one never lets students author anything (Mortimer & Scott, 2003).[3] And the convergence is wider than one pair of researchers. Christian Moore-Anderson, a working biology teacher, arrives at the same structure through conversation theory: pre-designed communication impedes conversation whether the activity is teacher-led or student-led; a teacher who reduces students' errors and deviations reduces her own information, since mistakes and elaborations are the essential feed of adaptive teaching; and teaching-with students "dances the tension" between convergence and divergence (*Teaching Meaning*). Three vocabularies, one tension.

Understanding why this matters for agency requires a distinction that is easy to blur in everyday speech but important in teaching: the difference between sense-making and meaning-making.

Sense-making is the learner trying to orient within a phenomenon: What is going on here? It is perceptual and relational, students working with what they can see, notice, half-recognize. Sense-making is often messy, incomplete, and surprisingly intelligent. It is essential for engaged agency: students cannot invest effort into what feels arbitrary or unintelligible.

Meaning-making grows alongside sense-making, but it is something more. Here the learner begins from their own standpoint, conceptual, experiential, or ethical, while remaining answerable to disciplinary norms. Meaning-making is not repeating what the teacher said. It is metabolizing the idea into one's own thinking: testing it, reframing it, taking a position toward it. This is the ground where epistemic agency begins to show itself.

Nancy's coin flowers show the difference cleanly. When her classmates asked *who gets the extra penny?* they were sense-making: trying to orient within the social reality the problem had opened up, working out what "evenly" could mean in a situation where perfect division wasn't possible. Nancy had moved into meaning-making. She wasn't just trying to figure out what the problem was asking. She was giving the problem a meaning, proposing that balance, not just arithmetic, was what the lesson could be about, and her arrangement was designed to make that meaning visible. She got an answer, but the answer was consistent with the meaning she was giving the problem, not the meaning the teacher had intended. That is an epistemic move, not a computational one.

The distinction also completes the shadow side of the map. Effortful agency lives in sense-making and stalls before meaning-making. The effortful student does make sense of the work; the effort goes into understanding what is being asked and completing it well, not into authoring anything of her own. She rarely pauses to ask why the idea should matter to her, and so the idea never becomes hers. That is why effortful agency can look so much like learning and still leave the student untouched as a knower: all the labor goes into making sense of what is asked, and none of it yet into making it mean something.

Dialogic truth is not relativistic; it is the social route by which students become answerable to disciplinary ways of knowing without being knocked off their footing as knowers. Claims remain open to revision, but they are not arbitrary. They are tested against evidence, reasons, and consequences.

## Internally persuasive dialogue: from static knowledge to lived expression

What dialogism is ultimately working toward is what Bakhtin called internally persuasive dialogue, and naming this concept helps clarify the difference between a classroom where students have absorbed content and one where students have taken it up as their own.

Internally persuasive dialogue is the difference between knowledge a student has received and repeated, and knowledge a student has metabolized: tested, reframed, positioned toward, made answerable to their own thinking. Matusov and von Duyke (2010) clarify that this concept has been flattened in educational settings to mean simply that a student has internalized the lesson's content, or that students participate in communities of practice where they partly define their own learning goals. Both interpretations are closer to Bakhtin's intention than rote recitation, but something essential is still missing. Internally persuasive dialogue, as Bakhtin intended it, includes a critical stance toward the discourse itself: toward the contemporary, historical, and cultural dimensions of how a discipline talks, and toward the student's own ideas in relation to it. Everything is testable, across the entire history of the domain's conversation.

This is a more demanding standard than most classrooms reach. But it names something real that teachers can feel when it is happening: the moment when a student stops reporting and starts thinking, when their contribution shifts from *here is what I was told* to *here is what I think this means, and here is where I'm not sure it holds*.

Bakhtin saw this as the goal of education in his 1945 essay on teaching grammar: not the memorization of rules, but the cultivation of voices capable of understanding why grammatical choices matter, what stances they create, and when the rule itself might be questioned. A student, he wrote, learns under what conditions a particular adjectival clause may be replaced by an adjectival phrase, but neither instructor nor textbook tells the student when and why this substitution ought to be made. Without understanding the purpose, one wonders why the student needs to know how to make the substitution at all.[4] The same question haunts biology: students learn what photosynthesis is, can label the diagram and recite the equation, and still have no idea what problem photosynthesis is solving, or why a plant that abandoned it entirely might still be a plant.

This is not merely a theoretical aspiration. Bakhtin's own teaching practice shows what it looks like to honor it.

In that same essay, Bakhtin shows something that sits in some tension with how his work is often applied in education: he taught directly. He taught grammar rules, stylistic distinctions, the conditions under which one construction could replace another. He was not running a seminar where students discovered grammar through free exchange. He was teaching, carefully and systematically, the tools students would need in order to have something worth saying.

The difference is purpose. Bakhtin teaching grammar was not contradicting dialogism. He was serving it. The grammar was in the service of what students would say, not a substitute for saying it. Students needed to understand why an adjectival clause and an adjectival phrase create different rhythms, different emphases, different stances toward a subject: not because grammatical terminology was the point, but because without that understanding they couldn't yet make deliberate choices about how to voice their own thinking. The tools were the condition for the dialogue, not its replacement.

This is the distinction that matters: concepts and vocabulary are the material students think with, not thinking itself. Direct instruction in the service of voice is not a betrayal of dialogism; it is what dialogism requires.

Angie and Nancy were both reaching toward internally persuasive dialogue, in different ways and at different levels. Angie was testing whether the algorithm was a sufficient account of what fraction division actually asks. Nancy was contesting what should count as a mathematical solution altogether. Both were doing what Bakhtin's most serious students do: treating the discipline's compressions as things that can be examined, questioned, and approached from their own standpoint. Both were shut down by classrooms that had no way to receive that kind of move.

A dialogic stance would have treated both contributions as truthful beginnings. With Angie: where does your model work, where does it break, and what is it trying to preserve? With Nancy, the counter-scene is worth playing out in full, because it shows how little the move requires. It is likely that Nancy did not think of her own work as mathematical; she was probably feeling compliant, not rebellious, and what was missed was how her aesthetic orientation had also produced a mathematical property. John did not need to already understand her arrangement. He could simply have described what he saw: this is neat; it seems to work; I wonder how it fits as a math model. And then opened it past her to the room: come over and see this cool thing. How do we explain it? The arrangement could have stayed out on the mat and run for days, without needing immediate closure.

Notice what that version of John's question does. *I wonder how it fits as a math model* reports a state, not a verdict: the flowers exist, they work, and the mathematical fit is an open question handed to the room. It does not ask Nancy to become a different kind of student, a mathematical one, in order to be received. It makes her existing student-ness sufficient by changing what the room is allowed to think about. And the time dimension is doing more work than it appears to. Letting the object run for days is not a technique; it is a reorganization of institutional time. The pacing guide wants the remainder problem finished in eight minutes. The running object says this thing is more interesting than the pacing guide, and the student who needs four days to recognize her own action as mathematical is not failing to keep up; the room is refusing to rush her. Call it what it is: temporal disobedience. The teacher's job, in these moments, is to protect the time thinking takes from the bell.

Those questions don't abandon the curriculum. They build toward it, from the inside of a real inquiry. And there is a return current the frameworks rarely mention: the encounter developed my agency too. Nancy's arrangement showed me something about mathematics teaching I had not seen; the taxonomy of this book lives in the relational space between teacher and student, and it runs in both directions.

I have been describing large moves: Angie contesting the algorithm, Nancy contesting what counts as a solution. But the thing this chapter is about shows up far more often in a smaller form, and I want to put one small instance on the page, because it is the kind of moment a teacher can actually wait for.

What it changed, for a moment, was the kind of participant he was willing to be, and that small shift, from recipient to interpreter, is the thing every other idea in this book is trying to make possible. ## Where I got stuck, and what it revealed

Carrying this into a required biology course turned out to be harder than the theory suggested, and the difficulty was clarifying.

The goal was clear: move students from static knowledge toward lived expression, through provocations that invite stances, in a classroom where those stances are actually testable. And Bakhtin's grammar teaching had shown me that direct instruction and dialogue are not opposites, that teaching concepts carefully is not a retreat from dialogism but its precondition. What I still couldn't resolve was more specific: not whether to teach concepts directly, but how to create the perceptual conditions under which direct instruction serves dialogue rather than replacing it.

My students couldn't yet take stances because they didn't yet have the conceptual footing that would give a stance something to stand on. You can't have internally persuasive dialogue about photosynthesis if you can't yet locate what photosynthesis is actually asking. The dialogic stance was available to me. The shared object of inquiry wasn't yet visible to them. I needed something that made the critical aspects of a concept visible before asking students to talk about them, something that created perceptual purchase first, so that the dialogue had something real to work on.

That gap is not a failure of dialogism. It is its precondition. And naming it precisely is what made the solution findable.

## What dialogism needs, and what it can't provide alone

This is where dialogism and Variation Theory meet, and why both are necessary.

Dialogism answers: how do you receive and respond to student thinking in a way that keeps them positioned as knowers? Variation Theory answers: how do you design the lesson conditions that give student thinking something worth thinking about in the first place?

These need each other. A dialogic classroom without Variation Theory can become unmoored: real student voice, but no perceptual structure for that voice to work on. Students talk, but they talk around a concept none of them can yet see. The dialogue is real but unanchored. (Moore-Anderson sees the same failure from the conversation side: when everything varies, the thread is lost, and it is lost soonest by the least knowledgeable and least talkative students; *Teaching Meaning*.) Variation Theory without dialogism becomes a better delivery mechanism, but still monologic: the teacher controls what varies, students notice what they're supposed to notice, and the epistemic bid has nowhere to go. The lesson is clearer, but the knower is still not in the room.

The bridge between them is the object of attention: what students need to be able to perceive before they can say anything real about it. Dialogism requires a shared object. You cannot have real dialogue about something no one can yet see.

I want to make a stronger claim here than "Variation Theory serves dialogue," because there is a way of knowing in this that is not reducible to talk. Emanuelsson proposes a middle space between acquisition, the inner trying to make sense of the outer, and participation, where the outer precedes the inner; he calls it constitutive: an interplay between the inner and outer worlds.[5] Where dialogism, frustratingly to me as a science teacher, keeps its focus on dialogue, the constitutive framing is about ways of seeing and discerning that shape what you can understand, with the teacher influential in the seeing and the meaning-making left to the student. Bateson said difference is the unit of mind; maybe it is the unit of learning too. This was Montessori's three-part cards, the pink tower, the rods: complex thinking happens when you can see the differences all at the same time. I watched it in my own homeschool. When we studied landforms with plain wooden pieces meant to accentuate shape, Ben started noticing landforms in the real terrain from a bridge; then in the continents puzzle; then in pictures across biomes. The differences he had discerned in wood let him discern them on actual ground. No dialogue carried that transfer. Discernment did, and it is its own road into knowing.

This brings us back to what the Germantown study was actually showing. The difference between the child who flourished in the library and the child who drifted wasn't motivation, intelligence, or access. It was whether an adult got close enough to create a shared object of attention, here, look at this, what do you notice, let's open it together, and then stayed in dialogue around it long enough for the child's perception to sharpen. That is how literacy is built: not through exposure alone, but through targeted social interaction around a specific object, with someone who can name what matters and invite the child to see it too.

What Montessori understood, what the middle-income library visits produced naturally, and what the four-year-old with the Do Not Open book was missing: it was always this. A shared object held between two people, with dialogue organized around it. The adult doesn't pour understanding in. The adult creates the perceptual conditions under which the child can begin to see, and then stays close enough to respond to what the child actually sees.

In a classroom of thirty ninth-graders, with a pacing guide and a curriculum full of encrypted knowledge, that proximal guidance cannot work the way it does on a library rug or a Montessori mat. You cannot get close to every student simultaneously. What you need is a way to create that shared object of attention at scale: something that does for a whole classroom what the adult in the library did for one child. It makes one critical aspect visible, holds it still long enough for students to perceive it, and opens the conditions under which real dialogue becomes possible.

The architecture of the book's middle, then, has three parts, and it is worth stating plainly before we go on. Dialogism is the ethic: how student thinking is received. Variation Theory is the perceptual design: how the object is made visible enough to think about. And intellectual apprenticeship is the sustained practice in which the two are joined, where direct instruction and the student's origination stop being rivals. Beneath all three runs the clock: Ito's phases, from Chapter 2, which tell you when a student can meet the object and in what mode. The ethic without the clock demands dialogue from students who are still lurking; the design without the clock shows the object before the hands are ready. Joining these three traditions, dialogism, Variation Theory, and Ito's phases of engagement, is, so far as I can tell, this book's theoretical wager. This chapter has been about the interaction. The next is about the instruction: how Variation Theory works, and what it looks like in a biology classroom where students are still learning to trust their own thinking.

Lezir did not walk into class on Tuesday wondering about Toxicofera and lost legs. It came into being over the course of being taught snake anatomy in a way that let him see what he was looking at and treated him as someone who could think about it. The inquiry was not the starting point of the lesson; it was its product. That reversal, inquiry as the consequence of teaching rather than its precondition, is the most important thing I have learned as a teacher, and it is what this chapter is finally about.

It matters most in schools like mine. The students who arrive already brimming with questions about the curriculum are, overwhelmingly, the students who were apprenticed into questioning somewhere else: at a kitchen table, in a museum, beside an adult who treated their noticing as worth something. To tell a teacher of students who were never given that apprenticeship to "start from the child's inquiry" is to tell her to start from something that is not in the room, and then to let her conclude, when it fails to appear, that the lack is in the children. It is not in the children. Inquiry has prerequisites, and the prerequisites can be built. The rest of this chapter is about how they are built.

## What disciplines pose

Before I describe how that building happens, I need to say something about why creating the conditions for it is so hard in the first place: not because students lack intelligence or curiosity, but because the disciplines themselves pose specific problems that conventional teaching makes worse rather than better.

Biology poses two such problems, and Christian Moore-Anderson, whose work on biology teaching introduced me to Variation Theory in practice, names them with unusual clarity.[1]

The first is that biology is not linear. It spans three fundamentally different domains, the organism, ecology, and evolution, each with its own logic, its own timescale, its own causal structure. A concept that makes sense at the level of the organism behaves completely differently at the level of an ecosystem or across evolutionary time. Students who do not yet see these as distinct domains will flatten everything into one undifferentiated mass of biological facts, and no amount of dialogue will help them think more precisely if they cannot yet locate which domain a question is even asking about. The student who answers a question about the function of an enzyme by describing what happens to a population is not confused about enzymes. They are confused about which domain the question lives in. That confusion is invisible to most assessments and central to most failures of understanding.

The second is that biology is causal and dynamic, but is almost always taught mechanistically. Students can memorize a labeled diagram of a feedback loop and still miss how a system regulates itself: how the parts interact, how the organism sustains itself over time in a way that a machine simply cannot. When a car needs gas, it does not go find some. An organism does. That difference, between a mechanism and a self-regulating system, is one of the most important things biology teaches. It is also one of the hardest things to convey through a diagram, because the diagram shows the parts but not the dynamic. Students see the representation and mistake it for the thing.

Both problems come down to the same underlying issue: students see the label but not the phenomenon, the symbol but not the relationship it encodes. They encounter the encryption problem the rest of this book has been describing, except that in biology the compression is not a jingle. It is a diagram, a definition, a labeled photograph. The form of the encryption changes from discipline to discipline. The problem is the same. And in science the missing substrate is not background knowledge in the generic sense; it is perceptual attunement, learning to see what varies, what matters, what is worth noticing. A child who has messed around with plants, watched things grow, noticed that some need more sun than others, has a foothold for photosynthesis. A child who hasn't has no spontaneous concept to scaffold.

This is why the perceptual work matters as much as it does. The students in front of me are perfectly capable of producing sentences that sound like they understand the material. What they are not yet able to do, what no amount of producing sentences will give them on its own, is to perceive the conceptual feature the sentences are pointing at. This chapter is about creating conditions under which perception of that feature becomes possible, and then about what happens between people once it is.

## Two registers around one held object

What happens in my classroom, when it works, operates in two registers at once, and the work depends on both. If the last chapter named how teachers *receive* student thinking, this one names how teachers make something *thinkable* in the first place.

The first register is a shared object held between us. The slide points at it, a contrast that varies one feature against a background that does not vary, but the slide is not the object. The slide is the vehicle. The object is the conceptual feature the slide is pointing at: the thing students need to come to perceive in order for the rest of the lesson to make sense. There is something specific being held in the room, and the fact that we are all looking at the same thing is what makes the rest of what happens possible.

The second register is what happens between people around that object. This is Lezir asking what he asked, and my response to him while the rest of the class continues to work the slide. It is improvisational. It treats the student as a knower whose thinking matters in its own right, not as a person who needs to be steered toward a destination they have not yet reached. It happens in real time, in the seconds before the moment closes, in the texture of an ongoing class.

Neither register is sufficient on its own. The shared object without the dialogic register is a lecture organized around a clever contrast: students may perceive what the lesson is pointing at, but the room is still flat, the thinking that emerges is still private, and the student whose mind is moving in a direction the curriculum did not predict has nowhere for that movement to go. The dialogic register without the shared object is what most attempts at dialogic teaching feel like when they fail: a room full of student voices working around a concept none of them can yet see. The two registers are not parallel tracks. They are the two halves of the same structure, and the structure is older than either of them.

## The older scene

The structure is older than the classroom. It is the scene the Germantown library work was actually showing. A four-year-old on an adult's lap, a book between them, the adult's finger pointing at something on the page, the child looking at what the finger points at, the adult naming it, the child asking a question, the adult answering, the child connecting it to something from outside the room. The child whose perception sharpens over the course of that exchange is not the child who has been exposed to the most books. It is the child whose adult got close enough to share an object of attention and stayed in dialogue around it long enough for the perception to develop.

In that scene the shared object is not the book. The book is the vehicle. The shared object is whatever the adult and child are looking at together at any given moment: a picture, a word, the way a character's face has changed across two illustrations. The adult chooses the book and points; the adult is not waiting for the child to set the curriculum of the read-aloud. But the adult is also not pouring information into a vessel. The child's noticing enters the read-aloud and changes it. The book stays the book. The reading is theirs.

This is what I am trying to do in a classroom. I cannot put thirty students on my lap, and I cannot make the page small enough for all thirty to see what my finger is pointing at. But I can do something structurally equivalent. I can choose the material. I can design conditions under which a specific conceptual feature becomes perceivable to all of them at once: a slide that holds a contrast still long enough for them to see it, a sim that lets them put a hand on a variable, an artifact that puts the critical aspect under their fingers. And then, inside that shared attention, I can stay close enough to specific students to receive what they are noticing, and to let what they notice enter the room as something the room now knows.

## What the shared object actually is

I want to be careful here, because a lot of the confusion in writing on dialogic teaching collapses at this point.

The shared object is not the slide. It is not the sim, the lab bench, or the artifact. Those are vehicles. The shared object is the conceptual feature the vehicle is pointing at: in Variation Theory's terms, the critical aspect of the lesson, the one feature whose discernment is what the lesson is for. It is what we are jointly attending to when we are jointly attending. It is what makes the moment a shared one rather than thirty private ones.

This matters because a lesson can have one shared object running across many different vehicles. The critical aspect that the dark reactions of photosynthesis depend on products from the light reactions can be made perceivable through a slide that contrasts a green plant with an Indian pipe, through a sim that lets students turn the light on and off and watch the downstream chemistry stall, through an activity that has them sketch the dependency in their own arrows, through a what-if question spoken aloud in the last five minutes. The critical aspect is the same in all four. The vehicles differ. The room is sharing attention to the aspect, not to the vehicle.

Variation Theory's contribution, in its strongest form, is a grammar for designing conditions under which a specific conceptual feature becomes perceivable: through contrast that varies only the critical aspect, through pattern breaks that interrupt the over-generalization the contrast will produce, through fusion that puts the parts back into the whole. The grammar is powerful and I use it. But the grammar belongs to the vehicle, not to the object. The object is the critical aspect itself. Designing the vehicle well is what makes the object perceivable. The object is what the room is for.

This grammar is not new, and I did not first get it from Variation Theory. I got it years earlier from Montessori, and recognizing the two as the same thing was part of what made Variation Theory feel like a homecoming rather than a discovery. A Montessori material is built to isolate a single quality. The pink tower varies one thing, size, while holding color, shape, and material constant, so that size is the only difference the child's hand and eye can find. The color tablets vary hue against an otherwise identical form. The sound cylinders vary sound and nothing else. This is exactly the logic Variation Theory names as variation against invariance: move the one critical aspect, hold everything else still, and the aspect becomes perceptible because it is the only thing changing. Montessori solved the problem of discernment through objects, physical things a child could pick up, on a mat, one at a time.

What I am trying to do is carry that same solution into content that cannot be held in the hand, and into a room of thirty students rather than one child on a rug. The contrast on the slide, the variable in the sim, the artifact pair on the counter: these are my attempt to do at scale, and for abstract ideas, what the pink tower does in the hand for size. I dwell on the lineage because it names the first thing any apprenticeship in thinking requires. Before a student can take a stance toward a concept, question it, or make it their own, the concept has to become perceptible: an object they can actually attend to rather than a word they are asked to repeat. The perceptible object comes first. Everything else is built on it, and it is the piece I return to at the end of this book.

One confusion is worth heading off, because Moore-Anderson is sharp about it: variation theory is not the activity called examples-and-nonexamples. A nonexample shows what an aspect isn't; variation varies the aspect itself, and only the second triggers the meaning-making of the aspect (*Teaching Meaning*). The two devices do different work, and this book uses both knowingly. Dysfunctional agency stands in my taxonomy as a non-example, marking a boundary; the classroom designs in this chapter vary critical aspects, building discernment. Boundaries and discernment are different gifts.

This is why a lesson can fail with a beautifully designed slide and succeed with a clumsy sketch on the board. The slide is not the object. If the critical aspect is not made perceivable, the slide has not done its work, no matter how elegant. If a clumsy sketch makes the critical aspect perceivable, the sketch has done what was needed. The judgment is not about producing the best vehicle. It is about producing whatever vehicle makes the critical aspect of this lesson perceivable to these students in this room on this day.

And it recasts a scene from Chapter 1. If John had been thinking in terms of critical aspects, he would have known that when he asked about the remainder, he had perceived a difference: two forms of math had come up. Nancy's arrangement did not answer the word problem, but it provided an interesting variation on one of its aspects, and her classmates' fairness question varied the remainder in another way, comparing the problem with and without a human context. Moore-Anderson puts the principle plainly: meaning cannot be transmitted, but there are ways of triggering meaning-making; we make meaning when we perceive a difference that makes a difference, so we must prompt students with differences (Moore-Anderson, *Teaching Meaning*). And, interestingly, students can raise those differences too. Nancy did.

## Choosing the critical aspects

If the critical aspect is the thing the whole lesson turns on, then the most consequential decision I make is which aspects those are, and there are never many of them. A lesson, in my experience, can carry two or three critical aspects that actually shape understanding. Not eight facts. Two or three discernments, each of which, if a student fails to make it, leaves everything downstream incoherent. The art is in finding them, and the way I find them is to ask where students reliably go wrong, because the critical aspects of a topic are usually the exact places where the discipline's compression hides a misconception.

Natural selection is my clearest example. Students arrive believing, almost universally, that it is a story about individuals improving: that a giraffe stretched for high leaves and its offspring inherited the longer neck, that animals adapt or learn within their lifetimes by trying, that the environment hands out the trait that is needed. None of that is selection. So the critical aspects of the unit are not the vocabulary list. They are the two or three discernments that dissolve that misconception:

- Natural selection works through death and reproduction, through who survives to reproduce, and how much, not through who tries hardest, learns, or improves. The mechanism is differential survival and reproduction, not individual striving.
- It is the population that changes across generations, not the individual within a lifetime. No giraffe grew a longer neck. The proportion of longer-necked giraffes in the population shifted, because the shorter-necked ones left fewer offspring.
- The variation was already there before it was needed. Selection sorts existing variation; it does not manufacture the helpful trait on demand.

A student who discerns those has understood natural selection, even if their vocabulary is still rough. A student who has memorized the vocabulary but not discerned them has understood nothing, and will reconstruct the giraffe-stretching story the moment the test is over. That is what I mean by a critical aspect that shapes understanding: not a fact to be covered, but a discernment the rest of the topic depends on.

And then, this is the part that makes the principle a practice rather than a slogan, each critical aspect gets tied to an activity built specifically for discerning that aspect, and built, where I can manage it, as something students play around with rather than something they are told. The aspects above are abstract; the messing-around is what makes them perceptible. For death-and-reproduction-not-striving, I want an activity students run rather than read: colored prey on a colored background, a predator that takes the ones it can see, the survivors reproducing into the next round, so they watch the population's color shift across generations. The thing they cannot help noticing, because it is the only thing the activity lets vary, is that no individual prey ever changed: the population changed, and it changed through who died and who reproduced. The misconception has nowhere to hide, because the activity was designed to isolate exactly the aspect the misconception gets wrong. A different aspect would call for a different activity. The activity is not decoration on the lesson, and it is not there to produce output. It is the vehicle for discerning one specific critical aspect, and it earns its place in the period only if it does that.

This is the discipline that makes "two things per class," later in this chapter, possible. If a lesson has two or three critical aspects and each has one activity built to make it perceivable, the period is full, and full of the right things. Everything else, the extra vocabulary, the supplementary worksheet, the coverage the pacing guide wants, is what gets cut, because it is not load-bearing and the discernments are.

## Ito's phases and the same underlying arc

What struck me, as I worked with Variation Theory and the dialogic register together over a couple of years, was that Mizuko Ito's phases, lurking, messing around, geeking out, describe the same underlying arc that Variation Theory describes, from a different vantage point. One view is the teacher's. The other is the learner's. Both are looking at the same thing.

Lurking corresponds to the whole. Students are present but not yet able to make sense of what they are seeing. They cannot yet differentiate the parts or locate the critical aspect. The whole gives them somewhere to be before they can understand. Messing around corresponds to contrast and separation. Students begin to poke at differences, to notice that this plant does not do what that plant does, to feel the limits of their initial generalization. Geeking out corresponds to fusion. The vocabulary starts to stick; students begin forming and refining concepts; the whole becomes something they can read, use, and explain to each other.

One thing this mapping makes visible is why teacher questions so often fall flat during the messing-around phase. Students hesitate not because they have nothing to say, but because they cannot yet confidently locate the object of the question: they have not had enough contact with the material to know which aspect of it the question is pointing at. A question asked too early, before the aspect has been located, asks the student to be precise about something they cannot yet see. They have no way to answer except by guessing what the teacher wants, which is pseudo-agency by another name.

Seen this way, Variation Theory is not a delivery mechanism for content. It is a design for how to move students through the phases: not by engineering enthusiasm, but by structuring what varies so that discernment becomes possible at each stage. The teacher controls what varies. Students do the seeing. And as students begin to geek out, something else starts to happen without being engineered: they explain to each other.

In fact, we are often anti-engagement precisely by skipping this structure. We demand fusion-level behavior from students who are still in the whole. We ask for precise language from students who have not had the contrast that would make precision possible. We force lurkers to talk and we block messing around, and then we wonder why the room goes quiet. The student with their head down has often been mislabeled disengaged when they were lurking, doing the legitimate early work of orientation in a domain they had not yet entered.

## Perceptual tripping points and other question types

Inside the dialogic register there are specific kinds of moves I have come to rely on, which I want to make explicit because they are part of how the shared object gets pointed at.

One is the perceptual tripping point: a place in the discipline where the conventional naming will mislead any student who is paying attention. Veins and arteries are the clean case. Students learn that veins carry deoxygenated blood and arteries carry oxygenated blood. Then they meet the pulmonary vein, which carries oxygenated blood, and the pulmonary artery, which carries deoxygenated blood. The rule collapses. The rule was wrong. Veins and arteries are named for direction relative to the heart, not for oxygen. A lesson that holds the pulmonary case beside the standard case and asks students to notice what has changed does something a definition cannot: it lets the student catch the trick of the language before the language traps them. The student who has been allowed to catch this once is, in a small but real way, no longer a person who memorizes biology. They have begun to notice that the names in the discipline are doing work the names do not announce.

Another is the perceptual reframe: a question that holds a familiar concept against an unfamiliar case and asks whether the concept still fits. Is an Indian pipe a plant? Is a virus alive? Is fermentation a kind of respiration? These are not gotcha questions. They force the student to articulate what the concept actually means by testing whether it holds at its edge. The answer matters less than the articulation. A student who has worked out, in their own sentence, why we still call an Indian pipe a plant has done conceptual work a student who memorized the definition has not.

A third is the prediction-under-perturbation question: the what-if. Will the rate of photosynthesis increase, decrease, or stay the same if we double the CO₂ and halve the light? These have no answer to copy. They require the student to commit to a position and defend it from inside their working model of the system. They are the moves most open to the dialogic register, because the student's reasoning is on the table. There is no inflection in my voice that signals *warmer*. There is only the student's own reasoning, committed to and defended.

My participation rates at the what-if stage are still low. Students are not yet confident enough to commit to a position in public, especially when there is no right answer to hide behind. But I have stopped reading that silence as disengagement. It is, more often, the silence of real thinking: of students working out what they believe before risking it. That working-out is exactly what I want. The stance comes first in private; with time, and a classroom culture that receives it safely, it begins to surface in public.

None of these question types are mine in any original sense. What is mine is the particular combination and the calibration of each to the students in front of me. Discern a feature. Test a concept at its edge. Reason about a system under perturbation. The intellectual work has the same shape whatever the level; what changes is the assumed background and the language of the prompt, not the cognitive demand.

## The dialogic register, in more detail

What the vehicle cannot do is hear Lezir.

The vehicle can hold the room. It cannot answer his specific question, cannot tell him that his hypothesis about how snakes might have lost their legs is closer to something true than he knows, and cannot register that his question came from his own attempt to make sense of what he was seeing: that it was an epistemic move, not a comprehension failure, not a tangent to be redirected. Only a person can do that, in real time, in the seconds before the moment closes.

The dialogic register is what I run alongside the shared object. I crouch beside students. I take their questions seriously. I tell them when they are close to something true, and when their reasoning has implications they have not yet seen. I let their contributions enter the room as contributions, not as deviations. I do this in the texture of an ongoing class, while the rest of the room continues to work with the shared object. I am not running two lessons in parallel. I am running one classroom in two registers, and the registers are answerable to each other: the shared object gives the dialogic register something specific to be a dialogue about, and the dialogic register gives the shared object the human reception without which a concept stays cold.

There is a reason this pairing is hard for pseudo-agency to survive. You can fake understanding of a worksheet. You cannot fake understanding of a blood spatter pattern when you are trying to make the evidence cohere: the material talks back. A held object resists performance, provided the teacher holds it lightly enough that a student's partial understanding can actually change where the conversation goes. If I already know the answer and am only waiting for someone to arrive at it, students will perform arrival. The object has to be actually open.

Over time, the dialogic register also builds something the shared object cannot build by itself: a knowledge base specific to this room. Lezir's question about Toxicofera and lost legs becomes the moment our class learned that snake limbs are not gone from the genome: they are silenced, and the silencing is itself a thing biology can study. Another student's question about whether snake venom is "really" digestive enzymes becomes the moment our class learned that the categories of digestion and predation share more chemistry than they advertise. A student's offhand mention of her grandmother's garden becomes the example we use for a week when we talk about pollination. Rabbit trails, when I can afford them, provide authorial outlets. These accumulate. By the middle of the year the class has its own corpus: cases, questions, and small disagreements no other classroom will have, because no other classroom contains these students. The knowledge is theirs, partly, because they made it.

## Knower stance — and why inquiry comes last

There is a strand of dialogic pedagogy, running through Freire, and radicalized in Matusov's authorial teaching, that holds real dialogue requires the student's own inquiry to lead. Freire's distinction between banking education, in which the teacher deposits knowledge into students treated as containers, and problem-posing education, in which students are co-investigators, is the version most teachers know. Matusov sharpens it: a dialogue whose endpoint is known in advance, he argues, is at best manipulation toward that endpoint. Aukerman's shared evaluation pedagogy applies the same instinct: a teacher who refuses to evaluate, even when a struggling reader reads the text "wrong," lets the student begin to take up a position as a knower rather than as the receiver of correct answers.[2]

I take from this strand the seriousness about students as knowers, and the refusal of banking education. I do not take the conclusion that the curriculum has to come from the student, or that having an intended object of learning corrupts the dialogue. I bring the conceptual material. I curate the shared object. I have decided, before class begins, what critical aspect this lesson is going to make perceivable. But I refuse the banking position by treating every student as a person whose thinking about that material is real intellectual work: whose partial understandings are partial understandings *of something*, whose divergent answers are evidence of what they are noticing, whose questions enter the room as contributions to what the room now knows. The endpoint of the dialogue is not the answer in my head. It is a student whose own thinking about the concept has developed further than when they walked in, including in directions I did not predict.

Here is the move I think the existing debate has not named, and it is the one I most want to put down plainly, because it is the thing I have been most frustrated by as a teacher. The field treats student inquiry as either a precondition for real learning, the progressive position, follow the child's interest, or an absence to be remedied, the traditional position, motivate them into it. Both are wrong in the same way. Both assume inquiry is something a student either brings or lacks. Neither asks where inquiry comes from. What I have come to see in my own classroom is that the right teaching generates inquiry. Lezir did not walk in wondering about Toxicofera; he developed that question over the course of being taught snake anatomy in a way that treated him as someone who could think about what he was seeing. The inquiry is not where the work starts. The inquiry is what the work produces.

This is not an abstract correction. It is the difference between a pedagogy that can serve my students and one that cannot. If inquiry is a precondition, then the students who don't arrive with it are out of luck, and the teacher is reduced to waiting for a question that will never come, or, worse, to deciding that the children who do not produce questions are not curious. If inquiry is a consequence, then it has prerequisites a teacher can actually build: a perceptible object, a stance that receives the student as a knower, time in the early phases without punishment. Build those, and the questions come: not because you found the right hook, but because thinking, once it has something to work on and somewhere to go, produces questions on its own. That is what inquiry is. It is not the fuel you need to start the engine. It is the exhaust of an engine that is running.

## The four-stage progression

What skill development actually looks like in my classroom, once the perceptual conditions are in place, is a four-stage progression. It does not happen all at once, and trying to rush it defeats the purpose. Each stage has to be fully inhabited before the next is worth attempting, and reading which stage a student is in is part of the perceptual work the rest of this book has been building toward.

The first stage is conceptualizing. Students put the content in their own words, in writing, without being given an answer to copy or a blank to fill. The slides are their reference. What they need is to make sense of what the slides are pointing at. I am watching not for accuracy but for whether students have a working model at all: whether they can produce a sentence that shows real contact with the concept. I calibrate by feel: after a few slides of lurking, do I start to see enough confidence to risk an answer out loud? When answers become more thought-out and less random, we move. When they don't, I don't push. Testing students before they have conceptual footing does not measure understanding. It confirms its absence, which they already know. And the writing comes before conversation on purpose. Conversation is how a teacher learns what meanings students have actually made (Moore-Anderson, *Teaching Meaning*), but students not yet confident enough to converse need a private channel first. The writing is the conversation's on-ramp; later, pulled into class discussion, it becomes shared meaning.

The second stage is accuracy and rephrasing. Once students have a working model, however rough, we refine it. This is where vocabulary starts to become theirs rather than the slide's. I return to their conceptualizations and ask them to tighten the language, to use the discipline's terms more precisely, to say the same thing in a way that would make sense to someone who had not seen the slides. For a while my method felt empty without this: a page to wrap up vocabulary and concepts in their own words, moving from their ideas and conceptions to knowing the words that build the skill, so that when they revise, they begin *from* the skill. It is not too tight and not too loosey-goosey; it harkens back to direct teaching and the student as knower at once. This is not correction in the traditional sense; it is closer to translation work, moving from their intuitive grasp toward the precise language that lets them think with it more powerfully. The goal is that the biological term stops being a label to memorize and becomes a tool they reach for because it does something their everyday language cannot.

The third stage is peer discussion and collaborative sense-making. This is where geeking out becomes social. Students who have reached this stage begin to behave like a study group: not reciting answers at each other but debating, pushing back, asking *but what about this case?* I have found it powerful to have students work through assessments collaboratively at this stage. It is another pass at articulating concepts, and you can hear who is beginning to geek out by listening for the student who is teaching.

The fourth stage is formal assessment, but assessed in a way that does not defeat students before they begin. Many of my students have IEPs. Testing that is inaccessible in its language or overwhelming in its length does not measure biological understanding; it measures tolerance for confusion. I differentiate not by lowering the conceptual bar but by removing the linguistic obstacles: accessible phrasing, manageable length, questions calibrated to what I have actually seen students engage with. I watch for the threshold where the task tips from challenging into defeating. Too hard and they stop; too easy and they are bored. The window is narrow and it moves, and reading it is the perceptual work this book has been building toward.

What makes the progression cohere, and keeps it from being a ladder of hoops, is that each stage is itself an act of becoming more skilled, and the student is positioned as a knower throughout. The first stage does not say: you are not yet a knower because you cannot produce a polished sentence. It says: you are a knower whose first attempt is what I want to see. The second does not say your first attempt was deficient; it says it was real, and now let's give it a sharper edge. The progression is the skill development, made structural.

## The skull station

In my biology classroom there are a few objects left behind by previous teachers and a few I have laminated and arranged myself: a set of evolutionary skulls along one counter, and a timeline of life laminated from a Montessori-style production, alongside a simpler version with blanks. I have set up that counter the way a Montessori classroom sets up a shelf, the full information visible alongside the blank version, the contrast built directly into the materials rather than added by a lesson script. It is the Montessori logic again, made into furniture. During the unit on evolution, when something on the slides triggers a connection to one of those objects, I invite students over individually to come look. It is not a hard sell. The skulls are simply interesting; they read as cool before they read as biology.

Once a student is at the counter, I ask what I have come to call "off the top of my head" questions. Look at those teeth: what do you think they were for? Why do you think this head is so big? What's in there making it that big? These are not questions I have an answer key to. They are the questions I would ask if I were the one wondering, which I often am. The student can ask back, and I can notice things, and they can notice things, and the back-and-forth gets going. Pretty soon the student who walked over to look at a skull is doing the thing: engaging with the material as a knower, sometimes saying something they would not have risked in front of the class.

What the skull station offers, materially, is contrast, the full Montessori card next to the blank, which is the form Variation Theory describes, except the contrast is built into the artifact pair itself. The student can move their eyes between the two versions and feel the structure of what they are looking at. The shared object is right there on the counter, and the dialogic register comes from me: the "off the top of my head" questions that treat the student as someone whose thinking is worth standing at the counter with.

The skull station also offers the form of differentiation I have come to think actually matters, which I would call respect-based rather than easier/harder. The standard differentiation language is calibrated by perceived ability and produces visibly different tasks for visibly different students, which telegraphs to everyone in the room which students have been judged less capable. Respect-based differentiation asks a different question: what does this student need in order to be respected as a knower right now? For a student who would be overwhelmed by too much at once, the simpler version of the timeline is a way in: not less, but the version that lets them be a knower in this moment. For the smart, turned-off student who has been bored and insulted by school for years, the harder version, lining up the time periods against the characteristics and pictures, working it out from the timeline text, is a way of saying I see what you are capable of and I am not pretending otherwise. For students already geeking out, I sometimes offer what looks like a task that helps the classroom, organizing or making materials, but is really a way of meeting the level they are working at. I tell them: you can say no. You can stop when you want. The agency stays theirs.

Jair, this semester, is a version of this differentiation in negative. He is intelligent and earns good grades, but when the tasks are not complex he is done early and spends the period joking with friends, because there is nothing in the room that has yet met him. I told him, mildly but plainly, that he has learned he is smart and gets stuff done but does not challenge himself, that my classroom does make challenges available, and that I expect him to take them. His friend Christian, meanwhile, had been off on his own examining the tree-of-life simulation I put up at the start of class and got into the evolutionary-periods task cards on his own. Today I put him on the cladogram activity, the hardest thing I have available, and he is doing it. The difference between Jair's response and Christian's, on the same day in the same room, is the difference between a student who has not yet been given a reason to take the harder task and a student who has and is taking it. Much of teaching is making the harder thing available without forcing it, and reading which student is ready to reach for it on which day.

What happens in the conversations at the counter, and this is the part that matters most for the perceptual argument, is that students surface partial knowledge they would not risk in front of the full class. A student offers an idea they are not sure about, half-finished, sometimes wrong in a way more interesting than being right. They sometimes propose an epistemic revision I had not thought of, and I am surprised, and I tell them so. The look that crosses their face then, the one I have come to think of as the "got me" look, is one of the most consequential things that happens in my teaching. It is the look of someone who has just found out that what they think actually matters to the person they are thinking with. That look is what the rest of the perceptual vocabulary of this book has been for.

The skull station is not novel as a pedagogical idea; it can sound like a thousand constructivist setups proposed across the past century. What is worth being careful about is the institutional condition under which it can actually happen in a public Title I classroom. The reason it works is that I am free to be at the counter: not preparing tomorrow's lesson, not formatting feedback, not writing IEP-accessible versions of an assessment in that moment. How I came to have that freedom, what made the production work of teaching stop consuming the attention the presence requires, is the subject of the next chapter, and I will hold it until then. Here I want only to mark what the freedom is for.

Because this is the equity argument the rest of this book has been circling, and I want to state it directly. What the skull station is, the full card next to the blank, the individually invited student, the "off the top of my head" questions, the respect-based differentiation, the side conversation where partial knowledge can surface, the protected option to say no, is exactly what expensive private schools with a tutorial, Socratic orientation have always provided. It is what progressive schools have offered by following individual children's interests. It is what Montessori classrooms build into the materials themselves. The wealthy and the privately educated have had access to this kind of teaching for as long as the model has existed. What I am claiming, on the basis of what I now see in my own classroom, is that these things can be delivered in a public school that serves high-poverty students. Intellectual apprenticeship is not a privilege some students bring and others lack; it is a form of access a school can either reproduce or withhold. The barrier was never that teachers in Title I classrooms did not know how to teach this way. The barrier was that the institutional conditions did not give them the time to be present in this way.

I want to be careful not to overclaim. Pedagogy cannot correct structural problems. It can mitigate them, a teacher with the right tools and conditions can do a great deal, but the structural problems are real and require structural responses. The Germantown study documented this in one direction; Alabama recently illustrated it in another, where a coordinated, sustained, accountable state effort, targeted investment in early-grade reading and math, teachers retrained in research-based instruction, dedicated coaches in schools, more support steered to the schools that struggled most, produced measurable change at scale: on the 2024 NAEP, Alabama posted the nation's largest gain in fourth-grade math and was one of only a handful of states to recover to pre-pandemic levels (PARCA, 2025; Alabama State Department of Education, 2025). [VERIFY: Alabama citations — the verify-list in the back matter covers these.] Pedagogy is necessary and not sufficient. The work I describe here cannot replace changing how schools are organized, funded, staffed, and held accountable. It can mitigate where the structures are not yet changed, and it can show, in the meantime, that the human conditions our most under-resourced students need are not impossible to deliver. They have only been impossibly expensive.

## Vehicles: the sim, the activity, the lab

Most of the rest of what I do, sims, activities, labs, is best understood as the choice of a vehicle for making a specific critical aspect perceivable. The question for each is the same: does this vehicle make the critical aspect of this lesson perceivable to these students, and is it the best available vehicle for that purpose?

The sim. Some of my best lessons now turn on something students can play with for ten minutes: a simulation, usually digital, that lets them manipulate one variable that matches the critical aspect. They run it, see what happens, change one thing, see what happens, make a guess and test it. What makes the sim work is not that it is interactive or digital. It is that the variable they manipulate is the critical aspect itself. If the critical aspect is that the dark reactions require ATP and NADPH from the light reactions, the sim must let the student turn the light off and watch the downstream chemistry stall. They cannot help noticing what the variation makes visible. They are not being asked to memorize a dependency; they are given an object that lets them feel it. The sim does for the lesson what messing around does for any domain learned voluntarily: it lets them poke at something until they have a feel for it.

Activities for noticing. Most of the activities I design are not for output. They are for noticing. I want students to handle something, notice something about it, and have a place to record what they noticed. The activity exists to put the critical aspect under their fingers and in front of their eyes. The output, if any, is a small note about what changed, what surprised them, what they have a question about. Drawing, when it serves the noticing, is a legitimate form. Slide annotation, letting them mark and write directly on what they are seeing, works because the thinking happens in the same place as the content. A worksheet fits meaning to a form the student has not made. A noticing activity gives the student something to make meaning of.

Labs done soberly. Labs cost time, setup, and class periods. I do them when the lab is the right way to teach the critical aspect: not because the curriculum prescribed a lab this week, and not because the lab is the photogenic part of biology. The question I ask before scheduling one is whether the embodied nature of the lab actually matters for what I want students to discern. If yes, it is worth the time. If no, the lab is theater that takes time from the real lesson, and I have learned to refuse the theater. This is the most boring claim in the chapter and the one most contested in practice. Schools love labs. But labs are pedagogically expensive, and many do far less work than the time they consume can justify. I used to treat labs like they were being prepared for FDA approval: serious steps, fine-print directions. Now I am messier. Directions are scant. If someone does it wrong, that is something to think about, not a failure to remediate. The lab is more honest when the student's noticing is what the lab is for, rather than adherence to a procedure.

## Two things per class

Once I understood what the perceptual conditions required, the design problem became one of simplification. Most curricula attempt too many critical aspects per lesson. Most teachers, including me when I started, used to do four or six things in a class period, because we have been trained that more coverage is more learning, and because we are anxious about looking like we are doing too little. The result is a class in which several concepts get mentioned and none get learned to discernment.

I decided to do two things per class. Not four. Not six. Two. A lesson built around a critical aspect or two, where students had to actually think: not copy, not guess from context. And an activity or lab focused on the same critical aspect, not on completing a procedure for its own sake. The rest, the elaborate dialogue scaffolds, the supplementary worksheets, went.

This sounds like a reduction of ambition. It is the opposite. Doing two things well takes more pedagogical work, not less. The two have to be the right two. The vehicle has to be designed to make the critical aspect perceivable. The thought question has to require thinking that cannot be retrieved. The cuts are pedagogical decisions, not laziness, and only the teacher can make them, because they require judgment about what these students in this room on this day actually need.

Remember Toma, from this book's opening pages: the student whose coldness had judged my classroom not worth his energy, until the afternoon I crouched beside his own uncopied sentences. That moment happened inside this structure, and it taught me what the structure was for. The framework was the right one. The entry point was what had needed adjusting: to simplify without reducing, the whole first, one critical aspect at a time, one contrast, a conceptualization in their own words, and to meet students where they could be successful and build from there. Why is that not the assumption for every classroom?

My slides carry a fair amount of text, to support students who were absent or are reviewing, but I scan each one and then talk freely. Reading the slides as written would be deadening. I highlight important words with my marker. I draw where it helps. Inserted question slides ask students to put the previous content in their own words. I ask for a thumbs up when most seem done. I do not go over the answer; that is critical. If I answer it, students just wait for me to. Instead: discuss your answer with a partner, go back and check the slides, or come check with me if you are still uncertain. When I grade their work and find a problem, I hunt them down to talk about it.

I do ask students to annotate the lesson slides as a means of accountability, but I do not evaluate their answers or make them speak aloud. The annotation is an approximation of lurking that serves institutional legibility, the system can see they engaged, but it also takes their hand and begins moving them from lurking toward messing around. They are touching the content with their pen before they are asked to touch it with their voice. By the time the dialogic register reaches them, they have already been in private contact with what we are looking at together.

Word banks instead of blanks when the vocabulary load is high. Slide annotation as a response space, so the thinking happens in the same place as the content. Grades heavy enough to matter; I am not going to pretend ninth-graders will engage purely for intrinsic reasons, and they need the grades to navigate the system. What I was not willing to simplify was the thinking itself. The thought questions do not have answers you can copy; they require students to apply a distinction they have just made to a new case, or to notice where a pattern breaks down. I work to make the questions hard because the thinking is hard, not because the syntax is hard; obscure phrasing creates a pseudo-rigor that tests familiarity with school language rather than grasp of the concept. And before students have the confidence to dialogue in public, they need confidence in their own thinking. I remind them constantly: I care about your thinking, not the answer. "Because" is not an answer. "I don't know" is not an answer. But anything that shows me your thinking is credit-worthy.

What I found, almost immediately, was that students found it doable. And doable turned out to be the condition for real thought.

Priya still talks. But she is also writing now: not notes, not copied answers, but actual sentences that show her working something out. Jaylen started thinking out loud, working something out and saying it to the room, not because I called on her but because she had something she wanted to test. Theo, who does not care about biology and says so freely, started leaning forward at the thought questions: not because he suddenly found DNA fascinating, but because the questions were puzzles, and Theo likes puzzles. Diego called me over to see if his sentences made sense. Because they had time to think during lessons, they began to verbalize more as a class, calling out answers even when they did not have to. They stopped being silent observers and started to participate. Not because I had found the right hook or relationship strategy. Because the lesson had created enough perceptual purchase for them to have something to say.

## Double booking

From the outside, this classroom still looks compliant. Objectives are visible. Vocabulary gets covered. The pacing guide would not immediately flag anything wrong. This is intentional.

I call it double booking, and I want to be precise, because it is a bit of a cynical phrase but an accurate one, and the concept originates with Matusov (2011).[3] Teachers who want to serve students honestly inside constrained systems often have no choice but to run two curricula at once: the one the institution can see, and the one that actually develops in students' minds. The first is the price of operating. The second is the reason to operate. What I produce for the system is a class that looks like vocabulary instruction, lab completion, standards coverage. What I am actually attending to is harder to see: whether students are beginning to experience themselves as thinkers.

And the pacing? I can more or less keep it now, because I demand less compliant paper-pushing and focus directly on students' thinking and the activities that support it. Doing two things well turns out to take less time than doing six things poorly.

Like the Germantown study showed in the library, proximity matters here too. I move among students during activities and labs: talking over aspects together, noticing their ideas, crouching beside someone who is stuck. Students feel less like objects of my teaching and more like workmates in a shared process. That is not a technique; it is a stance.

The work has moved from managing activities to noticing agency. My practices have become simpler: two things per class, real questions, thinking rewarded rather than correctness. What has become more complex is what I notice. I am watching differently now, listening for the beginning of interpretation, for the moment a student starts to revise what they thought, for the places where the room opens and the places where it closes down. Not equity as sameness. Equity as growing capacity.

## Where to start

If you teach, the practical version of this chapter is smaller than the theory, and you do not have to remake everything to begin.

Start where you are. Pick one unit, the next one you have to teach anyway. Before you plan a single activity, ask what the two or three critical aspects of that unit actually are: not the vocabulary, not the standards list, but the discernments the whole topic depends on, which are usually hiding wherever students reliably go wrong. Write each one as the misconception it corrects. For natural selection, mine are death-and-reproduction-not-individual-striving, population-not-individual, and variation-came-first. Yours will be different for your unit, but there will only be two or three, and naming them is most of the work.

Then, for each critical aspect, design one thing students can play around with that makes that exact aspect perceivable: a sim, a sort, a contrast they can manipulate, an artifact they can handle. The test for the activity is narrow: does it isolate the aspect, so that the thing the misconception gets wrong is the one thing the student cannot help noticing? If it does, it earns its place. If it is just an activity, engaging, hands-on, but not built to make a specific aspect perceivable, it is taking time the discernment needs.

Then write thought questions that cannot be answered by copying: questions that ask the student to apply the discernment to a new case, or to find where a pattern breaks. And then cut everything else in the unit that is not serving one of those two or three aspects. That is the hard part, and it is a pedagogical decision only you can make, because it requires knowing what these students in this room actually need.

That is the whole entry point: one unit, its two or three shaping aspects, one play-around activity for each, questions that require thinking, and the discipline to cut the rest. The framework is general; the place to begin is whatever you have to teach on Monday.

## Where inquiry comes from

I began with Lezir's question, and I want to end where it points. The reason the question matters is not that it was brilliant; it was an ordinary question, the kind a curious fifteen-year-old asks when he can finally see what he is looking at. It matters because of where it came from. It did not come from Lezir's prior interest, because he had none. It did not have to be motivated out of him. It was produced: by a perceptible object, a stance that received him as someone who could think, and enough time in the early phases that he was not punished for being where he was. Build those conditions and the questions come. That is the whole of it.

This is what I most want other teachers to take, because it is the thing I was never told and spent years failing without. We are told to teach from inquiry. We are not told that inquiry has prerequisites, or that those prerequisites are exactly what our most under-served students were never given, or that building them is the actual work. Inquiry is not the fuel you need to start. It is what a mind produces once it has something to work on and somewhere for the work to go. Everything in this chapter, the perceptible object, the two registers, the question types, the four stages, the skull station, the two-things discipline, is in service of that one reversal.

So the chapter lands on three things at once. Inquiry is not something students simply bring. Dialogue is not just talk. And the teacher's work is to construct a shared object and a shared practice of attention so that more students can enter the work as knowers; perception is where responsibility begins, not where it ends. One distinction is worth seating here because the chapters ahead depend on it: a student shaping the conditions *around* their learning, the pace, the format, the task, is exercising something adjacent to the agency this book is after; a student shaping meaning *within* the learning is exercising the thing itself. Both matter. Only the second is what the apprenticeship finally builds, and participation alone does not guarantee it.

There is a name for what all of it adds up to, which I take up fully later in the book: an apprenticeship in intellectual authorship, or more simply, an intellectual apprenticeship. The other names I have reached for, the knower-stance, the two-register design, the curated object, are not competing labels. They are the actions that make the apprenticeship happen. What the apprenticeship requires first, and what makes it possible at the scale a public classroom demands, is the subject of the chapter that follows.

## The argument (don't re-derive this each session)
The problem in classrooms, especially high-poverty ones, is not student motivation but suppressed or under-developed agency. Beneath agency lies a substrate she calls INTELLECTUAL AUTHORSHIP: the capacity to treat one's own thinking about a subject as real and consequential — to author meaning within content one didn't choose. That capacity is built through an INTELLECTUAL APPRENTICESHIP: a deliberate, in-school rebuilding, for students who weren't given it early or elsewhere, of the apprenticeship advantaged students got informally.
- Agency taxonomy: shadow forms (dysfunctional, pseudo, effortful) and functional forms (engaged, epistemic, democratic, authorial). Intellectual authorship is the substrate beneath all of them; agency is its visible expression. Pseudo-agency = agency without authorship.
- Agency is a developmental CAPACITY, not a trait — built from the outside in (grounded in the EF/SES literature). Autonomy =/= agency; offering autonomy before the substrate exists produces avoidance and anxiety, not agency.
- Inquiry is a CONSEQUENCE of teaching, not a precondition: inquiry has prerequisites.
- The apprenticeship has five movements: perceptible object -> joint attention/proximity -> legitimate peripheral participation -> dialogic answerability -> graduated transfer.
- Patall's "backfire" finding (the agency cycle fires for higher-achieving / lower-stereotype-vulnerable students and fails for the rest) is the empirical signature of the missing substrate.
## Naming (settled — keep consistent)
The practice has ONE name: intellectual apprenticeship. The capacity it builds is intellectual authorship. Everything else — the knower-stance, two-register design, the curated/perceptible object, respect-based differentiation, the four-stage progression, the skull station, two-things-per-class — names an ACTION that serves the apprenticeship, not a rival concept.

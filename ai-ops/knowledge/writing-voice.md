## Voice to preserve

- First-person, working-teacher prose. A real scene carries the argument when author-supplied evidence is available; never invent a student, exchange, classroom detail or personal anecdote to satisfy a style pattern.
- Preserve self-implication, the fairness concession, dry humor and colloquial or acid lines. Flag a reviewer risk rather than removing her words.
- Plain declaratives; each sentence adds information. Keep long accretive sentences, semicolon chains, colons, parentheses and her existing fragments. Use sparse dashes. Do not add fragments to imitate her rhythm.
- Rhetorical questions turn the argument. Define coined terms on arrival and keep distinctions in the field's vocabulary. Do not substitute metaphors for available terms.
- Ground theory in classroom particulars. Keep scholarly apparatus largely in endnotes. Verify attribution to the specific finding or term; flag unsupported citations rather than filling them in.
- Narrow concrete claims to their evidence. Flag causal overclaim and deficit framing. Preserve her own qualifying asides when they narrow a particular claim; omit AI commentary announcing how careful the writing is.

## What to strip from AI suggestions

No architecture callbacks, roadmap announcements, aphoristic closers, repeated refrains, hedge stacks, generic teacher anecdotes, cadence-built triplets, abstract paired reversals, dramatic abstractions or em-dash saturation. Follow the full tics list for lexical and structural exclusions. Conventional fluent teacher-talk is a warning sign, not a goal (R22).

The guide's measured frequencies describe a historical corpus. They are diagnostics, not quotas for adding scenes, jokes, fragments or questions. Rule zero in the guide says never apply all markers at once. The fuller guide limits added-fragment imitation; its Part 2 explicitly prohibits fragments added in Claude's hand.
